An assessment of municipal solid
waste management practices and
challenges in Mogadishu, Somalia
Anas Madar1*, Abdirahman Geelle2 and Samira Osman3
1Department of Agricultural and Environmental Science, Institute of Research and Innovation, Salaam
University, Mogadishu, Somalia, 2Somali Civil Aviation Authority, Mogadishu, Somalia, 3Ministry of
Education, Culture & Higher Education, Mogadishu, Somalia
The present study aimed to assess municipal solid waste management practices
and challenges in Mogadishu, Somalia. The speciﬁc research objectives were to
examine the state of municipal solid waste management practices, challenges,
and strategies for improving practices and promoting best practices. Data were
collected using a mixed-methods cross-sectional design from 273 respondents
including waste management workers and residents. The ﬁndings reveal that
current practices are largely ineffective, with 49.1% of respondents rating
services as “poor”; 46.2% of households currently resort to roadside dumping
due to a lack of formal infrastructure. Signiﬁcant barriers include inadequate
ﬁnancial resources, insufficient institutional capacity, technological deﬁciencies,
and a lack of skilled personnel (58.3%). Practical recommendations include the
implementation of “pay-as-you-throw” policies to ensure ﬁnancial sustainability,
the establishment of small-scale community recycling hubs to reduce landﬁll
pressure, and the formalization of private-sector partnerships through rigorous
legislative oversight and standardized disposal sites.

attitudes, citizen perception, waste management, community practice, environment,

1 Introduction
The issue of solid waste management has become a signiﬁcant challenge in many
African cities, including Mogadishu, the capital of Somalia (Godfrey et al., 2019). The
growing population, rapid urbanization, and economic development in these regions have
contributed to the increasing generation of municipal solid waste, leading to signiﬁcant
environmental and public health concerns (Assefa, 2017). According to estimates, in 2016
the global community generated approximately 2.01 billion metric tons of municipal solid
waste annually, with an average of 0.74 kilograms produced per person each day (Ram et al.,
2021). However, at least 33% of this waste was not managed in an environmentally
responsible manner, contributing an estimated 1.6 billion tons of carbon dioxide emissions,
which account for roughly 5% of global greenhouse gas outputs (Shahab et al., 2022).
In response, the Paris Agreement of 2017 involved over 80 nations commit to reducing
emissions and improving waste management practices (Wang and Stanisavljevic, 2019).
Solid waste is broadly deﬁned as unwanted or discarded materials and byproducts that
are no longer needed by the original user (Omar et al., 2019). This includes a wide range
of items, such as household garbage, institutional waste, street sweepings, construction

Solid waste management (SWM) consists of various of strategies

objective of this research is to evaluate the existing framework
of municipal solid waste management in Mogadishu to identify
systemic gaps and propose sustainable interventions. Speciﬁcally,
the study addresses current state, institutional, ﬁnancial, and
technical challenges of municipal solid waste management. To
better understand the factors inﬂuencing citizen participation in
municipal solid waste management practices, this study adopts
the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) proposed by Ajzen (1991).
The TPB is a widely recognized social psychological model that
links beliefs to behavior, suggesting that an individual’s intention
to perform a given behavior is the most immediate determinant
of that behavior (Hunter et al., 2019). Previous studies focused
on general waste management; this research is the ﬁrst to apply
the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to Mogadishu’s speciﬁc
socio-political context.
2 Research methodology
2.1 Study site
Mogadishu is the capital and largest city of Somalia. The
city is situated in the Banadir region which is made up
of seventeen districts and the estimated population is 2.5
million (AREA OF RETURN INFORMATION BROCHURE
MOGADISHU, 2020). The city is the second-fastest growing
globally, with an annual growth rate of 4% (Hassan et al.,
2024). About 23% of this population are Internally Displaced
People (IDP) who live in informal settlements, making access
to basic sanitation and waste disposal facilities a problem
in the city (Hassan et al., 2024). The study was carried out
all 17 constituencies with (217,108) Households [Estimation,
2014 (UNICEF Somalia, 2014)]. The target population was the
waste management workers; Environmental Cleaning private
Companies, Mogadishu subdivision (county) sanitation directors,
dumping sites operator, Mogadishu Municipality legal advisors,
Directorate of Environment of the Oﬃce of the Prime minister,
environmental experts, health workers, and waste generators.
2.2 Research design
This study utilized a descriptive, mixed-methods cross-
sectional survey design (Trochim and Donnelly, 2001) to assess the
role of municipal solid waste management practices and challenges
in Mogadishu (Umer et al., 2019). This approach allowed for the
integration of quantitative data from questionnaires and qualitative
insights from interviews to provide a comprehensive assessment

2.4.1 Pilot testing and qualitative detail
The questionnaire (Sections A: Demographics, B: Practices, C:
Barriers) was pilot-tested with 30 residents from a non-sampled
district to ensure reliability (Cronbach’s alpha). Furthermore, 10
semi-structured interviews were conducted with key informants,
including
district

and
private

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis to
identify recurring themes such as infrastructure deﬁcits and
ﬁnancial constraints.
2.5 Methods of data analysis
Quantitative data were edited, coded, and analyzed using SPSS
and Excel to generate descriptive statistics, including frequencies
and percentages. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic
analysis, where interview transcripts were categorized into themes
such as “infrastructure gaps,” “legislative oversight,” and “behavioral
barriers” to complement and triangulate the quantitative ﬁndings.
2.6 Sample size
To determine the sample size, the Krejcie and Morgan formula
was used (Krejcie and Morgan, 1970). For a target population of
217,108 households at a 95% conﬁdence level, the target sample
was 384. While the ﬁnal achieved sample was 273 (response rate
of 71.1%), this resulted in a margin of error of approximately 5.9%,
which is slightly higher than the initial 5% target.

3.2 The state of municipal solid waste
management practices in mogadishu
This section presents ﬁndings from the study, which aimed to
assess municipal solid waste management practices in Mogadishu.
Solid waste management in Mogadishu is undeniably in a
critical state, marked by a pervasive lack of proper infrastructure
and eﬀective systems. The overwhelming majority of residents
(49.1%) perceive solid waste management as “poor,” with an
additional 20.9% rating it as fair. Qualitative insights reveal that
this infrequency leads to “waste piling,” where residents feel forced
to use roadside dumping (46.2%) as a survival mechanism This
mirrors ﬁndings in Garowe, where household waste often exceeds
1 kg per day, yet municipal services only reach a fraction of the
population (Nour and Olusegun, 2024). This is further exacerbated
by ineﬃcient collection systems, with most respondents (46.5%)
reporting weekly waste collection, and a signiﬁcant portion (30–
40%) of urban waste remaining uncollected, which directly impact
less than half of the population. This aligns with broader trends
cited by Wilson et al. (2013), who noted that 30–60% of urban solid
waste in developing countries often goes uncollected, serving less
than 50% of the population.
The challenges extend to the very foundations of waste disposal,
as a staggering 86.1% of respondents highlight the absence of
essential facilities at disposal sites, including compaction, proper
enclosures, and drainage. This severe deﬁciency in formal disposal
infrastructure directly contributes to the prevalent household waste

4 Limitation of the study
1-This study has several limitations: the cross-sectional design
prevents drawing causal inferences; the achieved sample (n = 273)
was lower than the target (n = 384), increasing the margin of
error to 5.9%; and the use of purposive sampling for experts may
limit the generalizability of the qualitative ﬁndings to other regions
in Mogadishu.
2-Respondents might provide socially desirable answers rather
than reﬂecting their true waste disposal habits.
3-A lack of oﬃcial municipal records made it diﬃcult to
compare primary ﬁndings with historical secondary data.
5 Conclusion
The study assessed the current challenges and strategies of
solid waste management practices in Mogadishu municipality.
The majority (49.1%) of respondents indicated poor solid waste
management in Mogadishu. The study identiﬁed several key
barriers to eﬀective municipal solid waste management in
Mogadishu. These included a lack of capacity, facilities, and
equipment among private waste collection ﬁrms for properly
managing waste collection, transfer, and disposal. Additionally,
personnel involved in solid waste management lacked the
necessary qualiﬁcations and skills to eﬀectively carry out waste
collection, transfer, and disposal operations. There was also a
reported shortage of landﬁll infrastructure for solid waste disposal.
Furthermore, respondents indicated that ﬁnancial allocation was
inadequate to support eﬀective solid waste management services,
including waste reduction, recycling, and recovery initiatives. The
study concluded that the most eﬀective strategies for improving

solid
