Part II section (5) of the central bank of Somalia Act 2012 the 
key mandate of the Central Bank of Somalia (CBS)
The Bank shall:
a)	 Formulate implement and be responsible for monetary policy and 
implement the foreign exchange policy.
b)	 Hold and manage the foreign exchange reserves of Somalia.
c) License, regulate and supervise all banks and financial institutions 
to foster the liquidity, solvency, and proper functioning of a stable 
financial system.
d)	 Formulate and implement such policies as to best promote the 
establishment, regulation, and supervision of efficient and effective 
payment, clearing and settlement systems.
e)	 Where appropriate, administer payment, clearing and settlement 
systems.
f) 
Act as banker and adviser to, and as fiscal agent for the government 
and public entities.
g)	 Act as sole issuer of legal tender Somali currency notes and coins; and
h) Shall compile, analyze, and publish the monetary, financial, balance of 
payments statistics and other statistics covering various sectors of 
the national economy.

GOVERNOR’S 
MESSAGE
It is my privilege to present the Central Bank of Somalia’s Annual Report 
for 2025, a year of steady progress in strengthening the financial system, 
advancing institutional reform, and supporting Somalia’s economic 
development. Throughout the year, the Bank remained focused on its mandate 
to safeguard financial stability, reinforce regulation and supervision, deepen 
financial intermediation, and modernise the country’s financial infrastructure. 
Somalia continued to implement its economic reform programme under the IMF-
supported Extended Credit Facility. Reforms in public financial management and 
institutional capacity helped preserve macroeconomic stability despite structural 
constraints and external shocks. Real GDP growth is estimated at 3.1 per cent in 
2025, while inflation stood at 3.7 percent.
The banking sector continued to expand, with total assets reaching US$2.3 billion. 
Capital adequacy and liquidity remained well above regulatory requirements, 
reflecting the sector’s resilience. Remittance inflows also remained strong, 
providing critical support to households, businesses, and the wider economy. 
The year marked important progress in strengthening the legal and regulatory 
foundations of Somalia’s financial sector. Parliament enacted the revised Financial 
Institutions Law in May, establishing a comprehensive basis for regulating non-
bank financial institutions. The CBS subsequently licensed the first seven Non-
Deposit-Taking Microfinance Institutions, broadening access to formal financial 
services for underserved households, women, young entrepreneurs, and small 
businesses. Parliament also enacted the Insurance Law, enabling the development 
and regulation of Sharia-compliant insurance services, including Takaful.
Modernising financial infrastructure remained a central priority. The rollout of the 
Somalia Instant Payment System introduced a real-time, interoperable platform for 
retail payments, available 24 hours a day, seven days a week. This is an important 
step towards a more efficient, inclusive, and digitally connected economy. 
The Bank also partnered with the International Finance Corporation (IFC) to advance 
the legal and regulatory framework for a Credit Information System and Collateral 
Registry. These initiatives will strengthen credit risk assessment, expand access 
to finance, and support private sector development.
Preparations for the reintroduction of the Somali Shilling also advanced. Work 
included reforms underpinning the proposed Currency Board Arrangement, 
amendments to the Central Bank of Somalia Act currently before Parliament, and 
the Board’s approval of reserve management regulations. These measures are 
essential to ensuring that currency reform is credible, carefully sequenced, and 
supported by strong institutional safeguards.

T
he global economy remained resilient during 2025 despite 
heightened uncertainty arising from trade policy shifts, 
geopolitical tensions, and persistent structural challenges. 
Economic activity was supported by easing inflationary pressures, 
generally accommodating financial conditions, and continued 
investment in technology and digital innovation. However, growth 
remained below historical averages, reflecting weaker productivity 
growth, elevated public debt levels, and increasing fragmentation in 
global trade. During the year, major economies imposed higher tariffs, 
prompting retaliatory trade measures that increased uncertainty in 
global trade and investment. Although negotiations subsequently 
reduced some trade restrictions, persistent risks of trade fragmentation 
and supply chain disruptions continued to weigh on global economic 
activity and the medium-term outlook.
According to the IMF’s January 2026 World Economic Outlook (WEO) 
Update, global growth is estimated at approximately 3.3 percent 
in 2025 and is projected to remain resilient at 3.3 percent in 2026 
and 3.2 percent in 2027. Inflation continued to decline across most 
economies, although price pressures remained above target in some 
advanced economies. Financial markets remained broadly stable, 
supported by improving inflation dynamics and expectations of 
gradual monetary policy normalization. Looking ahead, global growth 
is expected to remain moderate, supported by technological innovation 
and resilient private sector activity. Nevertheless, downside risks 
persist, including renewed trade tensions, geopolitical uncertainties, 
elevated sovereign debt vulnerabilities, and slower-than-expected 
productivity growth. These developments underscore the importance 
of sound macroeconomic policies and strengthened international 
cooperation to support sustainable and inclusive global growth.

Advanced economies (AEs) continue to exhibit relatively stable 
growth, supported by resilient labor markets, technological investment, 
particularly in artificial intelligence, and easing financial conditions. 
In contrast, growth in emerging markets and developing economies 
(EMDEs) remains more heterogeneous, constrained by tighter global 
financing conditions, high debt burdens, and limited policy space. 
Notably, a significant share of low-income countries is projected to 
experience weaker per capita income growth compared to pre-pandemic 
levels, highlighting a widening development gap. AEs are expected 
to grow at 1.7 percent in 2025 and 1.8 percent in 2026, supported by 
easing monetary policy, more accommodative financial conditions, 
and fiscal support as the adverse effects of trade barriers gradually 
diminish. In contrast, EMDEs are projected to sustain stronger 
growth, exceeding 4.0 percent in both 2025 and 2026, underpinned 
by improving financial conditions, resilient domestic demand, and 
continued structural adjustments.
African economies remained resilient in 2025, navigating a complex 
global environment characterized by multiple external shocks, 
geopolitical tensions, and structural challenges. Despite continued 
exposure to global shocks, including commodity price volatility, tighter 
financial conditions, climate-related risks, and ongoing geopolitical 
tensions, the region maintained strong economic performance. 
According to the African Economic Outlook 2026, real GDP growth in 
Africa accelerated to an estimated 4.4 percent in 2025, compared to 
3.5 percent in 2024, supported by improved agricultural production, 
supportive macroeconomic policies, and relatively high commodity 
prices. Growth was broad-based across the continent, with the 
majority of African economies recording stronger performance than 
in the previous year, underscoring the region’s resilience. Looking 
ahead, growth is projected to moderate slightly to 4.2 percent in 2026 
due to lingering geopolitical uncertainties and external headwinds, 
before strengthening again in the medium term, reflecting Africa’s 
continued role as one of the fastest-growing regions globally.
East Africa remains the fastest-growing region, with growth projected 
at 6.4 percent in 2025, supported by strong domestic demand and 
expansion in the services sector. West and North Africa, with growth 
rates of  4.7 percent and 4.1 percent respectively, have benefited from 
increased extractive activity and improved services performance, 
while Central Africa is estimated to grow by 3.9 percent, supported 
by renewed investment in mining and infrastructure. In contrast, 
Southern Africa continues to experience relatively slower growth 
of 2.5 percent, reflecting structural constraints, including energy 
shortages and weak external demand, before recovering to 4.4 
percent in 2027.

1.1	Global Trade
Global trade in 2025 operated in a complex and evolving environment, 
marked by heightened geopolitical tensions, rising trade barriers, 
and persistent policy uncertainty. Despite these headwinds, the 
global trading system demonstrated notable resilience, supported 
by adaptive supply chains, front-loading of goods ahead of tariff 
increases, and continued demand for technology-driven investment. 
According to the IMF’s World Economic Outlook (January 2026), 
World trade volume growth is estimated to have rebounded to 4.1 
percent in 2025, but is projected to slow to 2.6 percent in 2026, 
as the temporary boost from inventory accumulation fades and 
the cumulative effects of tariffs and trade restrictions intensify. 
The global trade environment has become increasingly shaped by 
reconfigured supply chains, the regionalization of trade flows, and 
strategic diversification away from traditional trading partners. 
Moreover, trade performance remains uneven across regions and 
income groups. While AEs have largely recovered and integrated 
into shifting trade patterns, many EMDEs, particularly low-income 
and fragile states, continue to face constrained export growth and 
limited participation in global value chains.

Global trade volume growth is expected to follow a distinctly non-
linear trajectory over the 2024-2026 period. In AEs, growth is expected 
to rise from 2.0 to 3.0 percent, and continue resilience in EMDEs, 
notwithstanding a slight moderation from 6.3 to 5.7 percent. AEs 
are expected to be 1.9 percent, while EMDE growth declines more 
sharply to 3.6 percent, suggesting a return toward more sustainable, 
medium-term growth levels following the temporary rebound in 2025.

1.2	Global Inflation and Commodity Prices 
Global inflation continued its gradual moderation in 2025, reflecting 
the combined effects of tighter monetary policies, easing supply 
constraints, and declining energy prices following the post-pandemic 
and geopolitical shocks. According to the IMF’s WEO report (January 
2026), global headline inflation declined to 4.1 percent in 2025 and 
is projected to fall further to 3.8 percent in 2026 and 3.4 percent in 
2027, signaling a steady return toward central bank targets across 
most economies. Despite this progress, inflation remains above 
pre-pandemic averages in several economies, especially in EMDEs, 
where structural constraints, currency depreciation, and food price 
volatility continue to exert upward pressure. 
This disinflation process, however, remains differentiated across 
country groups. In AEs, inflation is expected to ease gradually amid 
slowing economic activity and moderating external demand, declining 
from 2.6 percent in 2024 to 2.5 percent in 2025, and further to 2.2 
percent in 2026. In contrast, EMDEs continue to experience higher but 
moderating inflation, with rates projected to fall from 7.9 percent in 
2024 to 5.2 percent in 2025, and further to 4.8 percent in 2026. This 
downward trajectory reflects improving supply conditions, tighter 
macroeconomic policies, and a gradual rebalancing of labor markets, 
particularly as wage growth moderates in export-oriented sectors. 
Food price dynamics remain a key source of pressure and volatility. 
According to the FAO Food Price Index, the index averaged 127.2 
points in 2025, a 4.3 percent increase from 2024, driven primarily 
by higher global prices of vegetable oils and dairy products, which 
more than offset declines in cereals and sugar.

1.3 Global Financial Stability
Global financial conditions in 2025 remained broadly stable, supported 
by moderating inflation, easing monetary policy expectations in some 
advanced economies, and continued liquidity in global markets. This 
contributed to improved investor sentiment, strong equity market 
performance, and generally steady access to financing for sovereigns 
and corporates. However, this apparent stability conceals underlying 
vulnerabilities in the global financial system.  According to the IMF’s 
Global Financial Stability Report (October 2025), asset valuations 
in certain markets appear stretched, with rising concentration risks, 
particularly in equity markets. At the same time, global financial 
vulnerabilities remain elevated, with high public and private debt levels 
increasing exposure to interest rate shocks and refinancing risks, 
especially in emerging market and developing economies. Despite 
improved market sentiment, EMDEs continue to face tighter external 
financing conditions, elevated borrowing costs, and exchange rate 
volatility. Overall, the IMF highlights that while short-term financial 
conditions have improved, risks of sudden market corrections and 
renewed financial stress remain significant, requiring strong policy 
frameworks and adequate buffers to preserve stability.
Global macro-financial conditions are further shaped by high public 
and private debt levels, especially in EMDEs, where debt has reached

2.1.1	 Crop Production 
Crop production showed significant seasonal divergence in 2025, 
highlighting the agricultural sector’s high sensitivity to rainfall variability 
and climate shocks. While the Gu season supported relatively favorable 
production in some regions of the country, the Deyr season was markedly 
poor, resulting in an overall mixed but weakened annual performance.
According to the Food Security and Nutrition Analysis Unit (FSNAU), 
during the 2025 Gu season, cereal production in southern regions was 
estimated at approximately 129,400 metric tons. This represents about 
14 percent above the long-term average (1995–2024), supported by 
generally average to above-average rainfall, timely land preparation, 
and expansion of cultivated areas. In riverine areas, particularly along 
the Shabelle, low flooding conditions facilitated recessional farming, 
further contributing to off-season production gains. However, this 
positive performance was not uniform nationwide; northern regions 
experienced near-total crop failure, with Gu production estimated at 
only 830 tons, around 93 percent below average, due to prolonged dry 
spells, pest infestations, and high input costs. In contrast, the 2025 
Deyr season performed exceptionally poorly, significantly undermining 
annual crop production. Cereal production in southern Somalia declined 
to approximately 15,600 metric tons, including off-season harvests, 
representing an 83 percent reduction from the long-term average 
and the lowest Deyr output recorded since 1995. The collapse in 
production was driven primarily by widespread rainfall failure, erratic 
rainfall patterns, reduced access to agricultural inputs, and persistent 
insecurity, which disrupted planting and harvesting activities.

Somalia’s external sector remains characterized by a structurally 
large current account deficit, reflecting a narrow export base and 
a strong reliance on imported goods and services. Persistent trade 
imbalances primarily drive the deficit, as domestic production remains 
insufficient to meet consumption and investment demand. Despite 
the widening deficit in goods and services, the current account has 
continued to be supported by significant secondary income inflows, 
particularly diaspora remittances, which remain the largest source 
of foreign exchange earnings and a key buffer against external 
financing pressures.
The second source is grant inflows from development partners 
and international institutions. These non-debt-creating flows have 
supported public investment, reconstruction efforts, and institutional 
development, while contributing to external stability. Together with 
remittance inflows, grant transfers have helped finance external 
imbalances and mitigate pressures on the overall balance of payments. 
In parallel, debt relief under international initiatives has significantly 
reduced external debt vulnerabilities and strengthened Somalia’s 
medium-term external sustainability.
Nevertheless, in the context of Somalia’s highly import-dependent, 
aid- and remittance- dependent economy with a largely de facto 
dollarized financial system and limited domestic production capacity, 
the external position remains vulnerable to a range of shocks, 
including fluctuations in global commodity prices, climate-related 
disruptions, geopolitical tensions, and any potential declines in 
remittance and donor inflows. These vulnerabilities underscore the 
importance of policies to broaden the export base, enhance domestic 
productive capacity, improve competitiveness, and attract foreign 
private investment. Strengthening these fundamentals will be critical 
to reducing external imbalances, increasing resilience to external 
shocks, and supporting sustainable and inclusive economic growth 
over the medium term.
4.1	Current Account
Somalia’s current account deficit widened further in 2025, reflecting 
persistent structural imbalances in the external sector and the 
economy’s continued reliance on imported goods and services. The 
deficit increased from US$2,494 million (20 percent of GDP) in 2024 to 
US$3,160 million (24 percent of GDP) in 2025. Although remittances 
and other current transfers remained robust, they were insufficient to 
offset the growing import bill, underscoring the economy’s continued 
reliance on imported goods and services.
The merchandise trade deficit widened from US$4,995 million (40 
percent of GDP) in 2024 to US$5,563 million (42 percent of GDP) in 
2025. Merchandise imports increased from US$6,652 million (54 
percent of GDP) to US$7,297 million (55 percent of GDP), reflecting 
sustained demand for food, petroleum products, construction 
materials, machinery, and other consumer and capital goods.

4.1.1 Capital Account and Financial Account
The capital account recorded net inflows of US$635 million (5 percent 
of GDP) in 2025, down sharply from US$1,826 million (15 percent

In 2025, capital inflows declined as debt relief-related transfers 
ended and development partners reduced external grant financing,

external resources.
The financial account recorded net inflows of US$436 million (3

and real estate. Despite this improvement, FDI remained insufficient 
to offset weaker donor-related financing, underscoring the need to 
broaden private capital inflows to strengthen the sustainability of 
Somalia’s external financing.

4.1	External Public Debt Developments
Somalia’s external debt position underwent a fundamental transformation 
following the attainment of the Completion Point under the Enhanced 
Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative on 13 December 
2023. This milestone marked the culmination of more than a decade of 
reforms aimed at strengthening macroeconomic management, public 
financial governance, fiscal transparency, and institutional capacity.
The implementation of debt relief agreements substantially reduced 
Somalia’s external public debt stock and improved debt sustainability 
indicators. Total external debt declined from US$5,234 million in 2018 to 
US$1,482 million in 2025, while the external debt-to-GDP ratio fell from 
61 percent in 2018 to 11 percent in 2025. These developments reduced 
debt vulnerabilities, strengthened fiscal and external sustainability, and 
created additional fiscal space for development priorities.
A significant component of the debt restructuring process involved 
bilateral creditors, particularly Paris Club members, which provided 
extensive debt cancellation and restructuring. As a result, Paris Club 
obligations declined from the largest component of Somalia’s debt 
portfolio to a negligible share of total external debt. Debt relief from 
multilateral institutions and bilateral partners further accelerated 
the normalization of Somalia’s debt profile, improving the country’s 
creditworthiness and access to concessional financing.
4.1.1	 External Public Debt Stock Developments
Somalia’s external public debt continued its downward trajectory in 
2025, reflecting the sustained impact of debt relief initiatives and 
prudent debt management policies. Total external debt declined from 
US$5,311 million in 2019 to US$1,482 million in 2025, representing a 
cumulative reduction of approximately 72 percent.
The composition of the debt portfolio changed significantly during the 
review period. Paris Club debt declined sharply from US$3,072 million in 
2019 to US$46 million in 2025, reflecting the successful implementation 
of debt relief agreements. Multilateral debt also fell from US$1,530 million 
to US$634 million during the same period. In contrast, obligations to non-
Paris Club creditors remained broadly stable, increasing from US$709 
million to US$799 million. The remaining debt stock is increasingly 
concentrated among non-Paris Club and multilateral creditors. The 
remaining non-Paris Club debt reflects ongoing bilateral negotiations, 
as these creditors are not part of the coordinated Paris Club debt relief 
framework and therefore require separate agreements with Somalia.
In the fiscal year of 2025, Somalia’s external debt stock stood at US$1,482 
million, with non-Paris Club creditors accounting for 54 percent of total 
debt, multilateral institutions accounting for 43 percent, Paris Club 
creditors accounting for 3 percent, and commercial creditors accounting 
for less than 1 percent. This creditor structure indicates that Somalia’s 
debt portfolio is predominantly owed to official creditors and remains 
largely concessional.

4.1.3	 Debt Composition and Portfolio Transformation

improved its sustainability profile. Paris Club debt declined from

T
he year 2025 marked a period of sustained progress and 
consolidation of reforms in Somalia’s financial sector under 
the leadership of the Central Bank of Somalia (CBS). Building 
on earlier reforms, the authorities focused on modernizing financial 
infrastructure, strengthening regulatory and supervisory frameworks, 
expanding financial inclusion, and safeguarding financial stability. These 
efforts were supported by continued improvements in governance, 
transparency, and institutional capacity, reinforcing confidence in 
the financial system. A major legislative milestone was achieved in 
May 2025 with the enactment of the revised Financial Institutions 
Law (FIL), which established the legal and regulatory framework 
for microfinance institutions and strengthened the foundation for 
financial sector development.
Building on this legal framework, the Central Bank of Somalia 
approved the first licenses for seven Non-Deposit-Taking Microfinance 
Institutions (NDTMFIs) in November 2025 through its Board of 
Directors. This marked an important step toward expanding access 
to responsible financial services for households, micro-entrepreneurs, 
women-owned businesses, and youth-led enterprises, particularly 
those that have historically been underserved by the formal financial 
sector. In parallel, the Central Bank continued to strengthen Somalia’s 
financial market infrastructure. In September 2025, CBS signed a 
partnership agreement with the International Finance Corporation 
(IFC) to support the legal foundations of the implementation of the 
Credit Infrastructure Project to establish two critical financial market 
infrastructures: a Credit Information System (CIS) and a Collateral 
Registry (CR). Both systems will be hosted and operated by the 
Central Bank of Somalia and are expected to enhance access to credit, 
improve risk assessment, and support private sector development.
A key milestone was the rollout of the Somalia Instant Payment 
System (SIPS), operated through the Somalia Payment Switch (SPS), 
enabling real-time, 24/7 transfers across banks and, progressively, 
mobile money operators, while supporting the national QR code 
standard (SOMQR). This has strengthened interoperability, improved 
efficiency, and expanded access to financial services, particularly 
for underserved populations.
Domestically, CBS advanced a comprehensive regulatory agenda to 
address structural gaps. Key developments included amendments 
to the AML/CFT framework alongside the enactment of the Takaful 
Insurance Law, supported by new regulations on licensing, capital 
and solvency requirements, and financial reporting. At the same 
time, financial integrity measures were strengthened through stricter 
customer identification requirements, including the mandatory use 
of a national ID for new accounts and the expansion of the Hubiye 
eKYC platform.

5.3 National Payment System
The National Payment System (NPS) continued to play a central role in 
advancing Somalia’s financial modernization in 2025, providing a reliable 
and efficient platform for digital transactions. By enhancing connectivity 
among financial institutions, the system has streamlined payment 
processes, strengthened transparency, and supported compliance 
with anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing standards.
The system demonstrated strong growth over the year, processing a 
total of 1,409,151 transactions with a combined value of approximately 
US$2,422 million. This represents a 10 percent increase in transaction 
volume and a 25 percent increase in total value compared with 2024, 
reflecting growing adoption of electronic payment channels and 
increased interbank settlement activity.
Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) transactions continued to account 
for the majority of transaction value, totaling approximately US$1,850 
million during the year and representing about 76 percent of the total 
value processed through the system. This highlights the system’s critical 
role in facilitating large-value interbank payments and maintaining 
financial system stability.
The Automated Clearing House (ACH) remained the primary driver of 
transaction volumes, processing the majority of payments within the 
system. Total ACH transaction value reached approximately US$572 
million in 2025, reflecting continued growth in retail and lower-value 
transactions, supported by increasing use of digital payment channels.
Overall, the continued expansion of the NPS underscores its growing 
importance within Somalia’s financial ecosystem. Its capacity to handle 
increasing transaction volumes while supporting efficiency, security, 
and interoperability positions its role as a key enabler of financial 
inclusion, innovation, and long-term economic resilience.

credibility.

national payment system. The Central Bank continued implementing

banks and mobile money operators. Given the widespread use of

7.1	Board of Directors
The Central Bank of Somalia continued to strengthen its corporate 
governance framework during 2025, in accordance with the Central 
Bank of Somalia Act (Law No. 130 of 22 April 2012) and ongoing 
institutional reform commitments under the IMF-supported reform 
program. The Board of Directors is the highest policy-making organ 
of the Bank and provides strategic direction and oversight over the 
Bank’s core mandates, including monetary policy reforms, financial 
sector supervision, payment systems modernization, currency reform 
initiatives, and institutional governance. The Board is composed of 
seven members: the Governor, who serves as Chairperson; the Deputy 
Governor, who serves as Vice Chairperson; and five non-executive 
members. The Governor, as Chief Executive Officer and Chair of the 
Board, is responsible for convening Board meetings and guiding 
strategic policy deliberations. In the absence of the Governor, these 
responsibilities are undertaken by the Deputy Governor.
In line with the provisions of the CBS Act, the Board continued 
to delegate specific operational and oversight responsibilities to 
specialized committees while retaining exclusive authority over 
core monetary policy decisions and strategic institutional matters. 
Throughout 2025, the Board maintained a high level of engagement 
and oversight, with particular emphasis on strengthening institutional 
governance, operational independence, transparency, accountability, 
and risk management frameworks. The Board continued to promote 
a governance culture grounded in professionalism, integrity, and 
adherence to international central banking standards and best 
practices.
The Board also provided oversight over the implementation of the 
Bank’s Strategic Plan 2025–2029, with a focus on currency reform, 
modernization of monetary and foreign exchange policy frameworks, 
enhancement of financial stability, expansion of financial inclusion, 
modernization of payment systems, and strengthening operational 
excellence across the institution.
In 2025, the Board was supported by several standing committees, 
including:
•	
Audit Committee 
•	
Licensing and Supervision Committee 
•	
Currency Committee 
•	
Human Resource Commitee 
These committees continued to support effective oversight and 
specialized review of the Bank’s operational, financial, regulatory, 
and governance functions.

7.3 Governance and Reform Roadmap
In 2025, Somalia continued to make significant progress on its economic 
reform agenda through close cooperation with the International 
Monetary Fund and other international development partners. The 
reform agenda remained focused on strengthening macroeconomic 
stability, enhancing institutional governance, improving financial 
sector resilience, and supporting sustainable economic growth.
The Central Bank of Somalia continued implementing reforms 
under the Extended Credit Facility (ECF) arrangement for the period 
2023–2026. The Bank maintained compliance with key structural 
benchmarks relating to central bank governance, financial sector 
supervision, transparency, and operational independence.

reintroduction project and the proposed Currency Board Arrangement

framework required for implementation of the CBA. In collaboration 
with the IMF, amendments to the CBS Act continued to be finalized to

compliance systems through enhanced risk management frameworks, 
improved operational controls, and reinforcement of Anti-Money

audit functions, procurement oversight, financial reporting systems, 
and information technology governance.

implementing initiatives to modernize monetary operations, strengthen 
financial inclusion, improve payment infrastructure, and enhance 
institutional effectiveness. The operationalization of the Somalia

infrastructure remained key priorities during the year.

through professional training programs, institutional capacity-building 
initiatives, and reforms to strengthen performance management 
and organizational effectiveness. These efforts contributed to the 
development of a professional, accountable, and performance-
oriented institutional culture.
