EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
1.1. Kenya 
The food system level of development is the key condition that defines the nature of private sector 
investments, especially in technology and food quality and safety related infrastructure.  
A pre-feasibility study completed in 2022 looked in detail at the food system level of development and in 
particular the dairy and domestic horticulture sectors. It concluded that while Kenya is the biggest economy 
in the region, its agrifood systems are yet to transition from purely traditional business models to structured 
and efficient supply chains, a change that is driven by the growth of the middle class and subsequent 
emergence of modern retail in urban areas.  
In the pre-feasibility study, investment in cold chain technology was analysed from the perspective of a 
potential business solutions to food loss and waste reduction, and business value addition through quality 
and food safety compliance gains. 
While the pre-feasibility study set out to present a high-level analysis of the investment opportunities, this 
report focuses on advising The National Agricultural Value Chain Development Project (NAVCDP) on 
planned investments in market and infrastructure development, safer urban food systems and policy and 
regulatory development, by presenting strategic opportunities that could be pursued to sustainably support 
infrastructure and cold chain development in the sector. 
This follow-up work elaborates on a couple of business cases, one for dairy and one for horticulture, 
identified as potential entry points for private sector investment. It builds ‘deep dives’ that demonstrate 
qualitative and financial parameters for successful lending and enabling-environment interventions that can 
be supported by NAVCDP.  
In the dairy sector, the ‘deep dive’ on cold chain investment opportunities focuses on one of the currently 
under-served areas of assistance - the Milk ATM business, which is seen as a market outlet for smallholders 
and a source of affordable nutrition to financially sensitive markets. It is a small business operation that 
unlocks market access potential for smallholder dairy cooperatives, MSMEs and the achievement of 
nutrition and food safety outcomes. It requires small investment and operation capital, however, it is 
currently fraught with food safety compliance issues, leading to risks of heavy fines and business closure, 
imposed by the food safety regulator – presenting a strategic opportunity for investment in de-risking 
formalisation of milk sales. 
Based on the in-depth review of the opportunities, the study suggest that the project works closely with 
Kenya Dairy Board (KDB) to improve its enforcement capacity and in parallel work with producers and 
ATM managers to support their capacity to comply with the milk safety standards. At the same time, the 
project should support consumer awareness campaigns, in line with the Nairobi County Food System 
Strategy on understating the relationship between safe and nutritious food and the risks posed by the 
continuing tradition of consuming unregulated raw milk, especially in case of under 5 years-olds. 
In the domestic horticulture sector, the ‘deep dive’ focuses on modern retail driven supply chains, with 
large aggregation, based on predictability and reliability of demand, as a driver to investment in 
infrastructure and productive partnerships development with farmers. Investment in cold chain 
infrastructure in the context of domestic markets is still a novel concept, which requires the creation of an 
eco-system of players to bring together demand, aggregation, technology and reliable suppliers.  
The inter-interdependence of such a model can in turn de-risk investment in logistics and infrastructure and 
create the capacity to service the demand for large volume of quality fresh produce to a niche but growing 
modern retail sector.  
The management of such operations requires professionalisation of food logistics and supply chain 
management in order to secure market share and return on investment. As opposed to the case of milk 
ATMs, such business operation target the affluent middle class retail markets, however provide a financially

consider mechanisms that can de-risk financing for their clients, such as ‘buy-back’ options.  
The pre-feasibility study identified wholesale food markets (WFM) as key points of horticulture 
aggregation and food loss and waste along within the food system in Kenya. This study elaborates on the 
opportunity for the project to support the adoption by government and private sector stakeholders of a 
holistic approach, and to take forward the overall regulatory environment that structures the market system 
as a whole, defines the role of wholesale markets, designates ownership, and defines partnership and 
revenue opportunities. This intervention is seen as the necessary precursor to any significant or 
transformative infrastructure investment. 
1.2. Rwanda 
Rwanda is a small country, with fast and rapid horticultural value chains, but with limited demand. Rwanda 
has a large rural population with a high degree of self-consumption and a small urban population, almost 
exclusively found in the capital Kigali. Formal, horticultural, export value chains exist, but the sector is 
young and not fully developed. Most trade happens in informal domestic and regional trade chains, with 
high food losses both at post harvesting and marketing stages, as well as low price premium to drive an 
upgrade of the value chains. Consumers prefer fresh food from open markets, which usually make it from 
farm to Kigali in less than 30 hours after harvest. Companies, cooperatives and other value chain actors 
lack professionalism to improve the efficiency of the value chains, and to take opportunity of technological 
solutions. On the other hand, Government and partners are eager to support farmers and to transition 
towards renewable energy solutions, resulting in cold chain investments beyond what is currently 
demanded, and hence stores are most times not in use. Less than 50% of facilities are in use, at an estimated 
utilization rate of 19% (USAID 2018). Cold chain technology is an important enabler for the formalization 
of food systems, however, it is not a driver.  
To achieve Rwanda’s long-term objectives, including reduced food loss and more modern food systems, 
cold chain solutions, from cold stores sourced by renewable energies to insulated trucks and boxes, are part 
of the answer. Short to medium term investments to spur this development may include: (i) build capacity 
of farmers and their institutions to decrease post-harvest losses; (ii) relocate existing storage capacity to 
places with large volumes of transactions at the moment; (iii) upgrade of the agro-logistics systems; (iv) 
build the capacity of private sector to improve their operations; (v) increase demand for high quality produce 
on domestic markets (by hotels, public institutions and educated consumers at large); (vi) remove 
bottlenecks for expanding exports; (vii) strengthen on-going interventions

2. BACKGROUND  
2.1. Analytical framework and key findings of the pre-feasibility study  
This report follows analytical work carried out in 2022, whereby the FAO Investment Centre and the World 
Bank conducted a pre-feasibility study about the potential launching of a new and dedicated investment 
vehicle to finance the development of cold chain in East Africa via appropriate financial solutions and 
backed by integrated market linkages, in order to reduce food losses, improve food security and increase 
farmers’ incomes. The pre-feasibility study also aimed to inform the World Bank on how tailored public 
interventions could help facilitate and maximizing private sector cold chain investments and financing in 
this area, looking into bottlenecks hampering further adoption 
The analysis was based on a comprehensive literature review of nearly 40 documents and more than 70 
physical and virtual interviews conducted in early 2022 with research institutions, the public sector 
(regulators, donors), logistic companies (technology and third-party logistic providers), farmers, small to 
large private companies (aggregators, processors, traders, retailers) and various financiers (commercial 
banks, leasing companies, impact funds and Development Financial Institutions). 
The pre-feasibility study highlighted that investment in cold chain needs to be approached as part of a larger 
transformation of traditional food systems towards modern systems that operate within standards on food 
safety and quality, reduction of food losses, and that implies improved value chain governance, structuring 
and efficiencies. In other words, investment in cold chain development is one aspect of the transformation 
of the entire ecosystem within which agrifood businesses operate and is the product of number of external 
drivers that shape the outcome of the investment (Figure 1). 
Figure 1: Different parts that shape outcomes (Source: https://foodsystemsdashboard.org/about-food-system)  
 
The externalities have a direct impact on the current food system dynamic and the incentives or 
disincentives for investment in cold chain development. In addition they directly influence the three specific 
drivers for cold chain investments, which can be broadly summarised as:  
  
 
Food safety regulations + Food quality requirements + Supply chain geography & length

modernisation etc. 
Also in 2022, Wageningen University put forward a conceptual framework for a methodology to assess

2.2. Main objectives and structure of this report 
To operationalise some of the findings of the pre-feasibility study, a second phase of this analytical work 
was done in 2023, aiming to inform specific ongoing World Bank investments in Kenya and Rwanda on 
how to strategically guide project interventions with regards to policy and regulatory support as well as 
financing of agrifood infrastructure development, and in particular cold chain technology deployment. 
In Kenya, the strategic guidance targets the operationalisation of the National Agricultural Value Chain 
Development Project, while in Rwanda it aims to support the Commercialization and De-risking for 
Agricultural Transformation Project. 
To inform this study, the FAO Investment Center reviewed more than 30 documents and conducted over 
30 interviews in Kenya, in Rwanda. This was done in close cooperation and with the valuable support from 
both the World Bank local teams and the ESMAP team providing guidance and inputs. 
The study has two parts that answer distinct key questions: 
First part - Kenya 
Build on the conclusions of the pre-feasibility study and provide strategic guidance to the NAVCDP 
management on several issues:  
 What are the qualitative and quantitative characteristics (Deep Dives) of selected businesses as 
intervention entry points that demonstrate viable cold chain investments in the small-scale dairy 
sector and in the domestic horticulture sector? 
o How could these businesses be supported to scale and engage with the NAVCDP target 
beneficiaries? 
 What do providers of finance and technology need to scale up the deployment of cold chain?  
 What conditions should be put in place to ensure the financial and operations sustainability of

following key questions:

 Is there a business case for the use of cold chain technology in the context of the domestic 
horticulture sector? 
 What are the regulatory, and financing frameworks as well as technological solutions, within which 
agrifood actors operate?

1. KENYA: National Agriculture Value Chain Development Project 
1.1. Overview of the NAVCDP 
The National Agricultural and Rural Inclusive Growth Project (NARIGP- P153349) and the Kenya Climate 
Smart Agriculture Project (KCSAP- P154784) have laid down a strong foundation for commercialization 
of agriculture in Kenya. The two projects from 2017-18 to 2021-22, mobilized nearly 1.1 million farmers, 
mostly smallholders, into nearly 37,000 Common Interest Groups (CIGs) and nearly 500 Farmer Producer 
Organizations. Over 10,000 community level extension workers are training farmers on 760 Climate Smart 
Technologies, Innovations and Management Practices (TIMPs) across 21 value chains. These TIMPs have 
been developed through 51 adaptive research projects with Kenya Agriculture and Livestock Research 
Organization (KALRO). Nearly 60 % of supported farmers have been linked to 505 FPOs; 177 FPOs have 
developed Enterprise Development Plans (EDPs) for leveraging formal finance.  
The newly approved National Agriculture Value Chain Development Project - NAVCDP (P176758) 
was declared effective on September 23, 2022. It will build on the strong foundation laid by NARIGP and 
KCSAP and shift the focus from production to demand-driven value chain development. Among other 
activities, this entails support for a series of private-sector infrastructure investments, such as cold chain 
development, that support aggregation and commercialization, scale-up value addition and market linkages.  
The value chain development approach will focus on the ability of off-takers and SMEs to enhance inclusive 
supply chain outreach that engage the created FPOs in economically sustainable commercial partnerships. 
It will support the enhancement of value chain services, both in terms of logistics (such as storage, transport, 
packaging etc.) as well as access to credit and financial services.  
The NAVCDP components and sub-components described below (see  
 
 
 
 
 
Textbox  1) frame the approach towards agro-logistics and value chain development, as well as support for 
the piloting of safer urban food systems, including market infrastructure and policy implementation support. 
the application of cold chain technology presents only one facet of the infrastructure upgrades envisaged. 
The objective of this report, following on the pre-feasibility study done in 2022, is to support NAVCDP 
implementation to direct its interventions as strategically as possible towards achieving the desired results 
with regards to infrastructure and value chain development and cold chain technologies as an enabler for 
that development, focused on dairy and horticulture, with cold chain as an enabler for food loss and waste 
reduction.  Cold chain technology is more demanding in terms of investment, management and use-
planning, than most other basic infrastructure and does not directly lend itself to integration into traditional 
supply chain business operations. Thus, the need for this report and guidance on where and how 
interventions can trigger transformation and generate benefits for the target beneficiaries.In addition, the 
findings will help the project reflect on the policy and institutional strengthening support necessary to 
support urban market infrastructure commitments, to ensure they achieve their desired development goals. 
As part of the NAVCDP overview, there is also a brief description of the domestic food safety regulation 
as well as the Nairobi County Food System Strategy which directly influence the scope and nature of the 
project interventions but beyond that also frame the environment within which private businesses operate 
and the incentives presented to them in terms of infrastructure and technology investments.

1.1.1. The domestic food safety regulatory environment  
A report by USDA on Food and Agricultural Import Regulations and Standards in Kenya (2022)2 states 
that Kenya’s agricultural and food trade regulatory mandates are handled by various agencies that are all

Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS), Directorate of Veterinary Services (DVS), Kenya Dairy Board (KDB), 
Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS), National Biosafety Authority (NBA), and the Department of Public 
Health (DPH). Kenya is currently reviewing its food safety laws and regulations through draft parliamentary 
legislation.

transform the agricultural sector ‘into a vibrant, productive and efficient sector that is competitive both

1.1.2. Nairobi City County Food System Strategy: Food Waste and Recovery 
As part of the food system transformation drive, the Nairobi City County elaborated a Food System Strategy 
published in 2022, and supported by NAVCDP, observes that within the city and nationally a policy and 
legal foundation is largely in place. The Strategy sets out a vision for the city food system and food 
distribution, which aims at formalization and integration of the system, together with improvement of the 
structures and dispersal of markets and other food outlets. Further it states that food processing will be 
improved by reducing the cost and delays in approvals for enterprises, together with improving the capacity 
of processors and promotion of consumption of processed food through which safety, quality, shelf-life and 
value would be better assured. 
However, the city's food system is largely informal, undocumented and inefficient and, therefore, is unable 
to sustainably deliver adequate quantities of safe, affordable, accessible and nutritious food to all residents, 
nor protect commercial interests of its players, and food loss and waste (FLW) occurs across all 
components. 
In the horticulture sector FLW is attributed to factors such as lowered consumption during cold season for 
some products, incidental cuts, breakages and crushing of the products, low quality, spillage, soaking in the 
rain, rotting and spoilage, over-storing, broken grain, chaff presence, over-ripening, rodent-bites, and other

b. training processors on marketing skills such as influential packaging; 
2. Promoting food recovery, through: 
a. Conducting sensitizations of stakeholders on the food recovery concept;  
b. Promotion of separation of food waste at household and institutional levels;

 Wind power: This energy supply method uses wind turbines to generate electricity to power

1.3.1. Models available to finance the acquisition or use of cold chain equipment in 
Kenya 
The Assessment of the Cold Chaim Market in Kenya published in 2023 by the Energy for Access 
Coalition identified the following financial models as being in use in Kenya   
Direct sale / Upfront purchase. Risks are limited for the equipment supplier. But clients must have 
enough financial muscles to pay the equipment upfront. In Kenya most agri SMEs and the vast majority 
of farmers lack this capital.  
Lease-to-own. Clients are required to make a downpayment representing 20-50% of the total cost of 
the equipment. The remaining balance is staggered and spread across a fixed term usually ranging from 
12 months to 3 years. After the lease period (once the payment cycle is over), ownership of the asset is 
transferred to clients. This model makes access to equipment more affordable and thus increases the 
number of potential clients (although final costs are more expensive). But this option represents an 
additional risk for equipment suppliers, who need to be comfortable with the risk profile and repayment 
capacity of the clients. As a result equipment suppliers are likely to restrict this option to larger clients 
operating on high-value export value chains.  
Asset finance. Financial institutions finance the acquisition of equipment on behalf of clients, who then 
pay them back the amount of money borrowed with interests. 
Sale-lease back. Financial institutions purchase the equipment on behalf of clients and then lease the 
equipment back to the clients. The ownership of the asset is moved to the financial institution for the 
duration of the lease. Clients are required to make a downpayment. After the lease period (once the 
payment cycle is over), ownership of the asset is transferred to clients. 
Rental model. Clients pay a flat rental fee per annum, month or day to use cold chain equipment. This 
requires less capital compared with the acquisition of an asset. But clients may still have to pay the rent 
during times of low utilization. The risk for clients is that they will not use the equipment as planned 
for the economic gains to cover the rental costs. This model offers some flexibility but still requires a 
certain level of planning.  
Pay as you store or Cooling as a service. Clients pay a fixed rate for using the equipment for a 
particular weight and time duration. This option offers more flexibility to clients who are simply paying 
for a service. It is considered as one of the most feasible ways to provide smallholders with access to 
cold chain8. However, operating such equipment can be a challenge, as multiple users may want to use 
the same asset simultaneously and for different products requiring different storage conditions and 
temperatures. The commercial viability of this innovative model has not been demonstrated yet.

1.3.3. Key bottlenecks to finance more cold chain investments 
Investment funds are already offering patient capital to finance providers of cold chain technology. 
This has taken the form of equity investments in the capital of these companies, like PEII+ in Sokofresh, 
or via loans financing the development of these companies, like SunFunder with InspiraFarms. 
Investment funds specialized in the energy sector appear to be better equipped for financing technology 
providers, probably thanks to a better understanding of the technological challenges associated. In 2020, 
InspiraFarms raised a USD 3m Series B equity round from different equity funds led by KawiSafi 
Ventures. This followed a USD 5m Series A equity round completed in 2018. 
Investment funds specialized in the agrifood sector will more naturally finance agrifood value chain 
players with cold chain investment plans, like aggregators or food processing companies. But ticket size 
(investment amount) is an important bottleneck. Most of these funds will not provide financing below 
USD 5m, which means that financing the acquisition of cold chain equipment is often too small for 
them. For debt, the interests earned on small loans do not compensate the fund’s transaction and fixed 
costs associated with the origination, structuring and monitoring of such loans. For equity, small 
investments are very illiquid, making future exits uncertain. Even a facility like AgriFi, which operates 
as a fund but with a higher risk appetite and with the flexibility to provide loans as low as EUR 0.5m, 
has not identified any Kenyan agri SME with cold chain investment plans and meeting its investment 
criteria. In Tanzania AgriFi did however finance an agrifood aggregator acquiring cold trucks and 
building decentralized collection centers with temperature-controlled storage rooms.  
The mandate of the Arch Fund is to invest in cold chain in East Africa. But it operates under another 
model in the sense that the fund is building cold chain installations from scratch and with the ambition 
to manage and run these assets before selling them by 2030. For the moment the Arch Fund has focused 
on the construction of large temperature-controlled warehouses in Nairobi, Kigali and Kampala. These 
assests will be mostly used to store products that will be exported or have been imported (essentially 
agrifood and pharmaceutical products). The Arch Fund is also planning to invest in cold storage units 
on the fields to store farmers’ products immediately after harvesting . But these investments in first-
mile cold storage units have not happened yet and there are doubts whether this part of the cold chain 
can turn into a profit center. 
For DFIs, ticket size is the main constraint. Most of them will not provide financing under USD 10m. 
Their risk appetite is even lower than many investment funds and they are not equipped to work with 
SMEs characterized by a certain level of informality and low accounting standards. DFIs entry point 
could be to finance the cold chain investment plans of larger companies, but their agribusiness clients 
in Kenya do not seem so interested in cold chain. For instance, one of the largest local agri-traders, 
ETG, does not operate on value chains requiring cold chain. Food aggregator Twiga has invested in a 
few cold storage units on farms but has not used them yet because of power issues. Twiga’s fleet of 
trucks does not have temperature controlled equipment. Its clients  do not use cold chain so the company 
has instead developed a a “fast chain” model. Moreover most DFIs offer only hard currency financing 
and as a result have been financing mostly exporters. DFIs will struggle to offer local currency loans at 
a competitive (and acceptable) rate to agrifood companies operating on domestic value chains. Another 
option for DFIs could be to invest in the capital of investment funds that will then finance cold chain

•

•

•

make cold chain investments in the horticulture domestic value chains.

and less sophisticated, and where the return on cold chain investments is less obvious than in export

Public and private donors are less concerned by the profitability of their investments. Their support

social or environmental impact. Indeed, cold chain investments in agrifood systems can be associated 
with the following impact: (i) reduction of food losses and waste, (ii) upgrade of food safety standards, 
(iii) rising incomes for farmers, (iv) better integration along value chains (with off-takers getting more 
visibility on farmers’ production in terms of both volume and quality), (v) food security enhancement 
and potentially (vi) reduction of carbon emissions. Still, more data could be collected and disseminated 
to better measure these different forms of impact. This would strengthen the economic rationale for 
donors and other development actors to spend public resources and/or offer concessional finance 
towards the development of cold chain. The Duke University, in partnership with the Shell Foundation,  
is currently working on a research project building more evidence base about how solar-enabled cold 
storage can lead to enhanced climate resilience. In a similar fashion, NGO DanChurchAid is about to 
conduct a pilot test in Kenya, providing five cold storage facilities to farmers and collecting data on 
their impact on prices and food losses. The public dissemination of the findings from these studies and 
pilot projects will help to strengthen the rationale for public interventions supporting cold chain 
investments. A clearer understanding of the public benefits of such investments is needed to establish a 
robust case for public investment. For the moment, donors have been offering concessional capital in 
the form of grants or interest free loans to cold chain technology providers meeting specific targets . 
GIZ has helped Sokofresh and the Shell Foundation has supported both InspiraFarms and Keep It Cool 
(fishery). This can play an important role to stimulate technology introduction and pilot specific 
technologies or business models. The challenge is then to scale up these solutions and attract more 
commercial financiers with higher requirements in terms of financial viability.  
1.3.4. The requirements of finance and technology providers in order to scale up the 
development of cold chain  
Assuming that finance and technology providers have identified concrete investees (SMEs, 
cooperatives, farms etc.) operating on the domestic horticulture and dairy sectors and with an interest 
to acquire or use cold chain equipment. Based on the above analysis, this report proposes five avenues 
to help unlocking more capital to finance such cold chain investments.    
1) The risk profile of the investees should be acceptable. They should meet certain requirements in 
terms of revenues, asset size, profitability, track record and collateral. These requirements can vary 
significantly depending on the financial institution. If these requirements are not met, the provision 
of de-risking instruments (like first-loss guarantees for banks or first-loss capital for funds) could 
provide additional comfort to financiers. These guarantees could theoretically replace the collateral 
that most agri SMEs do not have. But local banks are often already benefiting from different forms 
of guarantees (from institutions like the African Guarantee Fund, EIB, USAID etc.). In practice, 
guarantees can be quite complex to implement at branches level and often result into additional 
administrative burden and transaction costs for financial institutions. Ultimately, guarantees alone 
are often insufficient to amend banks’ lending strategies and investment criteria. In order to 
successfully unlock more financing, de-risking tools have to be carefully designed and with a strong 
involvement of the bank’s top- and mid-management.  
2) Investees should have the capacity to properly operate cold chain equipment. The provision of 
technical assistance to SMEs, cooperatives or farmers in the form of trainings can help to address 
this concern.
=== PAGE 24 ===
 
 
24
3) Financiers need a strong commitment from cold chain equipment suppliers to offer after-sale 
support like spare parts, maintenance and repair services. Technology providers should have some 
skin in the game to ensure that interests are aligned and they will not disappear once the equipment 
has been sold. Buy-back guarantee schemes can address this concern, with the equipment provider 
taking the responsibility to re-purchase the equipment at a pre-agreed valuation in case of default 
from the borrower (or at least to help identifying buyers for second-hand equipment seized by the 
financier as collateral). This would also help to mitigate the financiers’ concerns about the liquidity 
and value of cold chain equipment in case of default from a borrower (please note that a buy-back 
scheme could also be signed with a more concessional development actor willing to de-risk 
financiers). Structuring the financing of the equipment via the technology provider can be another 
way to answer these challenges. The supplier of equipment will be naturally incentivized to provide 
the right level of after-sale services so its client can make the best use of the asset and pay the 
supplier. However these solutions increase the risks of the technology provider, which may prefer 
to limit their activities to less risky clients like exporters of high-value crops. Technology providers 
may ask to benefit from some de-risking instruments (e.g. first-loss guarantees) as well in order to 
enter into buy-back structures.  
4) Financiers could better understand cold chain technology. Donors can play a key role in this. 
Technical assistance could help to improve their level of knowledge and make them more 
comfortable with the different cold chain technologies. For instance GIZ has provided trainings to 
Kenyan banks on cold chain and solar cooling. Lessons learnt from pilots should be widely 
disseminated.  
5) More specifically, providers of technology need10 (i) patient capital to invest in research in 
technology and business models to develop more affordable cold chain solutions adapted to the 
local context, (ii) affordable credit lines (in both hard and local currencies) to fund working capital 
needs (building stocks of cold chain components locally, offering more attractive payment terms to 
customers etc.), (iii) simplified import procedures and reduced custom duties to import key cold 
chain components that are not produced in Kenya, (iv) de-risking instruments (like first-loss 
guarantees) to supply customers with a more risky profile (e.g. agri SMEs operating on domestic 
value chains and lacking collateral etc.), and eventually (v) grants or results-based-financing to pilot 
innovative cold chain solutions and disseminate the lessons learnt from these experiences. 
Flexibility is key as the cold chain market is evolving rapidly . The different technology providers 
operating in Kenya are at a very different stage of development and require different solutions. 
Finally, the design of these financing solutions requires a good understanding of the local agrifood 
context and particularly of the actual demand and needs from the different stakeholders along the 
targeted value chains.  
Aceli Africa (“Aceli”) could be an interesting partner for the World Bank and NAVCDP teams in the 
perspective of offering tailored de-risking instruments facilitating cold chain investments in the country. 
Based in Nairobi, Aceli is a market incentive facility offering financial incentives to private lenders in 
East Africa to unlock lending for agricultural small and medium enterprises (“agri SMEs”). Launched 
in 2020, Aceli has mobilized more than USD 74m in lending to 600 agri-SMEs in East Africa. Its 
objective is to mobilize USD 600m in financing from commercial banks and impact investors to support 
agri-SMEs, with the goal of improving the livelihoods of over 1 million farm and agri SME workers by 
2025. Incentives take the form of (i) portfolio first-loss coverage mitigating lenders’ risk and 
representing up to 9% of the loans value, and (ii) origination fees representing up to USD 16k per loan 
to compensate the higher transaction costs associated with agri SMEs lending Technical assistance for 
both agri SMEs and lenders completes the setting. 
Aceli is now exploring the applicability of this market incentives model to the off-grid renewable energy 
sector, including solar powered cold storage for agrifood products. The idea is to apply a similar 
approach to mobilize more financing in the off-grid cold chain sector or to scale up the provision of 
cold chain services in the agrifood sector. To achieve this Aceli is studying the possibility to partner 
with local banks, technology providers as well as with large off-takers (sourcing products from agri 
SMEs and with certain requirements in terms of food quality and safety).

1.4.1. DEEP DIVE 1: Milk ATMs (dairy) 
The milk ATM, also known as the automated milk dispenser, is a relatively recent innovation in Kenya 
that has revolutionized the sale and distribution of milk. The idea was first introduced by a Kenyan 
entrepreneur, Anthony Mutua, in 2010. 
Mutua, who had a background in engineering, was inspired to create the milk ATM after he noticed the 
challenges that small-scale dairy farmers faced in selling their milk. Traditional milk selling methods, 
such as selling door-to-door or at local markets, were often unreliable and time-consuming, and farmers 
often struggled to get fair prices for their products. 
To address these challenges, Mutua came up with the idea of a milk dispensing machine that would 
enable farmers to sell their milk directly to consumers without the need for intermediaries. The machine 
would be easy to use and would provide 
consumers with fresh, hygienic milk at 
affordable prices. 
After developing the initial concept, Mutua 
worked with a team of engineers to design and 
build the first milk ATM prototype. The 
machine was a simple, automated dispenser that 
allowed customers to purchase milk using coins 
or mobile payments. The milk was stored in a 
refrigerated tank, and the machine dispensed the 
exact amount of milk that the customer paid for. 
The first milk ATM was installed in a Nairobi 
suburb in 2010, and it was an immediate 
success. Customers appreciated the convenience

of being able to purchase fresh milk at any time of day or night, and farmers benefited from the reliable 
market that the machines provided. 
Today, milk ATMs are a common sight across Kenya, and they have become an important part of the 
country's dairy industry. The machines have helped to increase farmers' incomes, reduce milk wastage, 
and improve access to fresh, affordable milk for consumers. 
The analysis of Milk ATMs is viewed as a strategic entry point for support and investment in inclusive 
dairy sector development and the use of cooling because it allows to focus the attention at Micro Small 
and Medium Enterprise (MSME) level, where informal milk enters the formal market. In addition, in 
the last few years milk ATMs have also been the focus of government efforts to regularise the industry 
and improve food safety and quality within the sector, while keeping the cost of milk affordable to an 
increasingly price-sensitive consumer. 
The ‘deep dive’ focuses on the operation of an ATM within the most common business set-up of an 
existing small to medium retail outlet.

Sensitivity Analysis 
The investment financing model uses average financing conditions as described by different banks in the 
interviews for this analysis and as presented in the section on Linkages between dairy and horticulture 
agribusinesses, providers of finance and technology.  
An investment in an average 300L a milk ATM machine, and other necessary equipment such as an 
additional fridge/freezer for milk storage and tamper proof aluminium cans would require an investment of 
about 2,742 USD. If 30% of the value is to be covered by the business that means that 823 USD need to be 
invested by the business upfront. 
In addition, annual operation costs are estimated to be around 777 USD for utilities and permits and around 
3,200 USD for a dedicated shop staff in charge of milk supply and ATM management/maintenance and 
customer service. The annual cost of permits is low, representing only about 17% of the annual fixed costs. 
Overall, the business maintains positive cash flows after financing and demonstrates capacity to service a 
loan. An overview of the business financial basics as well as solvency and profitability metrics are described 
in Table 2. In terms of operations, the qualitative analysis describes that the business works in the context 
of the two dairy production seasons in Kenya which reflect the rain-dependency of the sector and influence 
the price of milk: the High Production and the Low Production seasons, with price fluctuations reflecting 
these production patterns accordingly, with price going up as supply decreases and down as supply 
increases. 
The financial analysis shows that if the price of milk remains equal to the low price at high production 
season the Milk ATM business will not generate returns. The current price mark-up is just enough to make 
the business profitable along the year, with the high-price season compensating for the low-price season 
and resulting in overall good returns generating a strong IRR of 42%.  
However, despite the positive IRR, the business sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the business operates 
within very narrow margins related to the volumes and price of milk being sold, meaning that the margins 
are narrow and sharp price fluctuations can easily negatively impact financial performance.  
The cash flow and financial profitability analysis for five years of operations demonstrates that an 
investment loan for the ATM and associated equipment, of a total of 362,000 Ksh will be repaid in just over 
a year if the conditions for financing consider a 15% annual interest rate and monthly payments. The Net 
annual cash flow (operation inflows – investment outflows – recurrent outflows) before financing is 
estimated to be around 2,245 USD, which further demonstrates the relatively small financial scale of the 
business. This investment and business size makes the milk ATM an accessible business model for macro 
and small entrepreneurs, and its small but positive returns make it an attractive and replicable model for the 
smallholder dairy sector.  
The biggest risk to investment, as detailed in the qualitative analysis is the food safety compliance issues, 
which emerges as the key focus to de-risk the investment. 
Conclusion - Overall Business Assessment  
The Milk ATM appears to be a viable business. It requires a relatively small technology investment, which 
can be funded through financial institutions and micro-credit facilities that have the capacity to work with 
such small ticket size. In addition, this type of investment is within the reach of many local entrepreneurs 
and offers a viable business model for connecting smallholder producers to the large dairy markets that are 
not serviced by the larger supermarkets due to the high cost of pre-packaged milk. 
Therefore, while this business operates on small margins, investment in creating a food safety compliant 
supply chain of pasteurized milk from the producer cooperatives will stimulate the proliferation of such 
technology and improve their stability by reducing the risk of closure.

The business model is driven by modern retailers’ order request predictability – both in terms of quantity

The first option, in the case of ownership by the aggregator the agribusiness side of organising the supply 
chain and providing the demand-driven foundation for this investment is well established and clear. In this 
case, the aggregator will need to secure financing for this investment which can come from either the 
technology provider in the form of ‘lease to own’ arrangement or a traditional bank loan for the investment.

In the second option, the collection centre is established by the technology provider, who is then looking

entirely different area of agribusiness but rather looking for agribusinesses such as aggregators and POs as

In the third option, the producer organisation can also be considered as a potential owner of this investment,

de-risking of the investment needs to be directly linked to a predictable and established supply chain 
relationship. In addition, while POs are experts at production, collection centre management is an exercise 
in logistics and post-harvest management which is more in line with the core expertise of aggregators than 
farmers themselves. 
The relationship between the agribusiness, technology provider and financing institution are complex and 
present a few alternative options. The key questions that need to be addressed to reassure financiers are: 
 How to design a structure that aligns all the interests of the different stakeholders involved?  
 How to make sure that all parties have skin in the game (especially the technology provider)?  
 How to mitigate the financial institutions’ concerns about the liquidity and value of cold chain 
equipment as collateral? 
There are a couple of options that can be considered: 
Option A: Lease-to-own 
 
Ideally the technology provider would finance the acquisition of cold chain equipment by the operator 
owning a collection center. This could take the form of a lease-to-own transaction. The operator of the 
collection center would make some down payment representing a certain percentage of the total cost of the 
equipment. The remaining balance would be paid to the technology provider over a period ranging from 12 
months to 3 years. After this lease period, ownership of the asset will transfer from the technology provider 
to the operator of the distributed collection center.  
This way the technology provider will be incentivized to provide the right level of after-sale services 
(maintenance, repair services, spart parts etc.) for its customer to make the best use of the asset and make 
all payments as scheduled. But the technology provider will need to raise local currency financing to fund 
the working capital needs associated with this transaction. The advantage of this scheme is that the financial 
institution providing a credit line financing the working capital needs of the technology provider should not 
need any buy-back guarantee. In case of default from the operator managing the decentralized collection 
center, the technology provider will become the owner of the asset and will need to find a buyer on the 
second-hand market. Interests would be aligned.
=== PAGE 45 ===
 
 
45
But for this structure to work, the technology provider needs to raise local currency financing at an 
affordable interest rate. This is an issue in Kenya where indicative rates range from 12% to 25%. Moreover, 
the technology provider should be comfortable with the counterparty risk of the asset owner. Which is not 
a given in the domestic horticulture sector.  
Technology providers usually offer financing to customers operating on high-value export value chains. It 
cannot be excluded that the technology provider may ask to benefit from some risk-sharing mechanism tool 
in order to accept to finance the owner of a decentralized collection center. 
Option B: Direct financing of investment 
 
The alternative would be for the financial institutions to directly finance the acquisition of cold chain 
equipment by the owners of decentralized collection centers. This way, a financial institution financing a 
decentralized collection center would have only one counterpart and will finance all its capex program (cold 
chain equipment, land, construction etc.). On the one hand, this is positive as it makes the structure simpler. 
On the other hand, financial institutions will be concerned by the level of after-sale services provided by 
the technology provider. A low level of maintenance services would jeopardize the whole project and the 
loan’s repayment. Another important question-mark lies in the financial institutions’ capacity to re-sell the 
cold chain equipment in case of default from a borrower and at what price. They may require some buy-
back guarantee scheme to be more comfortable about these risks. Which would complicate the design and 
implementation of the structure.  
All in all, the investment in cold chain in the domestic horticulture sector, under the business scenario

1.5. Municipal Wholesale Food Markets (Horticulture) 
The pre-feasibility study identified wholesale food markets (WFM) as key points of horticulture 
aggregation and food loss and waste along within the food system in Kenya. To support NAVCDP to invest 
strategically in market infrastructure improvements, this study examines the market network and 
management, as a way of identifying key bottlenecks and weaknesses to the potential sustainability of 
infrastructure investments. 
The main wholesale market in Nairobi, Wakulima market, was taken as a case study to provide the context 
for an analysis of the regulatory environment and business practices around market management and how 
these impact potential investments in infrastructure and cold chain upgrades that can reduce FLW.  
In addition, several case studies from other countries are also presented to benchmark potential 
developments and investment in market development. 
Overall, a key premise for this analysis is that any infrastructure upgrade must be done as part of a wholistic 
approach to market development and examine the socio-economic context and role that wholesale markets 
play within it, as well as the ownership and management frameworks that shape the trade and logistics 
services provided by various stakeholders – from public to private. 
Thus, the analysis focuses on market management as a necessary precondition for investment in upgrade 
of facilities and the development of cooling infrastructure to support cold chain development along the 
entire horticulture supply-chain from farm to consumer. 
Key Question: What conditions should be put in place to ensure the financial and operations sustainability 
of infrastructure-related investments and upgrade of wholesale food markets?   
 
1.5.1. Food supply chain and distribution context for urban wholesale  
A wholesale market is shaped by its role in the agri-food supply system and its context. Highly structured 
agrifood systems with clearly defined market networks and interlinkages between wholesale markets and 
retail, for example, will provide a significantly different enabling and business environment for investment 
in particular upgrades, specifically cold chain, than looser systems where wholesale and retail linkages and 
overall supply chain operations are less structured and less regulated. 
Kenya falls into the latter type of context as described above. Key actors in the supply chain include farmers, 
aggregators and wholesalers who buy bulk food products in rural areas and transport these on trucks to 
Nairobi, where they are sold, generally directly from the back of the trucks. Buyers include urban 
wholesalers operating primarily within the Kenyan capital, and various urban retailers selling produce to 
end-consumers.12 Almost 80 % of all produce from the farm gate is assembled and transported to the city 
by wholesalers based in rural communities. 
Rural collection of products is dispersed and only 2 % of the produce flows through formal aggregation 
markets. Wholesalers work with smaller aggregators and visit farms directly.  
In recent years, supermarket chains play a small, though increasing, role in sourcing fruit or vegetables 
directly from large producers as well, currently only 4 % of fresh fruit and vegetable production is 
channeled through supermarkets, while 56 % of fruit and vegetables are marketed through traditional open-
air markets and 36 % through kiosks, both of which are the habitual place of purchase for such goods and 
neither of which uses cooling facilities.

Kenya is characterized by a weak public regulation of food trading, including the weak enforcement of the 
existing rules and norms for wholesale and retail trade, by public authorities.  
This context has consequences on the capacity of WFM to deliver their services to food systems actors. 
WFM are usually poorly maintained, with a mix of retail and wholesale activities taking place in their 
perimeter. WFM may not offer accessible or competitive entry costs for trading agricultural products, 
resulting in the growth of informal channels for food marketing. 
No national regulation governing wholesale markets and wholesale trade exist in Kenya. Each county 
applies its own regulation of WFM which are directly owned and managed by Municipalities.  
All markets have a mix function of wholesale and retail (wholesale in the early morning, retail the rest of 
the day) providing poor primary processing services to users. Revenue models are established by the 
municipalities, mainly based on fees collection from traders or retailers for market access and to rent stalls, 
and fees paid by intermediaries per unit of commodity traded on the market. They are collected daily by 
municipal agents. License fees for operators (yearly) are also part of the revenue model.  They represent an 
important share of municipal budget (e.g. 2 M USD for Wakulima and Gikomba markets in 2019)13. In 
addition, main dealers of the markets collect fee (informally) from smaller operators to allow access the 
market and trade on site.  
Food distribution context of fruits and vegetables in Kenya is characteristic of emerging food economies 
(low-income countries): 
 Food marketing is fragmented or dispersed through a large network of small rural and urban 
retailers.  
 The traditional sector is composed of small retailers such as small independent shops, grocery 
stores, local kiosks, and retail markets.  
 Modern supply chains are still growing (e.g. the share of supermarkets is small compared to local 
retailers) and represent less than 5 % of food distribution.  
 The informal sector is dominant and generally shapes supply chains, which are dispersed or 
fragmented, with many actors in commercialization. This leads to high food loss rates, increase 
speculation in perishables and encourage unregulated trading. 
Municipalities invest poorly the fee collected in maintenance or development of the market most of the 
time, resulting in outdated or decaying facilities. They usually directly organize waste management and 
cleaning of the markets with municipal services, as well as the security on site. These services are clearly 
poorly delivered and hygiene on the markets is often an issue. Finally, no obligation in terms of food safety 
control is required from market management. Laboratories or food safety agency controls are not the rule 
and markets do not play a role in food safety enforcement. 
The Wakulima case study below highlights the current management framework and revenue model define 
the operations of this market, showing how informality and weak market governance framework impact 
the capacity to create the necessary conditions for investment in OPEX and CAPEX- demanding 
infrastructure.

Several attempts to concentrate wholesale activities in one new wholesale food market led by the 
Government of Kenya have failed, due to a lack of political support and current fragmentation of trading 
activities. In practice, the activities and collection of daily user fees is done through an informal yet fully 
recognised structure of elected trader representatives who de facto manage the daily operations of the 
market and communicate issues to the formal management. 
Revenue model 
Revenues are based on two kinds of market fees. The first is a fee paid by traders or retailers for market 
access and to rent stalls; the second is a fee paid by intermediaries per unit of commodity traded on the 
market. The fees constitute an important contribution to the city council’s budget. NCC collects daily 
fees from the main wholesale food markets (in partiular Wakulima and Gikomba), representing annual 
revenues of USD 2 million in 2019. Majority of wholesale food market revenues are used to fund general 
public service expenditure in Nairobi, rather than maintenance of market infrastructure or investment in 
new facilities for trading fresh products. The local authorities consider the wholesale food markets as a 
tax-collection mechanism and source of income for the public budget, rather than as a tool for upgrade 
of the agri-food supply chain in the city. 
Food Loss and Waste 
The volume of waste generated daily by the market, as well as the deficient sanitation and hygiene 
conditions within the outdated and congested infrastructure, remain persistent problems for the 
municipality. The lack of sanitation infrastructure and drainage also means that the market floods when 
there is heavy rain, in turn affecting the hygiene and sanitary situation of trading activities.  
Most waste is generated by the accumulation of relatively waste left behind the retail operations and 
wholesalers keeping their products overnight in the trucks they transport in, often without adequate (if 
any) packaging and crating. An estimated 30-45tons of waste a day are removed from the market, a 
waste which is not viewed as a significant or recuperable loss by the individual traders.  
Existing plans for upgrade of facilities 
Wakulima is to be relocated by the County Government due to a network of new roads going through 
the area. In the last few years, on behalf of the users, the County has constructed a new market with 
improved facilities, including cold chain, which is located 5km from the current site, in a different 
administrative ward. However, despite the improved infrastructure both market management (formal 
and informal) and traders have refused to relocate for a number of reasons, which include: lack of clarity 
on the management and user arrangements; relationship with other existing retail markets in the area 
which would view this as competition and create conflict; lack of consultation with the current users and 
provisions for the impact this would have on trade and customers (both retail and wholesale); the lack 
of expressed desire of the current users to upgrade their work conditions at the expense of relocation; 
lack of clarity on management structures, fees, maintenance and a cost/benefit analysis for the new 
premises that would ensure their continued operations.

1.5.3. Key steps in Wholesale Fresh Market development 
WFM upgrade and development is a process that requires far more than investment in infrastructure 
creation. Among the key principles that should guide the process are those of ownership, participatory 
planning and economic and financial sustainability. To ensure the long-term success of WFM upgrades a 
series of key crosscutting dimensions should be closely addressed: 
 The regulatory framework of wholesale trade. Policy and fiscal frameworks support the 
evolution of markets and promote successful investments in these infrastructures, supporting as 
well sustainable business models and urban interventions that contribute to local or regional 
socioeconomic development. Addressing the regulatory framework when undertaking wholesale 
food market projects ensures having instruments for WFM development and financial sustainability 
while effectively working towards agrifood systems efficiency. The main objectives are to create 
an enabling environment for wholesale trade and WFM development. It should be built from the 
consultation with market actors to capture the context of the wholesale trade sector and how the 
regulation will serve their operations and those of the supply chain. 
 WFM governance. Inclusion of the different market actors in decision-making structures are key 
to successful investment processes and efficient operations. The multiplicity of actors involved in 
market operations, and the public nature of their services reinforce the need of inclusive forms of 
governance of markets and other core agrifood system infrastructures. 
 WFM Business model. Market investments and economic activities should also be based on 
inclusivity, given the diversity of actual and potential market investors and operators, as well as 
based on flexibility, given changing preferences and demands from consumers.  
 Infrastructure and logistics. Successful projects need to pay close attention to the suitability of 
market locations, the suitability of market concepts and functions to the operational context, and 
the adequacy of facilities and equipments to users’ needs, even anticipating food systems stresses. 
Upgrades in wholesale food markets, as intermediary stages in food value chains, then become strategic 
interventions impacting upon the overall stability and sustainability of domestic agrifood systems, with 
related contributions to global food markets.

of the WFM network.

1.6. Conclusions  
 
What are the qualitative and quantitative characteristics (Deep Dives) of selected businesses as 
intervention entry points that demonstrate viable cold chain investments in the small-scale dairy 
sector and in the domestic horticulture sector? 
The two Deep Dives into the milk ATM and Horticulture Aggregator business models linked to a 
decentralized cold storage facility, reveal that even when the technology cost of investment and operations 
costs can be met, the successful performance of the business depends also on other factors related to 
compliance with food safety and quality standards (as is the case for milk) or ability to optimize logistics 
and capitalize on emerging structured retail demand. 
In the case of milk ATMs, the key risks associated with the investment are the availability and reliability 
of the pasteurized milk quality and safety, as well as the technology performance, both of which are 
currently a major hurdle to regulatory compliance and result in business closures. 
In the case of investment in cold rooms, the success of the investment depends on the capacity to manage 
complex food logistics and supply chain dynamics that can optimized utility of the facility through 
predictable demand and supply transactions. The operations require expertise in logistics and overview of 
the market which is best performed by a larger aggregator and as part of a larger operation with similar 
installations and capacity to maintain temperature-controlled environment all the way to delivery at point 
of retail. 
Both milk ATMs and large aggregators wanting to expand structured formal supply chain networks depend 
on quality and consistent supply of produce. This demand is what offers an opportunity for the producer 
organizations (POs) supported by NAVCDP to demonstrate their production capacities and establish 
themselves on the market. The project POs can reduce investment risk by demonstrating improved capacity 
to deliver safe and quality produce. 
 
What do providers of finance and technology need to scale up the deployment of cold chain?  
Financing for MSME development, requiring small investment capital support with little collateral and lean 
interest rates is what is on the demand-side. Overall, financial institutions lack understanding and capacity 
to deal with the agri-logistics technology and food chain supply risks. However, a few financial insitutions 
have a positive track record in the dairy sector and expressed a strong interest to finance milk ATMs 
investments but may require some capacity building to better understand assess the different business 
models, technologies and companies operating in this field. 
The risk profile of these investment projects should be considered as acceptable by the relevant financiers. 
If this is not the case, the provision of de-risking instruments (like first-loss guarantees for banks or first-
loss capital for funds) could provide additional comfort to financiers. Guarantees could theoretically 
replace the collateral that most agri SMEs do not have. But they are also quite complex to implement at 
branches level and often result into additional administrative burden and transaction costs for financiers. 
Ultimately, guarantees alone are often insufficient to significantly amend banks’ lending strategies and 
investment criteria.  
Investees should have the capacity to properly operate cold chain equipment. The provision of technical 
assistance to SMEs, cooperatives or farmers in the form of trainings can help to address this concern.   
Financiers could better understand cold chain technology. Technical assistance could help to improve 
their level of knowledge and make them more comfortable with the different cold chain technologies. 
Lessons learnt from pilots should be widely disseminated.  
Financiers need a strong commitment from cold chain equipment suppliers to offer after-sale support like 
spare parts, maintenance and repair services. Technology providers should have some skin in the game to 
ensure that interests are aligned and they will not disappear once the equipment has been sold. Buy-back 
guarantee schemes can address this concern, with the equipment provider taking the responsibility to re-
purchase the equipment at a pre-agreed valuation in case of default from the borrower. This would also help
=== PAGE 58 ===
 
 
58
to mitigate concerns about the liquidity and value of cold chain equipment in case of default. Structuring 
the financing of the equipment via the technology provider can be another way to answer these challenges.  
More specifically, providers of technology need16 (i) patient capital to invest in research in technology 
and business models to develop more affordable cold chain solutions adapted to the local context, (ii) 
affordable credit lines (in both hard and local currencies) to fund working capital needs (building stocks of 
cold chain components locally, offering more attractive payment terms to customers etc.), (iii) simplified 
import procedures and reduced custom duties to import key cold chain components that are not produced 
in Kenya, (iv) de-risking instruments to supply customers with a more risky profile (e.g. agri SMEs 
operating on domestic value chains and lacking collateral etc.), and eventually (v) grants or results-based-
financing to pilot innovative cold chain solutions and disseminate the lessons learnt from these experiences.  
The role of public finance in the context of cold chain deployment is primarily linked to the de-risking 
role that sector development projects, such as NAVCDP play in the production sector, through climate-
proofing agricultural outputs and strengthening POs to engage effectively in commercial partnerships. 
Public sector resources are also important to invest in the enabling environment and food system review 
and development, which in turn helps to leverage private finance and de-risk lending through increased 
transparency, structuring and formalization of the food supply chains. 
 
What conditions should be put in place to ensure the financial and operations sustainability of 
infrastructure-related investments and upgrade of wholesale food markets?   
A key precondition for the sustainability of infrastructure-related investments and upgrade of wholesale 
food markets is the deployment of a holistic approach with appropriate management and revenue models 
in place.  
Wholesale Food Markets (WFM) cannot be seen in separation from the food system of which they are part, 
and which shapes their role and organization, in Kenya they are in the centre of the informal market which 
comprises around 80% of trade.  
The role of public-private partnerships is key in the sustainable ownership and management of essential 
agro-food infrastructure and logistics hubs such as WFMs. Market characteristics, such as volumes traded, 
nature of operations and role in the overall supply chain are key determinants to the potential management 
arrangements to be considered in Kenya.

2.1. Background and objective of the report

subsistence or smallholders; and produce consumed at home or bought at rural markets. On the contrary, a

2.3. Marketing of horticultural produce 
The horticultural value chain can be characterized as short and rapid: a tomato is estimated to reach its final 
retail point within no more than 30 hours of harvest (USAID 2018), with transport time estimated at 
maximum 6 hours from any point in Rwanda to Kigali. However, produce often arrives with great quality 
and quantity deterioration largely due to physical mishandling on rough roads, lacking of packaging and 
crating and exposure to direct sun. There are significant price fluctuations in the agricultural markets (see 
Figure 11) indicating a low degree of integration and rendering earnings unpredictable. Domestic supply 
chains (including to regional markets in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Burundi and Tanzania) are 
largely informal, with only exports to international markets (Europe, Middle East) and high-end markets 
(selected hotels in Kigali) characterized as formal, modern supply chains.

The domestic market value chains in Rwanda rely largely on aggregators, middlemen and other buyers 
excavating produce from local areas using open trucks, collected either at farm or at cooperative or other 
types of collection centers, and based on informal relations between farmers and traders (no supply 
contracts, no stable demand and no agreed upon prices); with limited adherence to standards; and relations 
can at times be characterized as mistrusting of one another. Several reports touch upon the professionalism 
and problem-solving ability of buyers and traders. WUR 2021 indicates that there are very few professional 
business intermediaries, with the majority being unsophisticated with little professionalism to innovate and 
make their businesses and relations more efficient. Further, KWM (2021) indicates that cooperatives are 
but weaker in trade of fruits and vegetables, than staples crops17.  
Farmers depend on buyers to come to their locality, without much prediction in terms of time, quantities, 
or prices. Some cooperatives are able to transport their produce to the nearest markets, and while this 
reduces transaction costs, it does not create reliability in terms of market. Transport costs are high: estimated 
at RWF 150 per mt/km with an average trip of 120 km, transporting 10 mt per trip (VE 2022). Produce is 
bought by traders at collection centers or transported directly to regional or Kigali markets by farmers; 
according to Key Informants, farmers do not wish to transport their produce to the markets, but are forced 
to do so, if collection by buyers is delayed, and the products risk spoilage.  
Open trucks are the most common means of transport, with produce packed in traditional sacks of about 50 
kgs and movement mainly at night when the temperature is cooler. Stakeholder interviews indicated that 
while traditional methods of transport and use of cloth sacks leads to some losses, it is still cheaper to incur 
those loses than to invest in upgrades such as the use of cold trucks and crates. It is also alleged that the use 
of crates (after preliminary sorting and packing) reduces the quantity of produce transported thus further 
impacting profits. KWM (2021) indicates that the business case for improved packaging has not been 
proven; despite reduced food loses, the quantity of produce transported at once reduces significantly. 
However, a more systematic emphasis on packaging and primary processing at collection sites, combined 
with road improvements, could unlock significant part of the food loses experienced and upgrade the quality 
of the horticultural supply chains.  
Majority of produce is sold in traditional, open, daily or weekly markets, both in rural areas and in Kigali. 
The value of produce sold amounts to USD 279 million per year (VE 2022). 6-10 open markets exist in 
Kigali; operate as both wholesale retail markets, with Nyabugogo being the major wholesale market. These 
markets implement limited quality standards; availability of produce is largely based on supply and not 
demand, and there is limited appropriate storage for unsold produce.  
Retail outlets have been growing over the past 3-5 years, with outlets like Sawa City, Simba Supermarkets 
and Frulep. They serve about 1-2% of the wealthiest households in Kigali (KWM 2021). These 
supermarkets are still relatively small and have limited fresh vegetable sections which are not necessarily

2.3.1. Consumer preference 
Consumer preferences and low purchasing power largely drive the market, in the absence of demand drivers 
like high-end retail and domestic processing. According to FAO Stat, the average consumption per capita 
per year of fruits and vegetables in Rwanda is 45 kgs per year, below the African average of 54 kg per year, 
and well below the global average of 134 kgs per year. WHO recommends consumption of 400g of fruits 
and vegetables per day, or 71 kgs per year; of which Rwanda consumes less than a quarter (WHO 2020).  
The National Institute of Statistics Rwanda’s Agricultural Household Survey 2017, finds that the diet of 
urban and rural Rwandans changes as income grows, but that both poor and non-poor households consume 
vegetables (VE 2022). Poor households eat one to two meals per day, consisting mainly of staples, roots, 
and tubers, supplemented by meat or fish, and cooked vegetables. Vegetables and fruits may take up to 7% 
of the budget. Wealthier households eat at least 3 meals per day, with fresh fruits and vegetables comprising 
up to 9.2% of the budget. This is in line with the sub-Saharan average of spending between 3 and 13% of 
income on fruits and vegetables, as poorer households tend to prioritize cheaper staples, combined with 
some cultural preferences (FAO 2020). Wealthier households are able to purchase in bulk, but only 8.5% 
of the urban population and by 0.2% of the rural population have fridges to continue the cold chain at home 
(RCOOL 2020); and there is a general poor understanding amongst stakeholder of food safety standards 
(WUR 2021). Anecdotal evidence indicates that most households use the retailers for dry and processed 
items, but still rely on local markets for fresh produce; in part due to price and perceived freshness; reports 
indicate that there is a perception that produce from cold rooms is not fresh (MINAGRI, page 13).  
2.3.2. Exports and formal value chains  
The formal export value chains account only for about 6% (KWM 2021) of produce. Rwanda targets a ten-
fold increase in horticultural exports between 2018 and 2024 and the sector has grown rapidly over the last 
few years (Dahlberg 2020). There are between 30 and 100 active exporters18, exporting mainly avocado, 
chilies (green and habanero), French beans and okra to the Middle East and Europe.  
The export sector can be characterized as young and lacks professionalization. For example there is no 
farm-to-airport logistics operator, leaving exporters to negotiate with airlines individually (Dalberg 2020). 
The sector is also bureaucratic with different places to do different documentation and phytosanitary 
paperwork). Key Informants indicate that to access working capital from banks, a letter of credit is needed, 
however, this is not accessible through the air freight export modality, and most lack stable contracts abroad.

more formal value chain to the domestic markets.  
2.3.3. Regional markets  
Rwanda exports meat, fish and milk, but also horticultural products to neighboring countries including 
DRC, Uganda, Burundi and Tanzania. The chains are less structured and with limited demand for quality 
standards, or enforcement (KWM 2021). Kenya is a largely unexploited market, partly due to bureaucratic 
boarder clearing processes. A Key Informant explained that his truck of 13 tons of avocado spent 5 days 
crossing the border into Kenya, by which time produce was spoilt. However, anecdotal evidence indicated 
that there is a large potential for exports, including in exploiting seasonal variations, for example supply 
during the dry seasons in Kenya.  
Several border points have cold stores installed and managed by the Ministry of Trade (MINICOM). 
Traders actively rent space to store their produce as they wait for clients. COVID 19 is reported to have 
negatively impacted cross-border trade (MINAGRI), and it is estimate to account for 3% of the horticultural 
trade, worth USD 9 million (VE 2022). The management model is reportedly successful and represents 
some of the few cases of cold chain infrastructure in market places in Rwanda (further discussed in the 
sections below). It needs to be further analyzed to capture best practices and opportunities.  
2.3.4. Certification  
Different types of certification exist in relation to horticultural produce. The GAP (Global G.A.P.) is the 
most important for export to European markets; but others also exist, for example the British Retail 
Consortium (BRC) and Ethical Trading Initiative (SMETA audit). A Rwanda GAP certification also exists 
but is not being implemented. Certification is expensive and only 3-5 companies currently have the Global 
GAP certification in Rwanda (KWM 2021 indicates that majority of exporters from Kenya and Uganda 
have this certification). The certification must be renewed every two years. However, the cost is found 
prohibitive by Key Informants; both due to the fact that inspectors need to be brough in from Kenya or 
other places abroad; as well as related investment required on farm, for example training of workers and 
installation of infrastructure, like the sufficient number of latrines; and personal protective equipment, 
harvesting structures, hygiene and traceability. The lack of certification is a bottleneck for expansion of the 
export market; other markets (e.g., the Middle East) do not impose certification restrictions; however, these 
markets pay less and exporters face more global competition and less stable demand.  
The domestic certification “Made in Rwanda” label is primarily for marketing, including in supermarkets 
and tourist locations in Rwanda. The acknowledgement of the certification abroad has not been established. 
HACCP Certification, a food safety measure, is the most prevalent on the local market and is gaining

2.4. Cold storage in Rwanda  
Cold chain development in Rwanda began in 2011 when the opportunity for horticultural exports was 
identified (VE 2022). This report mainly uses the term “cold storage”, to cover a number of processes 
through the value chain, which may slow down the metabolism of fruits and vegetables and extend their 
shelf life. Cold storage can play an important role in primary processing, packing and sorting in the rural 
areas, adding value to the produce and in reducing food losses. About 42 facilities for storge of horticultural 
produce exist; majority accessible to smallholder farmers; and some private; a full mapping of the existing 
cold room infrastructure is presented in Annex. USAID 2018 estimates that there is around 6161 cubic 
meters of cold room capacity in the country (2019); with the ability to store 470 MT of produce. Less than 
50% of those accessible to farmers are operational; and even fewer are in use; utilization is estimated at 
1146 MT (USAID 2018), i.e. 19% of the space, when excluding flowers. The used space largely targets 
exports; however, even here, there is no evidence of uninterrupted cold chains in the country, outside more 
closed production systems operated by a companies with control over all nodes of the supply chain; and 
moreso for floriculture than horticulture at large. A number of cold rooms existing for storage of imports; 
as well as for milk and vaccines.  
The types of cold storage relevant for horticultural marketing in Rwanda include: (i) cold stores, in varying 
sizes and locations; both publicly and privately owned. WB 2020 indicates that 9% of companies for whom 
cold storage could be relevant, have a cold room; VE (2022) presents the case of six companies which have 
developed vertically integrated business, which includes cold chain provision (VE 2022), though the size 
of operations is limited. (ii) cold trucks in limited use on domestic markets but prevalent for airport 
transport. WB (2020) estimates that 5% of the relevant companies have refrigerated trucks; (iii) cold storage 
unites (boxes) at marketplaces, still in the pilot phase; (iv) domestic refrigerators, used in 1.6 percent of 
Rwandese households (RCOOL 2020), but available in small shops. Some solutions are constructed cold 
storage with packaging and preprocessing rooms, while others are mobile or marketing cold units; and (v) 
traditional methods. There is limited availability of other supportive technology (like refer trucks, thermal 
bags, insulated boxes and cold trucks).  
Traditional methods of storing and handling produce are in use in Rwanda, and, in many cases, perform 
well in terms of the length of the transport chains and the demands of the producers, as well as the 
preferences of farmers (VE 2022). Consumers also have a preference for “fresh produce” directly from the 
farm the same night, not from the cold store. Rwandan temperatures (20-27 degrees during the day and 10-
14 degrees at night with high humidity during rainy seasons) are acceptable levels for passive cooling 
(WUR 2021a). Challenges in providing the right cooling at the right time (in the most energy efficient way,

In addition to low utilization, research from USAID (2018) shows that the related facilities, like a packing

despite this being the intention, due to dependency on rainwater to use the facilities. These related services

critical, both to maintain quality of the produce and ensuring food safety, as well as to enable buyer’s easy 
access. USAID (2018) document that 21% of inspected cold rooms were not clean; 29% had maintenance 
issues; and 38% lacked a waste dumping place. They further document that up to 50% of the cold stores 
visited have earth or rock surroundings. 37% is either cemented, tarmacked, paved or tiled.   
Cold trucks remain expensive although available in the local market for example with Thermo King and

from 250F for a regular truck (Key Informant). The professionalism and reliability of the cold truck 
operators must also be apparent to farmers to develop proper engagements. In addition, the business case 
for using cold trucks to transport horticultural produce from the farm is not obvious. One Key Informant 
had won a cold truck through a competition but did not demonstrate knowledge on how to use this for 
business, besides anecdotes about high income streams. There was also no evidence of insulation tucks 
being available in the country, i.e., simple insulated units which can keep down the temperature of the 
produce, without actually cooling it. In addition to using trucks as means of transportation, several Key

are small. A truck can for example park in a village for 1-2 days as avocado is being harvested; to store the 
produce. Once harvesting is done, the trader takes the avocado directly out. One pilot took place in exporting 
avocados to Dubai via sea; according to Key Informants, this exercise required import of reefer containers 
for transport, currently not available in the market.

Criteria to be considered when investing in cold storage solutions should include the management models,

2.4.1. Location  
According to USAID (2018), 11% of cold storage infrastructure is used as collection and distribution 
infrastructure; 75% at farm level; 13% at border posts; and 2% for industrial research and training related 
to agriculture processing. The locations have largely been decided based on quantity of production expected 
in the futures. However, these locations neither correspond to locations in which transactions already take 
place and which could be improved with the given technology; nor to the locations of majority of food 
losses (which happen at harvesting and post-harvest stage, during transport, as well as in traditional open 
retail and wholesale markets).  
Traditional markets in Kigali as well as regional centers are the main places where produce is sold and 
purchased. There is limited cold chain storage available at these, although several stakeholders requested 
solutions especially for overnight storage of horticultural markets. However, there is limited experience in 
the country on how such infrastructure would be managed and priced. The Kigali Wholesale Market, 
currently under construction, will seek to drive market development, formalize and improve chains, as well 
as introduce new commission agent models for aggregation, amongst others through introduction of cold 
storage. the market envisages to be a bulking and wholesale market for formal and informal exporters 
(except for those who have direct backward linkages with farmers and thus do their own aggregation); as 
well as for trade within Kigali. The Market envisages to have state of the art cold chain available to traders, 
managed by a management company, either through concession or other market management models (this 
has not yet been determined in detail). It is envisaged that farmers will supply directly to the Kigali 
Wholesale Market, and that exporters would buy directly from there, thereby cutting out the 
trader/aggregator/middleman function and pushing towards formalization of the domestic market. Lessons 
developed under Municipal Wholesale Food Markets (Horticulture) above, are also relevant for the 
Rwandan context.  
For exports, where there is a clear case for the need of cold chains, the entry of the produce into the cold 
chain, as well as primary processing, tends to happen in Kigali, near the export points, and not in the rural 
areas; however, there does not seem to be a price or significant quality premium for produce entering the 
cold chain already at farm or collection center level.  
2.4.2. Management and maintenance   
There is no dominant model for management of the existing cold storage. In some cases, fees are paid for 
storage on monthly or daily basis, depending on the space occupied, or for the whole unit (for example in 
the facility operated by the Private Sector Foundation in Musanze, as well as in MINCOM cold rooms at 
border points; as well as for the one operational leasing business identified (see below). Other spaces are 
provided for free; in some cases with the consumer needing to pay for electricity and other direct costs. 
This is commonly handled by the District. There is one example of a storage facility being owned by NAEB 
but managed by a private company; however, this is in relation to flowers. Finally, there is infrastructure 
installed, maintained and exclusively used by private companies, without providing access to others, for 
example SawaCity and Eat Green. Very few examples exist besides these, with majority of the installed 
cold stores for horticulture not having clear ownership or management models. Full details are provided in 
Annex 6.  
VE (2022) document in detail the situation around the SAIP cold rooms; including limited understanding 
by cold room operators; lack of maintenance contracts; no formal management structures and no business 
models for their use. They conclude that infrastructure maintenance contract could be entered into with

Centre of Excellence for Sustainable Cooling and Cold Chain (ACES), hosted within the University of 
Rwanda. ACES will deliver courses on cold chain technology, operations, and maintenance to students to 
fill part of the capacity gap. 
2.4.3. Business models  
As described above (section 1.3.1), there are several models documented in detail in Kenya, for the 
acquisition of cold chain equipment or the access to cooling as a service. These include upfront purchase; 
lease-to-own; asset financing; sale-lease back; rental models; and pay as you go / cooling as a service. For 
Rwanda, majority of existing infrastructure is public-financed; however, a few cases within each models 
do exist.  
A few companies are setting up access to cold storage and packing space to enable buyers to supply better 
quality, and document the model of Garden Fresh who are piloting the use of aggregators to purchase from 
farmers and supply high quality produce; KWM (2021) documents this experience. Another example if that 
of Izere, who, using cold trucks, facilities and primary processing machines purchased with the support of 
a matching grant, use cold storage from farmer collection point to premium buyers in Kigali. This company 
was one of selected few to receive matching grant support under the SAIP project. The cross-border cold 
storage infrastructure seems to be working under a MINICOM model where traders pay monthly 
subscriptions to the district officers, with maintenance costs covered by MINICOM. For several other 
infrastructures, it’s not clear how maintenance will be ensured. Supermarkets such as SawaCity and Simba 
Supermarkets, as well as high end hotels like Radisson and Marriot, own cold chain infrastructure financed 
through their own means. Other hotels, like Lemigo, have received “matching” financial support to upgrade 
their infrastructure. Common for all of these are that they are privately owned; located within the business 
locality in Kigali and do not provide access to cooling services for other stakeholders.  
A few examples exist of companies offering cold storage solutions through leasing. One of these is Izumi 
who operates two stores where individual companies can rent space (cubic meters) for storage of 
horticulture and dairy products, respectively, in Kigali markets (see Error! Reference source not found.). 
The store is operational, however, not fully utilized. Another example is Munyax Eco, who is piloting a 
leasing model in rural areas; however, initial engagements with cooperatives have not been successful. The 
technology was received through an innovation finance development program; the company is a solar 
equipment provider (outside the agricultural sector). EnDEV have similarly piloted a “pay-as-you-go” solar 
cooling access, however, the initial pilot faced challenges in the uptake by users and the lack of solid 
business cases to guide the investment and its proposed use. Common for these experiences is that, while 
the technology has been efficient, the business model (ownership, operations, and location) has not matched 
the supply chain. Some of the stakeholders cited that the cooperatives with whom they worked did not live 
up to the expectation; and others that there was very little user demand for the facilities. Both Munyax Eco 
and EnDEV were considering alternate locations and leasing models for the technology.

2.5. Technology and functionality  
Rwanda has a quite good coverage of electric grid, compared to neighbors. Government envisages that at 
least 48% will have access to off-grid power solutions by 2024. Still, electricity consumption per capita is 
the lowest in the East African Community (at 30 kWh) due to the high cost. Electricity is largely generated 
in thermal power plants where petroleum is used for energy generation and consumer prices are subsidized 
at high cost to the Government (RCOOL 2020). However, several reports indicate that the cost of electricity 
for cold storage usage is a concern for users; and that cold room users at times face challenges with power 
supply interruptions. FAO (2021) demonstrates a clear case for adopting renewable energy options, which 
is also confirmed through field work.   
Several types of cooling systems are used in the country. Relevant technologies are available through a few 
resident suppliers, but largely imported from abroad. Available technology use both traditional and green 
energy sources and provide different models for companies or clients to purchase and install the equipment. 
Examples of technology providers include Geerlofs, Inspira Farms, and D-Grid Energy and Freshbox. As 
concluded by WUR (2021), there is a need to ensure the quality standards are adapted to the local context, 
sometimes providing less high-tech options.  
Cold rooms constructed by NAEB and MINICOM are generally on-grid; the SAIP infrastructure is off-
grid, with plans to develop hybrid options; and 17% of cold storage happened with charcoal coolers 
(USAID); others again use zero-energy cooling systems (built with brick and sand). While solar options are 
largely promoted and at times driving the investments, several technological challenges are reported with 
this approach; including insufficient cooling (KWM 2021); short time span of batteries (4 hours) (VE 2022); 
and the need for grid electricity if the weather is overcast for 1-2 days (Key Informant at cold room in Kigali 
and others). Several initiatives are on-going of testing and piloting various cooling and energy technologies, 
e.g., SE for All, and ACES; in most assessed cases, technological challenges relate less to the actual

experience of different stakeholders on ground. Stakeholders have confirmed that the mentioned challenges 
could and probably would be overcome by the stakeholders themselves, if there were sufficient economic 
rationale and capacity to do so. For future investments, hybrid solutions would be most relevant.  
2.6. Regulatory and policy environment  
In 2019, Rwanda adopted the National Cooling Strategy; spelling out strategies related to renewable energy 
usage, refrigerants, cooling systems specifications, requirements for labelling etc., largely focused on end 
use like air conditioners and refrigerators in private homes. The final objective of the strategy does mention 
agriculture but does not go beyond the need to mobilize investors. Under the ACES, a specific cold storage 
strategy will be developed, covering health, energy and agriculture sectors, based also aligned to a Post 
Harvest strategy which is at final stages of approval. The Strategic Plan for Agriculture Transformation 
(PSTA) Phase 5 (currently under development) is expected to follow the drive from the PSTA4, towards 
using energy efficient cooling to improve supply chain logistics.  
Food regulation has in Rwanda to date largely focused on the more perishable, and potentially harmful to 
human health products, like milk and meat. Several gaps exist for horticulture. The Rwanda Standards 
Board (PSB) has set a number of standards for horticultural products on the processing side, noting that 
adherence to these standards is voluntary as well as inspection of cold room calibration and temperature; 
all of which comes at the cost of the user20. They also provide HACCP certification. There is no information 
available on the degree of compliance, but Key Informants have indicated that privately produced coolers 
(e.g., altering second hand imported storage facilities from Kenya) are used in the market and at times use 
coolants which are banned21as well as other compliance issues. Hospitality service providers in addition 
carry out their own certifications.  
For fresh products, the Rwanda Inspectorate, Competition and Consumer Products (RICA) is the relevant 
authority, but are faced with several gaps, since their establishment in 2017. RICA’s mandate lies within 
quality control of fresh and unprocessed food; compliance with competitive processes; and consumer rights. 
RICA actively inspects milk collection centers and milk transport; as well as the NAEB packhouse in 
Kigali; including verifying that the cold chain is maintained. They also undertake Rwanda GAP 
certification.  
A Code of Practice for Fruits and Vegetables exists; as well as laws and Ministerial orders; however, a 
technical regulation for handling of fruits and vegetables on the domestic market is not in place. Specific 
regulation and standards are missing on temperature and handling aspects, both for fresh and processed 
products, leaving compliance with phytosanitary measures optional. No monitoring and enforcement exist 
at the local level, and no data on compliance is available. Another regulatory gap exists in the area of food 
safety, where the FDA is still awaiting final approval of the relevant laws and regulations, in draft form 
since 2013. 
Exported crops are however subject to both technical regulation and inspection at the point of departure, 
but inspection is rarely carried out upstream of the value chain. Most of inspection in the Kigali packhouses 
relates to phytosanitary compliance and, although no statistics exist anecdotal evidence suggest regular 
cases of non-compliance. Processes like packing and handling are rarely looked into. Non-compliant 
produce is directed to local markets. Some cases do exist of rejection of produce in export markets.

2.7. Financing  
As in many countries in the region, the agricultural sector in Rwanda receives less financing and loans from 
banks than other sectors. Rwanda has 5 large commercial banks which hold 76% of all assets, with almost 
50% of those held by the Bank of Kigali which is partially state-owned; but 11 commercial banks exist, as 
well as formal and limited liability micro-finance institutions, development and cooperative banks as well 
as a network of SACCOs (RCOOL 2020). The study further indicates that 89% of the banks are interested 
to finance energy-efficient cooling appliance and other green financing; but only about 1/3 actively do so 
and Key Informants indicate challenges to access such funds in terms of collateral, ticket size, credit history 
etc. The cost of cold chain to purchase, operate and maintain cold storage is high. A cold storage unit (the 
box itself, without construction) is about USD 25,000 including import and installation (Key Informant); 
and between USD 100 and 300,000 for a 50 sqm cold store with multiple power supply options (WUR 
2021). CLASP (2022) estimate that the cost of financing cold storage investments is 15%22; and for this 
reason, no real market exists for the equipment.  
Almost all investment is public or through development partner initiatives. VE (2022) have documented 
the experience of Eat Green, a company collecting from out growers and maintaining a cold chain all the 
way to premium markets and consumers; the company invested 30% and borrowed the rest from a bank. 
No other cold chain infrastructure financed through banks has been identified. Other investments are driven 
by the desire to implement innovative and/or energy efficient solutions, where for example VAT 
exemptions exist for the import of solar equipment. However, as described above, many of these solutions 
have not been implemented adequately in the agricultural context. Key Informants indicated that, but they 
had not been able to find qualified partners on the ground to use it (e.g., cooperatives or traders engaged in 
horticultural production). Others, that the locations for deploying the infrastructure was not correct, seeking 
to use the innovative technologies to create a business, rather than strengthen one.

2.8. Conclusion  
Significant investment already exists into cold chain infrastructure in Rwanda; however, majority is public 
or donor funded. Most of the existing infrastructure is not in use and it is not evident that cold chain 
solutions, at the moment, contribute towards decreased food loses. However, cold chain remains relevant 
if Rwanda is to reach middle income status by 2035 and high-income status by 2050; and as urbanization23 
and demand for premium quality produce grows, and as food chains become “food smart” and modern, 
contributing to the global agenda of reducing food waste and food losses. The Rwandan agri-food system 
for horticulture requires upgrading before being ripe for more cold chain investment, outside the existing 
financing opportunities (like CDAT). There is need for a “pull” effect and formalization of the value chains; 
this can be stimulated through increased exports, consumer demand on the domestic market, as well as 
through regulation.  
Formal export chains have a more organized agri-food system, but still require upgrading. The export sector 
is young and cannot currently drive domestic market formalization. In domestic markets, only a very small 
premium market demands, and is willing to pay for, increased quality and shelf life. VE (2022) reject that 
there is no commercial rationale for smallholders to utilize cold rooms due to obvious benefits in reducing 
losses, increasing aggregation quantities and thus bargaining power and the potential to reach high value 
markets especially if the cost to the farmer is reduced, for example through larger storage facilities; 
however, the study also reflects that farmers face significant barriers to obtain these potential benefits. 
KWM (2021) concludes that affordability greatly impacts cold chain usage, in particular when market 
linkages are not assured. This report concludes that affordability is not the main constraint to cold chain 
investments, but rather a lack of clear business cases for how farmers can reap the presented opportunities 
in the current market context. Farmers remain dependent on buyers to come and collect their produce, and 
 
23 International Growth Center (2020) estimate that Kigali city might grow with 2.5 million by 2032, adding 
375,000 urban households who will need to be fed.

as such there is limited scope for negotiation and limited demand for more efficient or structured value 
chains.  
However, several opportunities do exist to further reduce food losses and modernize the value chains. First, 
efficiency and effectiveness of cold storage infrastructure investments could be improved through the use 
of mobile storage and expanding the use of cold trucks; in strategic places and promoting specialized 
business models in places where trade already happens, for example in rural and urban markets; There are 
several aspects which are currently promising in terms of strengthening domestic demand, including (i) 
new initiatives like the Kigali Wholesale Market and other business hubs; (iii) increasing urbanization and 
evolution of Kigali as a modern city and (iii) expanding hospitality sector in both Kigali and tourism areas. 
Driven by AirRwanda and 5 star hotels, food safety standards are penetrating lower levels of the market (4 
star hotels and slowly towards supermarkets), providing interesting opportunities for farmers and suppliers 
to enter into the formal chains. However, it should be noted that contracts are not commonly used, providing 
a hindrance for suppliers and farmers to expand their business.      
2.9. Opportunities for investments and next steps  
Recommendations for immediate action by World Bank and its partners, in reflection of the above analysis, 
are grouped into 7 areas, further described in detail below: (1) upgrade of the agro-logistics systems; (2) 
remove bottlenecks for expanding exports; (3) build capacity of farmers and the institutions to decrease 
post-harvest losses; (4) relocate existing storage capacity to places with large volumes of transactions at the 
moment; (5) build the capacity of private sector and financiers to improve their operations and tailor these 
to the agricultural context; (6) strengthen the regulatory environment; and (7) increase demand for high 
quality produce on domestic markets (by hotels, public institutions and educated consumers at large). In 
addition, it is assumed that already planned interventions (e.g. C-DAT matching grants and further HACCP 
certification) would continue as planned.  
1. Build capacity of farmers and their institutions to decrease post-harvest losses. Traditional 
investments into good agricultural practices and standards for harvesting (timing vis a vis ripeness, 
harvesting methods like leaving part of the stem) and post-harvest handling, should continue. This 
includes optimal use of agro-chemicals in accordance with best practices in the area of efficient 
and healthy production and to increase post-harvest properties of vegetables and fruits. In addition, 
hospitality sector key informants uniformly expressed interests in more diverse varieties: within 
the tomato family, sweeter mangoes, herbs like chives. These could be grown with support, 
including in greenhouses. Similarly, trainings on food handling (picking methods, assessing fruit 
ripeness, washing, pesticide application) and its impact on health and safety would be beneficial, 
from the farmer level, to middlemen and suppliers. VE (2022) propose the development of Standard 
Operation Procedures.  
 
2. Place storage capacity in places with larger volumes of transactions at the moment. The 
philosophy here is to use the improved technology and best practice to improve current market 
transactions in places where trade actively happens, and where a high degree of food losses occur. 
Investments in cold storage infrastructure in markets places have to date proven successful and in 
demand by stakeholders (e.g., MINICOM; mobile leasing units), although the experience is limited. 
Storage, including as a service, should be placed in  rural and urban retail and wholesale markets 
and handed over to the districts or market operators for storage of vegetables overnight. Given the 
low opportunity cost associated with non-use in its current locations, storage could initially be done 
for free, stimulating demand at the same time. Similarly, development of management and business 
models are a prerequisite for any investment. Private sector actors expressed the need for more 
storage capacity (as a service) for example in Kigali, as that of suppliers remains limited. This could 
be large packhouses for storing produce for the local market across seasons.
=== PAGE 77 ===
 
 
77
3. Upgrade of the agro-logistics systems along the value chain and includes (i) developing skills  
(through training and financing) in certification and agro-logistics e.g. the use of crates and packing 
materials from the farmer collection centers along with mobile and simple cooling solutions (like 
thermal boxes) and washing and handling of produce; (ii) upgrading of storage units to standard 
and with related facilities, for example packing rooms, stable power supply and fork lift access for 
larger quantities; (iii) last mile connectivity like road access to cold stores. Finally, (iv) a detailed 
study on food losses throughout the value chains would help to target interventions.  
 
4. Build the capacity of private sector to improve their operations and to obtain relevant 
certification for domestic markets (HACCP). This would include actors involved in transport 
and cold chain logistics. A few concrete avenues are recommended here. (i) The private sector 
(including cooperatives) lack professionalization, as well as access to funds for investment. 
Through its existing programs, including the CDAT matching grants facility, the World Bank, and 
other partners through other programs, must ensure that business development services are 
provided to beneficiaries, before, during and after business plan development and receiving of 
financing. In addition, agri-food logistics and quality standards can be enforced through selection 
criteria for any business plan to receive matching grants. This support should include developing 
technical specifications for any procurement, promotion of low cost technologies (like insulated 
boxes) and promoting renewable energy options. (ii) development of business cases for agri-
logistics companies, market management and cold chain operators could demonstrate where and 
how cold chain and related investments “make business sense”. This is for both cooperatives, SMEs 
in logistics, supermarkets, exporters etc. (iii) support skills development directly targeting 
technological needs like cold room maintenance or spare parts construction, through e.g. ACES, 
Rwanda Polytechnic or other avenues. Short courses and exchange visits could be tailored for the 
purpose, and students from existing companies sponsored.  
 
5. Increase demand for high quality produce on domestic markets (by hotels, public institutions 
and educated consumers at large). Demand is critical for formalizing value chains, and at least 
three concrete avenues could be pursued in parallel. (i) increasing local purchase by hotels. 
Currently, a small group of premium hotels and restaurants exist, serving tourists, but largely large 
international conferences. They have cited challenges in procuring from the local market and tend 
to import premium quality vegetables. Government should put in place incentives for these to 
source from local markets, for example tax or VAT exemptions, financing to invest directly into 
working with suppliers or similar. This can overtime increase the demand for premium vegetables 
and fruits. (ii) Government remains a large buyer of food in the local markets, including 
horticultural products. Programs include school feeding, subsidies for canteen operators in public 
buildings (not necessarily monetary but on items like electricity, water, security, etc.), as well as 
feeding of prisoners and soldiers. Hospitals like King Faisal Hospital are already off-takers of 
premium quality produce. One way to increase the impact of these purchases on the local markets 
include to set minimum standards for the use of fruits and vegetables in the programs vis-à-vis 
stables, for example through upgrading nutritional guidelines; and to set quality requirements in all 
public procurement processes, for example on the quality and temperature of tomatoes, traceability 
etc. (iii) Finally, public awareness campaigns on nutritional and health benefits of consuming more 
and better fruits and vegetables are likely to have a big effect on demand, in particular in Kigali 
and as the urban population grows, as is expected in the coming years. RICA, Ministry of Health, 
MINAGRI, FAO and other partners would be well suited to do this.

78
6. Remove bottlenecks for expanding exports. Short term, concrete interventions could include: (i) 
increasing certification of produce by for example training local certifiers, providing, as part of 
matching grants or other investments, financing for certification and improvements of farm 
facilities and strengthening related inspection and enforcement by the regulator (Rwanda Bureau 
of Standards and/or RICA); (ii) dedicating shares of existing lines of credits or matching grants or 
other financial instruments to horticultural exporters using, or intending to use, different types of 
cold chain (for example trucks); and (iii) providing business development services to exporters to 
professionalize their business and marketing contracts abroad. This could include short courses or 
exchange visits.  
 
7. Strengthen on-going interventions and funds; tailored to the agricultural context. Technical 
assistance facilities should be available to support ongoing public and private investments to ensure 
that highly specialized agricultural and value chain expertise is included in design and execution. 
Concrete examples include: (i) strengthening of the business models behind technological 
initiatives, e.g. ensuring that, in the promotion of solar technologies for agriculture, management 
and supply systems are well developed and criteria for awarding a grant; (ii) Further, banks and 
other financing institutions and funds (like those mentioned in Table 4 above) should be trained 
amongst technical as well as senior staff (decision makers) on the identified business models and 
other aspects of agriculture which are critical to the financial viability of investments; and (iii) 
promote flexible financing models to stimulate innovation and uptake of different business and 
technology models. (ivi) In parallel, technical support should be provided to government entities, 
for example NAEB, to ensure that systems used around the packhouse drive the value chain and 
stimulate private sector development; and (iv) close collaboration amongst all relevant stakeholders 
for flagship projects like the Kigali Wholesale Market, for example in developing the market 
management models which are to date not well developed in Rwanda.  
 
8. Strengthen the regulatory environment. Technical and financial support should be provided to 
RICA to fill legislative gaps, as well as to decision makers on the relevance of this regulation, to 
ensure that its importance is acknowledged and legislation, laws and policies passed in a timely 
manner. This should be complemented by investments into inspection and monitoring, as well as 
development of systems to track compliance.  
3. Take-aways from regional perspective and ways forward  
The findings with regards to cold chain deployment in Kenya presented in the pre-feasibility study and in 
this report, as well as the findings with regards to deployment of cold chain in the horticulture sector in 
Rwanda highlight several common issues. They point to the need of assessing cold chain development in 
the context of the broader agenda of food system development and more specifically the following: 
1) Insufficient maturity of domestic food systems to support investment in cold chain technologies 
at scale, including low domestic demand for quality produce, combined with high price 
sensitivity. 
Approach cold chain development interventions from the perspective of long-term wholistic 
support for food system upgrade and transition from traditional to more professionalised, efficient 
and structured agro-logistics.    
2) Current traditional supply chain business models and operations have alternative solutions for 
FWL management – including value and physical losses, which is based on food waste recovery
=== PAGE 79 ===
 
 
79
through product diversion to poor and food insecure markets, providing a level of social protection or 
rejection of the products from the human food chain and diversion to animal feeds. 
In-depth assessment of the domestic consumer market in terms of purchase power segmentation, 
food-security and potential impacts of passing on the cost of investment to final consumers. 
3) Food supply chains are much more localised than in countries with more ‘mature’ and extensive 
supply chains, resulting in localised solutions and shorter distance to markets which operate on the 
principle of fast transactions of a limited volume of perishables. 
Support improvements in low-cost and low-tech primary processing, crating, and packaging as a 
way of reducing food damage, waste and loss within the existing system of operations. 
4) The domestic market modernisation, structuring and food quality and safety regulation evolve 
based of the emergence of demand for sub by an affluent market provided by the middle class and 
the development of a formal retail servicing this demand. 
Tailor interventions to fit the development stage of the food system and the purchase capacity of 
different market segments with view on supporting emerging trends of modernisation 
5) SMEs, aggregators, food companies and others lack professionalization to innovate, grow their 
business and absorb capital  
Business development support should be provided, with a long term perspective, and without 
distorting markets 
6) There is a need for consumer awareness and education around food quality and safety that can 
drive regulatory development and subsequent private sector structuring and upgrade of food 
handling processes. 
Work with retail in improving consumer awareness and demand for quality and safe foods to 
provide the demand for broad-based food system transformation. 
7) The development of formal regional markets will provide a significant boost in the need for cold 
chain technology deployment as supply chains will lengthen and food quality and safety 
requirements will have to meet regionally agreed standards. 
Support regional market development in terms of logistics and regulation to stimulate the business 
deployment of new technologies, including cold chain and unlock current food system development 
bottlenecks due to the limitations of smaller or less affluent domestic market. 
8) Wholesale food markets remain the subject of inefficient public sector-dominated management 
and revenue models that preclude sustainable private sector investment in infrastructure upgrades, 
such as cold chain.  
As the basis for infrastructure investment, work with government and private sector on piloting 
models for market management that seek to build on existing international experience and provide 
more efficient and financially sustainable market system development models. 
9) Wholesale markets are viewed through a narrow infrastructural perspective rather than a 
complex socio-economic service. 
Engage with government and private sector on creating a broad market network strategy for the 
food sector, which clearly defines the flow of goods and services from production to retail and the 
efficiency gains at each stage of re-sale/handling.

Annex 1: Description of finance providers in Kenya 
Acumen. Acumen structures and manages funds investing in early stage social enterprises. In 2022 Acumen 
launched the Pioneer Energy Investment Initiative: Powering Livelihoods Using Solar (PEEI+), a USD 25m 
investment initiative investing early-stage capital in companies that provide renewable energy-powered appliances 
to microentrepreneurs and smallholder farmers in India and East/West Africa. In 2023 PEEI+ invested in Sokofresh.  
AgdevCo. An investment fund specialized in the financing of agribusiness companies in Africa and managing a 
USD 300m portfolio. Based in London with an office in Nairobi. AgDevCo has been looking for investment 
opportunities in Kenya in the cold chain sector but has not yet identified opportunities meetings its investment 
criteria. Minimum ticket size is c. USD 5m.  
AgriFi. Based in Brussels and 100% funded by the European Commission, this EUR 120m facility offers debt and 
equity to agrifood SMEs, microfinance institutions and impact funds with a link to smallholder farmers. Minimum 
ticket size is USD 0.5m. In 2022 AgriFi provided a USD 1.5m loan to East Africa Fruits, a Tanzanian agri-SME 
distributing fresh fruits and vegetables directly from farmers to urban customers (local vendors, supermarkets, 
restaurants and hotels). Use of proceeds included the acquisition of refrigerated trucks, the building of two 
temperature-controlled collection centres in rural areas as well as machinery and working capital needs. 
Arch Fund. Based in Nairobi, this investment fund was planning to close a USD 100m fundraising to build and 
operate c. 8 large cold chain operations in East Africa in partnership with local conglomerates. Its first cold 
warehouse was built in Nairobi (Tatu City Special Economic Zone) and will be inaugurated in Q2 2023. Arch Fund 
is also planning to acquire a fleet of temperature-controlled trucks and to deploy small cold storage units on farms 
in partnership with technology providers. Investors include DFIs (the European Investment Bank committed USD 
18m, AfDB USD 10m) and commercial investors (African Rainbow Capital and others). 
Co-Op Bank. The Co-operative Bank of Kenya is among the five largest commercial banks in the country and the 
primary bank for agricultural cooperative societies. Incorporated in 1965 as a cooperative, it is now a licensed and 
regulated commercial bank listed on the Nairobi Stock Exchange but co-operative societies and union have kept a 
majority stake. The bank’s assets amounted to KES 562bn24 as of December 2022. Co-Op has successfully financed 
the acquisition of cold chain equipment by dairy cooperatives (coolers, cooling truck and tanks). In 2021 it received 
a USD 10m subordinated loan from Kfw-backed eco.business Fund for on-lending to sustainable agribusinesses 
and farmers, including “cold storage solutions that reduce post-harvest losses25”. The credit line comes with 
“support mechanisms to assist farmers to make a successful pivot towards sustainable, climate-smart agriculture.” 
EFKEN. This financial leasing company provides lease finance with a 2-3 years maturity. EFKEN was launched 
in 2020 and shareholders are a mix of DFIs (Kfw), funds (AgDevco) and foundations (Small Foundation). Minimum 
ticket size is c. USD 20k. Its sister company operating in Tanzania (Equity For Tanzania of EFTA) was created in 
2005 and manages a USD 12m portfolio (tractors, trucks etc.). EFTA has already financed the acquisition of cold 
trucks by aggregators, cooperatives and food processing companies.  
Equity Bank. Founded in 1984 as a provider of mortgage financing, Equity Bank later became a microfinance 
institution and then one of the largest commercial banks in Kenya, with KES 894bn26 of assets as of December 
2022. It is also the largest bank in Africa in terms of customer base, with 16.9 million customers in Kenya, Uganda, 
South Sudan, Rwanda, Tanzania and DRC. Agriculture is considered as a priority area by its management, who has 
set the objective to grow the share of the agrifood sector in the total portfolio to 30%. Equity bank is supported by 
IFC, FMO and many other DFIs.  
Kawisafi Ventures. Based in Nairobi, this USD 67m equity fund invests in innovative companies deploying 
renewable energy solutions in Africa. Kawisafi is backed by Acumen and is a shareholder in InspiraFarms.

Annex 2: Description of technology providers 
EcoZen. Based in India, EcoZen designs and supplies solar powered cold storage rooms of different sizes. In Kenya 
EcoZen only ships cold storage units, which requires a local technology partner to assemble and service. EcoZen 
has also supplied cold storage units to clients in Rwanda.  
FreshBox. Based in Kenya, FreshBox designs, manufactures and distributes solar-powered cold storage solutions. 
Its flagship product is a large commercial cooling unit that can hold over two tons of fruits and vegetables and is 
targeting market vendors. The technology is also found in Rwanda.  
InspiraFarms. Based in Kenya, InspiraFarms designs, develops, installs, services, and finances cold chain 
technology for fresh fruit and vegetables, flowers and animal protein value chains. The company is the leading 
domestic supplier of cold chain equipment for the agrifood sector. Most of the imported components come from 
Italy. The majority of customers are exporters. InspiraFarms is also a lead supplier in Rwanda; amongst others, 
their technology was used for the SAIP cold stores.  
SokoFresh. Based in Kenya, SokoFresh imports kits of finished units (from EcoZen) and then assembles and 
services first-mile mobile cold storage units. The company has pioneered cooling-as-a-service for farmers. Contrary 
to other technology providers, SokoFresh also helps farmers to find buyers via its market linkage platform and even 
operates some cold storage units itself, turning de facto into an aggregator of fruits and vegetables.  
Solar Freeze. Based in Kenya, Solar Freeze offers first-mile cold chain solutions including mobile powered solar 
cold rooms; cold chain distribution, logistics and refrigerated transport; and cold storage as a service.

Annex 5: Compatibility Chart for Short-term Transport or Storage 
Thompson, J., A. Kader, and K. Sylva. 1996. Compatibility chart for fruits and vegetables in short-term 
transport or storage. Oakland: Univ. Calif. Div. Ag. and Nat. Res. Publ. 21560 (poster). 
The best storage environment for an individual fruit or vegetable depends on its unique requirements for 
temperature, relative humidity, and ethylene exposure. Most compatibility charts for mixing products 
during postharvest handling divide fruits and vegetables into eight groups. In practice it is very difficult to 
separate products into this many groups-very few wholesale or retail handling facilities, if any, have eight 
temperature-controlled rooms. 
 
 We have developed a Three-Group Chart that is easier to use and still provides good product life 
information (click image at left for larger view of the poster; actual size in 22 x 28inches). This publication 
includes poster and thermometer diagrams. For additonal information, see item description (pdf); for 
purchase, see our Order Form (pdf). 
Group 1: 32o to 36oF (0o to 2oC), 90% to 98% relative humidity contains the majority of produce in North 
American food-handling operations, including most leafy vegetables, Cole crops, and temperate-origin 
fruits and berries. 
Group 2: 45o to 50oF (7o to 10oC), 85% to 95% relative humidity contains citrus and subtropical fruits and 
many fruit-type vegetables. 
Group 3: 55o to 65oF (13o to 18oC) ), 85% to 95% relative humidity contains common root-type vegetables, 
winter (hard-rind) squashes, and most tropical fruits and melons. 
In grouping products that are potentially incompatible, we assumed (1) that storage is for 7 days or less and 
(2) that ethylene concentrations are kept below l ppm. The 7-day storage time allows us to recommend 
storage temperatures for some chilling-sensitive products that are colder than long-term storage. For 
example, we put citrus fruits in Group 2 (45 to 50oF). Although many citrus fruits can be damaged by long-
term storage in this range, 7 days' storage in this rarely causes chilling injury. This temperature has the 
added advantage of slowing the growth of decay-causing fungi, as compared to warmer-temperature 
storage. 
Ethylene must be kept below l ppm for all groups, particularly Group l, which contains most of the very 
sensitive green vegetables. Ethylene can be kept low in cold storage by (l) separating storage rooms from
=== PAGE 94 ===
 
 
94
ethylene sources such as ripening rooms; (2) ventilating with outside air; (3) eliminating the use of propane 
equipment in storage areas; and (4) using an ethylene scrubber. Ethylene levels in refrigerated highway 
trailers appear to be fairly low, probably because of high air-infiltration rates. A limited number of surveys 
indicate that ethylene is below l ppm in these vehicles. However, ethylene levels may be damaging when 
ethylene-sensitive products are shipped with ethylene producers in new vehicles, which tend to be tightly 
sealed. 
Group l requires a higher humidity because it contains many leafy vegetables, which lose water quickly and 
wilt easily. The two higher-temperature groups require a lower humidity because these products lose water 
more slowly than green vegetables, and lower humidity helps maintain fiberboard box strength. 
Following these recommendations will not necessarily eliminate all damage caused by less-than-optimum 
temperature, relative humidity, or ethylene exposure. Damage from these causes is the sum of all abuses 
after harvest. For example, moisture loss starts at harvest and can amount to several percent before the 
product leaves the cooler at the farm. The product may look fine until the last small loss of moisture at 
wholesale storage. The solution for the problem may not be simply increasing humidity al wholesale 
storage-moisture loss in transport, cooling, and before packaging should also be reduced. 
At retail stores, which rarely have three temperature-controlled rooms, Group 3 products can be held in an 
air-conditioned corridor or preparation room. At wholesale distribution facilities, which may have more 
than three storage rooms, two rooms can be designated Group 1, one for vegetables (Group 1A) and one 
for fruits [and melons] (Group 1B). This separates the ethylene-sensitive vegetables from the fruits, some 
of which produce ethylene. The Group l fruit room could also be kept at the lower humidity range (85% to 
95%) recommended for the Group 2 and 3 rooms. The Group 3 room can also be used for ripening products 
such as pears and stone fruits. 
We have developed a simple coding system that designates the three storage groups. Each box of produce 
should have the appropriate code printed on both box ends, allowing warehouse personnel to easily 
determine in which room to store the product. The poster is accompanied by four temperature codes, which 
are printed on separate sheets to be placed on cold room doors.
