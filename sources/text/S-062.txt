FOREWORD
S
omalia has made meaningful progress in strengthening economic management and advancing key 
structural reforms, including in the electricity sector, despite operating in an environment marked by 
declining external support, repeated climate shocks, and persistent security challenges. This edition of the 
Somalia Economic Update - Navigating Shocks, Powering Growth, assesses recent developments and highlights 
priority actions to sustain macroeconomic stability, reduce vulnerability to external and climate shocks, and 
unlock growth through more reliable, affordable, and sustainable energy.
On the macroeconomic front, Somalia’s attainment of the HIPC Completion Point in December 2023 has marked 
a turning point, restoring fiscal space, improving debt sustainability, and strengthening macroeconomic 
credibility. These gains have been underpinned by continued reforms in public financial management, 
domestic revenue mobilization, and financial sector oversight. Notably, macroeconomic stability has been 
maintained despite overlapping shocks, reflecting prudent economic management amid heightened climate 
and external risks.
Recent global oil price shocks and supply disruptions have further underscored Somalia’s vulnerability to 
external shocks. As a highly import-dependent economy with near universal reliance on diesel-based electricity 
generation, global fuel price increases transmit rapidly to domestic inflation, production costs, and household 
welfare. Higher fuel prices raise the cost of electricity, transport, and food, weakening firms’ competitiveness and 
disproportionately affecting poor and vulnerable households.
Against this backdrop, electricity sector reform has taken on heightened strategic importance. The report 
documents progress in strengthening the sector’s institutional and regulatory foundations, including the 
enactment of the Electricity Act, the establishment of the National Electricity Authority, and the rollout of 
licensing and tariff regulations. These steps are beginning to reduce fragmentation, improve oversight, and 
create a more predictable environment for investment. At the same time, growing deployment of solar and 
hybrid systems signals early movement toward reducing reliance on imported diesel for power generation.
Electricity sector reforms are therefore central not only to improving access, affordability, and reliability, but also 
to strengthening macroeconomic and climate resilience. By enabling a gradual shift toward least-cost renewable 
generation and more efficient systems, these reforms can reduce exposure to global fuel price shocks, lower 
economy wide production costs, and limit the transmission of external volatility to inflation and poverty. Over 
time, reduced dependence on fuel based generation will support energy security, ease balance of payments 
pressures, and reinforce fiscal and macroeconomic resilience.
The World Bank Group will continue to support Somalia in consolidating recent gains, advancing priority 
reforms, and translating improved macroeconomic and sectoral foundations into durable improvements 
in livelihoods.
Hideki Matsunaga 
World Bank Group,
Country Manager for Somalia

oomaaliya waxay horumar muhiim ah ka samaysay xoojinta maareynta dhaqaalaha iyo horumarinta qaab-
dhismeedka aasaasiga ah, oo ay ku jiraan qaybta korontada, in kastoo ay ka shaqeyso jawi lagu sheegay 
in hoos u dhaw ku yimid taageerada dibadda, isbeddelada cimilada ee soo noqnoqda, iyo caqabado amni 
oo joogto ah. Daabacaaddan lagaga hadlayo Dhaqaalaha Soomaaliya, Shoogga Socodka, Kobaca Awoodda leh, 
waxay qiimeyneysaa horumaradii ugu dambeeyay waxayna muujinaysaa tallaabooyinka mudnaanta leh ee lagu 
ilaalinayo xasilloonida dhaqaalaha guud, yareynta nuglaanshaha shoogaga dibadda iyo cimilada, iyo furitaanka 
kobaca iyadoo la adeegsanayo tamar la isku halayn karo, la awoodi karo, oo waarta.
Dhinaca dhaqaalaha guud, gaaritaanka Soomaaliya Dhibcaha Deyn Cafintii Diseembar 2023 waxay calaamad u 
ahayd isbeddel, taasoo soo celisay booska maaliyadda, hagaajisay joogtaynta deymaha, iyo xoojinta kalsoonida 
dhaqaalaha guud. Guulahan waxaa saldhig u ahaa dib-u-habeyn joogto ah oo lagu sameeyay maamulka 
maaliyadda dadweynaha, dhaqdhaqaaqa dakhliga gudaha, iyo kormeerka qaybaha maaliyadda. Si gaar ah, 
xasiloonida dhaqaalaha guud ayaa la ilaaliyay in kastoo ay jiraan shoogyo is daba joog ah, taasoo muujinaysa 
maareyn dhaqaale oo taxaddar leh iyadoo ay jirto khatarta cimilada iyo tan dibadda oo sii xoogaysatay.
Dhacdooyinkii ugu dambeeyay ee qiimaha saliidda adduunka iyo carqaladaha saadka ayaa sii xoojiyay u 
nuglaanta Soomaaliya isbeddelka shoogaga dibada. Maadaama ay tahay dhaqaale aad ugu tiirsan alaabaha la 
soo dhoofiya oo ku tiirsan koronto ku salaysan diesel, kororka qiimaha shidaalka caalamka si degdeg ah ayuu 
ugu gudbaa sicir-bararka gudaha, kharashka wax-soo-saarka, iyo daryeelka guryaha. Qiimaha shidaalka oo sare 
u kacay ayaa kor u qaada qiimaha korontada, gaadiidka, iyo cuntada, taasoo daciifisay tartanka shirkadaha isla 
markaana si aan sinnayn u saameeyay qoysaska saboolka ah iyo kuwa nugul.
Iyadoo laga duulayo xaaladdan, dib-u-habaynta qaybta korontada ayaa heshay muhiimad istiraatiiji oo sareysa. 
Warbixintu waxay diiwaangelisaa horumarka xoojinta aasaaska hay'adaha iyo xeerarka ee qaybta, oo ay ku jiraan 
ansixinta Xeerka Korontada, aasaaska Hay'adda Korontada Qaranka, iyo hirgelinta xeerarka ruqsad-siinta iyo 
qiimaha. Tallaabooyinkan waxay bilaabayaan inay yareeyaan kala qaybsanaanta, horumarinta kormeerka, iyo 
abuurista deegaan la saadaalin karo oo maalgashi ah. Isla waqtigaas, korodhka isticmaalka nidaamyada soolarka 
iyo mida shidaalka iyo korontada isku dhafan ayaa muujinaya dhaqdhaqaaq hore oo lagu wajahayo dhimista ku 
tiirsanaanta diesel-ka la soo dhoofiyo si loo isticmaalo soo saarista tamarta.
Dib-u-habaynta qaybta korontada waa muhiim ma aha oo kaliya hagaajinta helitaanka, qiimaynta, iyo kalsoonida, 
balse sidoo kale xoojinta dhaqaalaha iyo adkeysiga cimilada. Iyada oo la suurtagelinayo in si tartiib tartiib ah 
loogu beddelo jihada la cusboonaysiin karo ee qiimaha jaban iyo nidaamyo waxtar leh, isbeddeladan waxay 
yareyn karaan saameynta qiimaha shidaalka caalamka, hoos u dhigi karaan kharashka wax-soo-saarka guud ee 
dhaqaalaha, isla markaana waxay xaddidi karaan gudbinta isbeddelka dibadda ee sicir-bararka iyo saboolnimada. 
Waqti ka dib, dhimista ku tiirsanaanta wax soo saarista shidaalka waxay taageeri doontaa amniga tamarta, yareyn 
doontaa cadaadiska dheelitirka lacagaha, waxayna xoojin doontaa adkeysiga maaliyadeed iyo dhaqaalaha guud 
ee sanadlaha ah.
Bangiga Adduunka wuxuu sii wadi doonaa taageerada Soomaaliya si loo xoojiyo guulihii ugu dambeeyay, 
horumarinta dib-u-habaynta mudnaanta leh, iyo beddelidda aasaaska dhaqaalaha guud iyo kuwa qaybaha la 
hagaajiyay si loogu sameeyo horumar waara  nolosha.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
S
omalia’s 
economy 
demonstrated 
notable 
resilience entering 2025, supported by continuous 
reform efforts to strengthen fiscal sustainability, 
institutions, and private sector resilience. Low debt 
levels following HIPC Completion, tax policy changes, 
and reforms to improve payments system, expand 
financial sector supervision and anti-money laundering, 
contributed to better macroeconomic management. 
Structural reforms in fisheries, telecommunications, 
and electricity, as well as ongoing harmonization of 
legal and institutional framework with East African 
Community (EAC) will improve private sector resilience 
despite a challenging external environment. These 
positive foundations underscore Somalia’s progress 
in strengthening macroeconomic credibility, even as 
vulnerabilities remain.
However, Somalia’s economic resilience was tested 
as a combination of shocks materialized over the 
course of 2025. The withdrawal of humanitarian 
and security-related assistance reduced aggregate 
demand, while adverse weather conditions disrupted 
agricultural 
and 
livestock 
production. 
Growth 
remained largely consumption-driven but slowed to 
3 percent in 2025 from 4 percent in 2023 and 2024, 
resulting in broadly stagnant real GDP per capita. 
Investment activity proved relatively more resilient—
supported by remittances and urban expansion—but 
export performance softened, reflecting Somalia’s 
narrow and climate-sensitive export base. Structural 
constraints, including high electricity and transport 
costs, limited access to finance, and internal 
fragmentation, continued to impede industrial 
development 
and 
private 
sector 
expansion. 
Inflationary pressures accelerated reaching 3.7 
percent in 2025 compared to 3.3 percent in 2024, 
driven mainly by food, utilities, and transport costs, 
although widespread dollarization helped contain 
broader price instability. The reduction in aid also had 
significant social consequences, with food insecurity 
increasing and poverty reduction stalling.
Lower official grants and weaker exports widened 
external imbalances, and reinforced Somalia’s 
structural current account deficit. The current account 
deficit widened to 9.6 percent of GDP in 2025 from 9.3 
percent of GDP in 2024. Remittances continued to 
play a critical stabilizing role, providing an important 
buffer for household incomes and foreign exchange 
inflows, and partially offsetting the decline in aid. 
Despite this resilience, the external position remains 
highly vulnerable to shocks, reflecting Somalia’s narrow 
export base and heavy reliance on imports of food, fuel, 
and consumer goods.
On the fiscal front, reduced grants, and softer 
domestic demand constrained revenue performance, 
while spending pressures, particularly for security and 
intergovernmental transfers intensified. As a result, the 
fiscal balance shifted to a deficit of 0.4 percent of GDP in 
2025, from a surplus of 0.1 percent in 2024. Nonetheless, 
fiscal 
management 
remained 
broadly 
prudent, 
supported by continued public financial management 
reforms and the fiscal space created by debt relief. 
Maintaining fiscal discipline amid declining external 
support will be critical to safeguarding macroeconomic 
stability and protecting priority spending. 
Outlook and risks. The near- to medium-term outlook 
has weakened relative to earlier expectations with 
recent global oil price surge adding another shock 
to Somalia’s economy. Economic growth is projected 
to remain modest at 2.8 percent in 2026 and pick up 
to 3.1 percent in 2027, constrained by continued aid 
reductions, climate variability, global price shocks due 
to the conflict in the Middle East, and limited productive 
capacity. Inflation is projected to rise to 6.0 percent 
in 2026 from 3.7 percent in 2025, reflecting global 
commodity price volatility, especially Somalia’s reliance 
on imported food and fuel, and domestic supply 
disruptions linked to climate and security conditions. 
Poverty reduction is expected to be gradual, with food 
insecurity remaining extremely sensitive to rainfall 
outcomes, aid flows, and price shocks.

Global price shocks associated with the conflict in 
the Middle East are heightening Somalia’s external 
vulnerability 
through 
multiple 
transmission 
channels. As a highly import-dependent economy, 
Somalia is exposed first through the trade and price 
pass through channel. Higher global energy and food 
prices are rapidly translating into higher domestic fuel, 
transport, electricity, and food costs, fueling inflation 
while weighing on port activity and customs revenues. 
The remittance channel reflects Somalia’s reliance on 
employment opportunities in GCC countries, where 
weaker economic conditions could slow remittance 
inflows and reduce household consumption. At the 
same time, heightened uncertainty is likely to dampen 
foreign and diaspora investment weakening capital 
formation and medium term growth prospects, while 
the combined effects increase fiscal pressures by 
compressing revenues and raising spending needs.
Risks to the outlook are predominantly on the 
downside. A deeper or more prolonged decline in 
foreign aid could further compress consumption, 
strain fiscal execution, and heighten humanitarian 
needs. Climate shocks, including droughts and floods, 
continue to threaten rural livelihoods and food prices, 
while security setbacks could disrupt trade corridors 
and deter investment. Prolonged global oil disruptions, 
geopolitical tensions, or changes affecting remittance 
channels—could further weaken external balances. 
Political and intergovernmental coordination challenges 
also pose risks to timely reform implementation.
Navigating 
shocks 
will 
require 
accelerating 
reforms that support growth and job creation and 
stepping up efforts to mobilize domestic revenues. 
Addressing Somalia’s deep-seated development 
challenges requires continued improvements in 
economic 
regulation 
and 
institutions, 
reducing 
regional 
fragmentation, 
and 
more 
affordable 
electricity. 
Consistent 
reform 
implementation, 
stronger coordination across levels of government, 
and increasingly constrained external support will 
be essential to sustain growth and create jobs. These 
reforms will also ensure the ability of the economy 
to better withstand mounting climate and external 
shocks. At the same time, enhancing domestic 
revenue mobilization is critical to expand fiscal 
space, safeguard fiscal sustainability, and enable the 
government to deliver more and better basic services. 
In this context, it is particularly important to reduce 
reliance on volatile external grants while foreign aid 
is declining. Improved domestic revenue mobilization 
requires broadening the tax base, modernizing tax and 
customs administration, and improving harmonization 
across federal and state levels (World Bank, 2025a).
Sustaining public financial management and debt 
management reforms remains essential to preserving 
macroeconomic stability and ensuring efficient use of 
limited resources.
Recent global oil price shocks and the deterioration 
in the outlook reinforce the urgency of reducing 
costs for households and firms and transitioning to 
more sustainable electricity generation. Lowering 
electricity costs would also deliver macroeconomic 
benefits, including reducing exposure to global 
commodity 
price 
volatility, 
easing 
inflationary 
pressures, and strengthening household purchasing 
power and firm competitiveness. Somalia’s electricity 
sector remains a binding constraint to growth 
and private sector development, characterized 
by fragmentation, high costs, vulnerability to 
commodity price volatility, and limited regulation. 
Electricity supply is dominated by small, privately 
operated systems that rely heavily on imported diesel 
generation, resulting in some of the highest tariffs 
globally and significant variability in service quality 
across locations. Weak sector governance, limited 
economies of scale, and insufficient transmission and 
distribution infrastructure constrain efficiency gains 
and hinder the integration of lower-cost generation 
sources. These structural weaknesses raise production 
costs economy-wide, disproportionately affect small 
firms and households, and limit the scope for industrial 
activity and job creation.

Executive Summary
Despite progress in expanding coverage in major 
urban centers, access to electricity remains uneven 
and reliability challenges persist. The 2025 Multi-
Tier Framework (MTF) survey indicates 71 percent of 
households report some form of electricity access, yet 
only 21 percent of electrified households receive more 
than eight hours of supply per day. Urban areas have 
seen gradual improvements in connections driven 
by private investment and population growth, while 
access in rural and peri-urban areas remains limited. 
Firms consistently identify electricity cost and reliability 
as major operational constraints, with frequent 
outages and voltage fluctuations requiring costly self-
generation and backup solutions. For households, 
high tariffs constrain consumption and welfare, while 
limited access exacerbates spatial inequalities and 
undermines service delivery, particularly in health, 
education, and water services.
Addressing these challenges requires a coordinated 
reform agenda focused on lowering costs, improving 
reliability, reducing vulnerability, and expanding 
access. Priority actions include (1) strengthening the 
regulatory and institutional framework to reduce 
fragmentation and improve oversight; (2) promoting 
least-cost generation, particularly through scalable 
renewable energy investments; and (3) investing in 
transmission and distribution networks to enhance 
system 
efficiency 
and 
enable 
interconnection. 
Improving coordination across federal and state levels, 
alongside targeted public and private investment, will 
be essential to unlock economies of scale, crowd in 
financing, and ensure that electricity reforms support 
broader objectives of competitiveness, job creation, 
and inclusive growth.

Global and Regional Environment
T
he global economy remains resilient in 2025 
despite trade tensions and policy uncertainty, 
but downside risks and uncertainty have intensified 
in 2026. Global growth is estimated at 2.7 percent 
in 2025, 0.4 percentage points above the June 2025 
projections but slightly below the 2.8 percent in 2024, 
driven by stronger than-expected growth in major 
economies (World Bank, 2026b). This resilience partly 
reflects front-loaded trade ahead of anticipated tariff 
increases; as this temporary support fades, global 
trade is expected to soften. Growth and inflation 
prospects remain subdued subject to elevated risks 
amid ongoing trade policy shifts, fuel price hikes and 
global tensions.
Sub-Saharan Africa’s recovery from successive 
global shocks is losing momentum in 2026. Rising 
geopolitical risks related to the conflict in the 
Middle East, together with mounting debt service 
burdens and long-standing structural weaknesses, 
are weighing on economic activity. Regional gross 
domestic product (GDP) growth is projected at 4.1 
percent in 2026, unchanged from 2025, but downside 
risks have intensified. Economic growth in 2025 
benefited from improved inflation management, 
ongoing fiscal consolidation, and reforms aimed 
at enhancing domestic revenue mobilization and 
strengthening debt management frameworks. 
Amid ongoing macroeconomic stabilization efforts, 
domestic demand continues to underpin economic 
growth across the region. Private consumption 
and domestic investment have been buoyed by 
accommodative monetary policy and improving 
external conditions, including the depreciation of 
the U.S. dollar, which has helped ease inflationary 
pressures. At the same time, elevated commodity 
prices—especially for precious metals and beverage 
commodities—strengthened revenues in several 
African economies in 2025 and are expected to 
continue to support the fiscal and external balances 
of resource abundant countries over the current year.
Downside risks to the growth outlook have risen 
markedly since the end of February 2026. The 
Middle East conflict has escalated sharply, with 
direct attacks on energy infrastructure and severe 
disruptions to shipping through the Strait of 
Hormuz. The magnitude of the spillovers to Sub-
Saharan Africa will depend on the duration of 
the conflict, the scope of regional transmission 
channels, and the extent of damage to critical 
infrastructure. These risks are further amplified by 
heightened debt vulnerabilities and a contraction 
in external financing—most notably a reduction in 
development assistance—placing additional strain 
on the fiscal positions of low income countries.
Recent Economic Developments in Somalia
Despite positive momentum in recent years, economic 
growth has decelerated 
Somalia’s economic growth moderated in 2025 
on the back of a sharp decline in foreign aid and 
drought conditions. Real GDP growth is expected to 
have reached 3 percent in 2025, a notable deceleration 
from the 4.1 percent growth recorded in 2024 
(Figure 1), resulting in broadly stagnant real GDP per 
capita. Somalia, one of the key recipients of foreign 
aid in the region, relied on external financing for 
security and humanitarian support. Both security and 
humanitarian aid were significantly curtailed in 2025, 
weighing heavily on consumption of households 
and non-profit institutions serving households. In 
addition, below-average rainfall during the October–
December 2025 Deyr season—at less than 30 percent 
of the seasonal norm—reduced crop production and 
agricultural output, adversely affecting rural incomes, 
food security, and export performance. This also 
impacted livestock, as below-average Gu rains and a 
largely failed Deyr season led to severe pasture and 
water shortages, worsening animal health conditions 
and reducing milk production.

Private consumption remains the primary driver 
of Somalia’s aggregate demand (Figure 2). Private 
consumption growth markedly slowed down to 3.1

slowdown linked to reduced foreign aid and the

remained strong, supported by remittances, expanding 
credit, and public investment. With significant 
slowdown in imports, the negative contribution of net 
exports to growth was scaled down. 
Weak competition, barriers to trade and investment, 
regional fragmentation, and infrastructure gaps are 
among the key barriers to firms’ growth (Bareisaite 
et al., 2025). High market concentration and weak 
competition allow dominant firms to distort prices, 
restrict entry, and crowd out smaller and new firms, 
reducing incentives to invest, innovate, and improve 
productivity. Barriers to trade and investment further 
confine most businesses to local markets, fragmenting 
economic activity and weakening incentives to 
upgrade skills, adopt modern technologies, or scale 
operations, which in turn constrains job creation and 
wage growth. Regional fragmentation exacerbates 
these challenges, with economic activity heavily 
concentrated in Mogadishu while firms in other 
regions struggle to formalize, attract investment, and 
grow, widening spatial disparities and limiting inclusive 
employment opportunities. Finally, infrastructure 
gaps and weak connectivity, particularly poor road 
conditions, seasonal flooding, and limited transport 
networks, raise costs, disrupt supply chains, and 
restrict firms’ ability to access broader markets, 
especially outside the capital. Together, these 
constraints reinforce low competition, narrow 
market reach, and uneven growth, holding back 
the development of a more dynamic, productive, 
and geographically inclusive private sector in 
Somalia.

Poverty reduction efforts were interrupted by 
declining humanitarian support and climate shocks 
Poverty reduction stalled in 2025, halting the 
progress made since 2022. After falling for 2 years in 
a row, poverty2 is estimated to have declined by 0.3 
percentage points in 2025, compared to the larger gains 
since 2022 (Figure 4a). The stalling was largely due to 
cuts in foreign aid, with nearly two-thirds of recipient 
households reporting that their assistance stopped, 
according to a phone survey conducted in July and 
August 2025 (Box 1). The number of beneficiaries of 
cash and in-kind transfers was drastically reduced as 
drought conditions worsened, and food prices rose. 
As a result, the share of households classified according 
to Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) 
in Crisis (Phase 3) and Emergency (Phase 4) increased in 
the second half of 2025 (Figure 4b).

External pressures heightened amid aid cuts 
The current account deficit is estimated to have 
widened to 9.6 percent of GDP in 2025 from 9.3 
percent in 2024, as external official grants declined 
(Figure 5). With major donors cutting humanitarian 
and security aid, external official grants fell by 4.2 
percentage points of GDP. Higher remittances and 
slight improvement in net services exports have 
somewhat mitigated the impact of falling aid and 
worsening merchandise trade deficit. Despite global 
uncertainties and softer aid conditions, remittances 
remained resilient, supporting private consumption 
and demand for imported goods. Remittances reached 
24 percent of GDP in 2025, up by 1.3 percentage 
points compared to 2024. At the same time, limited 
diversification of exports, both in terms of products 
and markets, and significant dependence on imported 
goods, have kept a high structural trade deficit. 
This highlights the predominance of structural 
constraints in the production and trade base, as well 
as the economy’s ongoing dependence on external 
financing to sustain its external position.
Total exports declined by 1.7 percentage points 
of GDP year-on-year in January-September 2025, 
albeit from a high base. Poor rainfall and limited 
water storage and irrigation facilities worsened 
pastures’ quality and downsized crops. As a result, 
livestock exports, accounting for roughly three-
quarters of total export receipts, contracted by 
0.8 percentage points and exports of crops and 
vegetable oil by 0.3 percentage points, year-on-
year, hence contributing most to overall export 
decline. Exports of fisheries have been volatile over 
the past two years (Figure 6a). Overall, the export 
composition continued to exhibit high concentration 
in few primary commodities and few markets, limiting 
resilience to sector-specific shocks.
Imports fell by 1.1 percentage points of GDP year-
on-year in January-September 2025 amid falling 
foreign aid (Figure 6b). Dwindling aid affected 
imports with direct imports from aid organizations 
falling and demand for food and medical products 
slowing, as private consumption growth moderated. 
Food imports fell by 2.5 percentage points year-on-
year, and imports of medical products contracted by 
0.8 percentage points. Imports of all other products 
expanded as Somalia’s economy continues to depend

Overall, 
monetary 
and 
financial 
sector 
developments in Somalia in recent years have 
been driven by institutional strengthening and the 
gradual modernization of the financial system. This 
is critical for the economy’s sustained growth as total 
credit to the private sector remains around 5 percent 
of GDP; one of the lowest in the world (Figure 8). The 
Central Bank of Somalia has continued to enhance 
its regulatory and supervisory capacity and improve 
financial sector oversight.7 Recent reforms have 
focused on strengthening governance, compliance, 
and operational frameworks in the banking system. 
The new Financial Institutions Law revised Anti-
Money Laundering/Countering the Financing of 
Terrorism (AML/CFT) Law, Takaful/Insurance Law, 
and National Payment System Law were all enacted 
in 2025 and early 2026. Regulatory authorities also 
made strong progress in compiling and disseminating 
financial sector data, enabling Somalia’s inclusion 
in key International Monetary Fund (IMF) statistical 
databases for the first time.

Somalia’s fiscal position remained broadly balanced 
but regressed compared to 2024. The Federal 
Government of Somalia (FGS) ran a deficit of 0.4 
percent of GDP in 2025 while in 2024 the budget was in 
surplus of 0.1 percent of GDP (Figure 9). Lower bilateral

Somalia’s attainment of the HIPC Completion Point 
has fundamentally improved its debt position.  Since 
December 2023, Somalia is assessed to be in moderate 
risks of debt distress for its external and overall public 
debt. Nearly all legacy external debt was eliminated, 
the largest of which was debt to Paris Club countries and 
negotiations with remaining creditors, mostly bilateral, 
continue to ensure comparable terms of debt relief 
with Paris Club agreement. At the end of 2025, public 
debt stood at US$1,155 million, marginally increasing 
from US$1,117 million in 2024, with the public debt-
to-GDP ratio stabilizing at 8.9 percent in 2025. Somalia 
is pursuing prudent debt management policy, with 
most of the budget financing provided by grants or 
highly concessional loans. FGS continues to strengthen 
its debt management capacity and adopted its first 
Medium-Term Debt Management Strategy and Annual 
Borrowing Plan in 2025. Nevertheless, Somalia has weak 
debt carrying capacity due to low domestic revenue 
mobilization, nascent institutions, and high exposure 
to external shocks. Somalia remains highly dependent 
on foreign aid and is vulnerable to security, climate, 
commodity price, and global growth shocks. These 
risks highlight the critical importance of strengthening 
domestic revenue mobilization, adopting a credible 
medium-term fiscal anchor, and enhancing debt 
management and institutional capacity to preserve 
fiscal and debt sustainability. 
Medium-Term Outlook, Risks and 
Reform Priorities
Somalia’s medium-term outlook has worsened with 
foreign aid cuts, erratic rains, and global shocks. 
Real GDP growth is projected to slow to 2.8 percent 
in 2026 and pick up to 3.1 percent in 2027 (Figure 
13a). The projection for 2026 was downgraded by 
0.7 percentage points compared to previous World 
Bank projections (World Bank, 2026a). The slowdown 
in economic growth reflects constrained private 
consumption due to further decline in foreign aid, 
worsening food insecurity, rising food and oil prices 
due to the conflict in the Middle East, and slowdown 
in remittances. Remittance inflows are projected to 
moderate due to regulatory changes in some host 
countries as well as headwinds from the conflict 
in the Middle East. Ongoing structural reforms to 
strengthen public institutions, improve the business 
environment, and deepen integration in the EAC are 
anticipated to sustain private and public investment 
and support accelerating GDP growth to 3.5 percent 
in 2028. Baseline medium-term growth is expected 
to be below the average for the past five years, 
as the growth potential of the economy remains 
constrained by fragility, limited productive capacity, 
and a narrow export base. Hence, even under the 
baseline, per-capita gains and job creation are likely 
to remain modest.

Somalia is highly exposed to spillovers from the Middle East conflict because of its structural dependence on 
imports, remittances, and external assistance. The conflict is expected to affect Somalia’s economy through three 
main channels: trade, labor, investment, and financial markets.
The trade channel is likely to be the most immediate and significant transmission mechanism, operating primarily 
through heightened volatility in global energy and commodity markets. Brent crude prices have risen sharply, 
contributing to an increase in domestic fuel prices—from about US$0.60 to US$1.50 per liter in early March 2026. 
Given Somalia’s heavy reliance on imported fuel and food, higher global prices are likely to raise domestic food costs, 
while rising transport and energy costs including diesel-based electricity generation could generate higher input 
costs and broad-based inflationary pressures, further burdening businesses and households. Disruptions to shipping 
routes have also reduced activity at Somalia’s ports, weighing on customs revenues given the continued reliance on 
package-based valuation.
The second major channel is labor and remittances, which are a critical source of income for Somali households. 
Somalia depends heavily on remittance inflows, including from Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries. A prolonged 
conflict could weaken these flows through softer labor demand, slower hiring, and increased repatriations in host-
country sectors such as construction, retail, and hospitality. Weaker economic conditions in host countries would reduce 
household incomes in Somalia, with adverse effects on consumption and welfare.
A third channel is investment. Recent investment momentum from Gulf countries to Somalia may slow amid heightened 
uncertainty. Gulf investors, particularly from the United Arab Emirates, have played an important role in logistics and 
port infrastructure. Delays or cancellations of planned investments would weaken capital formation and medium-term 
growth prospects. Diaspora investment could also soften, as conflict-related disruptions weigh on economic activity, 
incomes, and investor confidence in affected host countries.
The macroeconomic and social impacts could be significant. Real GDP growth is projected to slow to 2.8 percent in 
2026, down from the 3.5 percent projected in October 2025. Inflation is expected to rise to 5.3 percent, compared with 
earlier projection of below 4 percent. Poverty could increase by 2.0 to 4.4 percentage points, pushing an additional 
290,000 to 680,000 people into poverty. The shock would also heighten fiscal risks, as weaker imports reduce customs 
revenues (60 percent of domestic revenues) while higher prices increase spending pressures for goods and services and 
social assistance.

Somalia’s 2026 budget reflects an optimistic view about revenue collection amid a weakening external environment and 
heightened uncertainty. Total revenue is projected to increase sharply compared to 2025 actuals, driven largely by higher 
projected grants; however, there is a high risk that these grants may not fully materialize, requiring consolidating spending 
or widening fiscal deficit beyond the 0.5 percent of GDP planned in the budget. Domestic revenue is expected to grow at a 
faster pace in 2026 than in 2025, but there is limited clarity on the specific policy or administrative measures underpinning this 
increase ahead of the elections, raising risks to revenue outturns. On the expenditure side, spending pressures remain elevated 
due to security needs and election related costs, while goods and services are projected to become the largest spending 
category. The wage bill is expected to increase only marginally, reflecting continued efforts to contain recurrent spending. 
Capital spending is projected to rise sharply—nearly tripling relative to 2025 actuals, supported by externally financed projects. 
Despite elevated food insecurity and cut in humanitarian aid, social benefits remain well below 2023—24 levels, limiting fiscal 
space to respond to potential humanitarian shocks.

policy priorities and investments needed to unlock 
the sector’s potential to support business, create jobs, 
and drive economic growth. 
Status of Electricity Sector
Somalia’s electricity sector has made gradual 
progress despite persistent structural constraints. 
Institutional reforms have advanced, with an enabling 
legislative framework in place, and a national 
regulator established. Electrification—driven entirely 
by the private sector—has expanded, particularly in 
urban areas alongside rising urbanization. Somalia’s 
renewable energy potential is substantial, and 
its development could reduce electricity costs, 
improve environmental outcomes, and enhance firm 
performance while easing pressure on household 
budgets. However, the sector continues to face 
significant 
challenges, 
including 
fragmentation, 
high electricity costs, and heavy reliance on diesel 
generation.
The enabling environment for the power sector 
has 
improved 
but 
remains 
constrained 
by 
implementation gaps. The Electricity Act, enacted 
in 2023, established the National Electricity Authority 
(NEA), which has adopted tariff setting regulations 
and issued licenses to 20 electricity service providers 
(ESPs). The National Transformation Plan (NTP) 2025-29, 
moreover, envisages putting in place a robust policy

Significant investment will be required to meet 
Somalia’s current and future electricity demand. 
Installed generation capacity is estimated at only 
300–400 MW, while government projections suggest 
demand could rise to 1,000–4,600 MW by around 
2037. Meeting this growth will require a severalfold 
increase in generation capacity, alongside substantial 
investment in transmission and distribution to 
connect currently isolated systems. The Ministry of 
Energy and Water Resources (MoEWR) estimates that 
approximately US$3 billion will be needed over the 
next two decades across generation, transmission, 
and distribution. Earlier planning supported by the 
African Development Bank for 2016–2025 indicated 
that about US$580 million could add roughly 200 
MW of new capacity. This includes 30–40 MW of 
solar PV, together with urban grid upgrades, mini-
grid development, and distribution improvements. 
Overall, least-cost analyses indicate that Somalia’s 
energy transition will depend on scaling renewable 
generation and strengthening networks, with grid 
extension, hybrid mini-grids, and off-grid solar offering 
the most affordable pathways to expanded access.
Given severe fiscal constraints, large-scale public 
investment remains limited, underscoring the 
importance 
of 
private 
sector-led 
solutions, 
particularly in distributing renewable energy. 
Beyond mini-grids operated by ESPs, private delivery 
models for standalone solar systems can expand 
access in remote and sparsely populated areas. 
Somalia currently sells an estimated 100,000–
150,000 off-grid solar products annually. Geospatial 
electrification analysis shows that solar home 
systems and mini-grids represent the least-cost 
options for electrifying dispersed and pastoralist 
communities. Scaling these solutions can help address 
affordability constraints while strengthening financing 
mechanisms, quality assurance, and distribution 
networks.

Nearly all power is produced using high-speed diesel 
generators, with renewable energy penetration well 
below regional peers. Electricity generation remains 
inefficient and costly. Many generators operate below 
technical standards due to limited investment in 
synchronization and efficiency improvements. This 
heavy reliance on diesel raises generation costs, 
contributes to environmental degradation, and 
increases carbon intensity, challenges that are further 
compounded by widespread reliance on biomass.
At the same time, Somalia has substantial renewable 
energy potential that could support a more 
affordable and sustainable power sector (Figures 
15a and 15b). Solar radiation averages around 6 
kWh/m²/day, among the highest globally, while 
coastal areas record average wind speeds exceeding 
8 m/s, indicating strong onshore wind potential. 
Hydropower potential along the Shabelle and Juba 
rivers is estimated at 100–150 MW. Integrating solar 
photovoltaic generation with battery energy storage 
offers the fastest and most cost-effective pathway 
to displace diesel, particularly in urban systems, as 
reflected in the 2025 Generation and Transmission 
Least-Cost Development Plan. The plan emphasizes 
diversification toward solar, wind, and hydropower, 
supported by clear implementation strategies and a 
mix of public, private, and public-private financing.
Fragmented electricity distribution, high reliance 
on expensive diesel for production, and limited 
investment have left Somalia with some of the 
highest electricity tariffs in the world. Somalis pay 
on average US$0.60/kWh, far above the Horn of Africa 
average of US$0.22/kWh (Table 4). Key reasons include 
costly diesel generation, poor equipment utilization, 
fragmented infrastructure, inefficient investments, 
significant 
losses, 
and 
inadequate 
regulation 
and planning. These disparities also highlight an 
opportunity for Somalia to connect regionally and 
access more affordable, sustainable power.

Short Term

the

through

as the main grid expands. National technical 
standards for interconnection, safety, metering, and

system compatibility and protect consumers.

consumer

and 
affordability

and 
visible

affordability through targeted subsidies (such as 
connection support and lifeline tariffs). These also 
include efficiency-driven cost reductions, as well 
as enforcing transparent disclosure, warranties, 
and after-sales service standards for standalone

and small enterprises.

planning,

The first-stage sample involved selecting EAs from which households were drawn, with an average of 20 
households per EA. The selection of EAs for the survey was conducted by Somali National Bureau of Statistics 
(SNBS) using the Somalia Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) 2020 sampling frame, updated in 2022 for 
the Integrated Household Budget Survey. The national frame comprised 9,136 EAs, including 7,308 urban and 
1,828 rural EAs. Due to security and access constraints, particularly in southern regions, the region of Middle 
Jubba was entirely excluded and a few urban and many rural EAs were deactivated and excluded from the 
sampling pool. 
A household listing activity followed, during which all households within the selected EAs were visited to 
determine their electrification status based on a connection to the grid/mini-grid. The final selection of 
households was implemented as follows:
-	 If all households in EA were either electrified or non-electrified, 20 households were randomly selected.
-	 For EAs containing both grid-connected and non-grid-connected households, where each category had 
more than 10 households, 10 households were randomly selected from each category.
-	 If an EA had fewer than 10 grid-connected households, all available grid-connected households were 
selected, and the remaining households were randomly chosen from non-grid-connected households to 
reach the target of 20 households. The same procedure would be applied if there were fewer than 10 non-
electrified households.
Following this methodology, the survey interviewed a nationally representative sample of 3,098 households.
