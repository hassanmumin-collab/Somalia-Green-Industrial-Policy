Executive Summary
The global economy demonstrated continued resilience in the first quarter of 2026, although 
momentum was tempered by the outbreak of conflict in the Middle East, which introduced new 
headwinds through higher commodity prices, firmer inflation expectations, and tighter financial 
conditions. Global growth is estimated at 3.4 percent in 2025 and is projected to slow to 3.1 
percent in 2026, before recovering modestly to 3.2 percent in 2027. Global headline inflation 
is projected to rise from 4.1 percent in 2025 to 4.4 percent in 2026, driven primarily by higher 
energy and food prices associated with disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz.
Somalia’s economy entered 2026 on a weaker footing than in previous years. Real GDP growth 
moderated from 4.0 percent in 2024 to an estimated 3.1 percent in 2025 and is projected to slow 
further to 2.6 percent in 2026. The first quarter of 2026 was characterised by an intensifying 
drought, a sharp external energy price shock, and continued retrenchment of foreign aid. 
Headline inflation is projected to increase to 5.9 percent in 2026, from 3.7 percent in 2025, 
as fuel prices more than doubled following the Middle East conflict, generating broad-based 
cost-push pressures across transport, food, and utilities. The Somali shilling depreciated by 
1.2 percent during the quarter, with the average exchange rate reaching SOS 31,956 per US 
dollar. Private consumption, underpinned by remittance inflows, remained the principal driver 
of domestic demand.
Somalia’s merchandise trade deficit widened further in 2026Q1. Total merchandise imports 
increased by 16 percent year-on-year to US$2,478.74 million, driven by higher demand for 
construction materials, clothing and footwear, medical products, and oil and gas. Total exports 
increased by 20 percent to US$345 million, supported by stronger livestock, animal skins, 
and fishery exports. Livestock remained the dominant export commodity, accounting for 75 
percent of total merchandise exports at US$258 million. Notwithstanding improved export 
performance, the trade deficit widened to US$2,134 million from US$1,844 million in 2025Q1, 
reflecting continued structural dependence on imported goods and a narrow export base. 
Fiscal performance in the first quarter of 2026 reflected continued progress in domestic revenue 
mobilisation alongside weaker external grant inflows. Total government revenue and grants 
reached US$154 million, representing 12 percent execution of the annual budget, while total 
expenditure amounted to US$171 million, resulting in a financing gap of US$17 million. Domestic 
revenue increased by 7 percent year-on-year to US$101 million, driven by improvements in tax 
administration and compliance. External grants declined by 18 percent to US$53 million, as 
bilateral disbursements fell to zero and multilateral inflows moderated. Capital expenditure 
remained low at US$7 million, equivalent to 3 percent of the approved annual capital budget, 
reflecting persistent implementation constraints.
Somalia’s financial sector continued to expand and strengthen in the first quarter of 2026, 
supported by regulatory reforms led by the CBS. Total banking sector assets increased to 
US$2,352 million, representing quarterly growth of 2 percent and annual growth of 16 percent. 
The sector maintained sound prudential indicators, with capital adequacy and liquidity ratios 
comfortably exceeding regulatory minimums. Inward remittance transfers amounted to US$1,588 
million during the quarter. The national payment system processed 337,788 transactions 
with a combined value of US$593 million, reflecting broader adoption of electronic payment 
infrastructure and continued deepening of financial intermediation.

yaraado oo uu gaaro 2.6 boqolkiiba dhamaadka sannadka 2026. Hoos u dhaca korarka

yaraanshaha taageerada dhaqaale ee dibadda uga timaadda Soomaaliya.

sarifka lacagtu gaaray 31,956 shilin Soomaali halkii doollar ee Maraykan ah.

A
ccording to the IMF’s April 2026 World 
Economic Outlook, global economy faces 
a major new challenge following the 
outbreak of war in the Middle East. Although 
recent economic growth has been supported by 
technology investment, easier financial conditions, 
and policy support, the conflict threatens these 
gains by increasing energy prices, inflation, and 
financial uncertainty. The IMF’s reference forecast 
assumes the conflict will be limited and disruptions 
will ease by mid-2026. Under this assumption, 
global economic growth is projected to slow 
downward to 3.1 percent in 2026 (a downward 
revision of 0.2 percentage points from the January 
2026 forecast) and recover slightly to 3.2 percent 
in 2027, remaining below the historical average of 
3.7 percent. Global inflation is expected to increase 
upward to 4.4 percent in 2026 before declining to 
3.7 percent in 2027. Without the conflict, global 
growth would have been revised upward to 3.4 
percent in 2026, showing that the war is the main 
reason for the lower growth forecast.
The report emphasizes that the effects of the war 
will not be evenly distributed. Emerging markets 
and developing economies, especially those 
that rely on imported energy are already facing 
economic weaknesses, and expected to suffer 
the most. If the conflict lasts longer or damages 
energy infrastructure, global growth could fall 
further to 2–2.5 percent, while inflation could rise 
above 5–6 percent, creating severe economic 
challenges worldwide.

1.1	Global Trade
Global trade continued to expand in early 2026, 
although momentum has weakened relative to 
the strong gains recorded during 2025. According 
to the IMF’s WEO, April 2026, world trade volume 
growth is estimated at about 5.1 percent in 2025 
and is projected to slow sharply to 2.8 percent in 
2026, before recovering to 3.8 percent in 2027. The 
2026 slowdown reflects weaker global demand, 
persistent geopolitical tensions, rising trade costs, 
and supply disruptions linked to the conflict in 
the Middle East.

Source: IMF WEO April 2026
According to the UNCTAD Global Trade Update 
of April 2026, the value of global trade in goods 
and services reached approximately US$35 trillion 
in 2025, an increase of about US$2.5 trillion (7.5 
percent) compared with 2024. Goods accounted 
for about US$1.8 trillion of that increase (around 7 
percent growth), while services contributed roughly 
US$700 billion (around 8 percent growth). UNCTAD 
estimated that the positive trend continued in the 
first quarter of 2026, with goods and services 
expected to grow by 2.5 percent and 2 percent, 
respectively.
According to the OECD International Trade 
Statistics of 2026Q1 report, G20 merchandise 
trade expanded strongly in the first quarter of 
2026, with both exports and imports rising by 
5.3 percent quarter-on-quarter compared with 
the fourth quarter of 2025, partly driven by trade 
of semiconductors and other high-technology 
products in East Asia. Estimates also indicate 
that G20 trade in services expanded modestly, 
with exports rising by 1.7 percent and imports by 
1.5 percent, despite disruptions to trade related 
to the ongoing crisis in the Middle East.
1.2 Global Inflation
Global inflation pressures have re-intensified 
in early 2026, reversing part of the disinflation 
observed in recent years. The impact of higher 
energy prices, driven by the conflict in the Middle 
East and disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, has 
been transmitted to broader commodity markets, 
raising inflation expectations and lifting headline 
inflation across most regions. According to the 
IMF’s WEO, April 2026, global headline inflation is 
projected to rise from an estimated 4.1 percent in 
2025 to 4.4 percent in 2026, before declining to 
3.7 percent in 2027. This represents an upward 
revision of 0.6 percentage points for 2026 and 
0.3 percentage points for 2027 compared to the 
January 2026 WEO, reflecting the impact of higher 
energy and food prices.

1.3	Global Financial Stability 
According to the IMF Global Financial Stability 
Report (April 2026), global financial stability risks 
increased in the first quarter of 2026 as markets 
adjusted to heightened geopolitical tensions in 
the Middle East. Since late February, global equity 
markets have weakened, while sovereign bond 
yields have risen amid higher energy prices and 
renewed concerns about inflation. The US dollar 
strengthened moderately, reflecting its traditional 
safe-haven role during periods of heightened 
uncertainty. Although financial markets have 
remained orderly, the IMF highlights that risks 
have become increasingly asymmetric, with the 
possibility of a sharper tightening in global financial
conditions if geopolitical tensions persist.
In AEs, financial markets remained broadly resilient, 
though long-term bond yields edged higher in several 
jurisdictions, reflecting concerns over elevated 
public debt, persistent inflation pressures, and 
uncertainty about the pace of monetary easing. 
EMDEs have been disproportionately affected by the 
recent market repricing. Capital flows to emerging 
markets, largely intermediated by nonbank financial 
institutions, have shown heightened sensitivity to 
shifts in global risk sentiment, with commodity-
importing economies and those with pre-existing 
vulnerabilities experiencing the largest impacts. 
Sovereign spreads have widened in several emerging 
markets, while local-currency bond markets came 
under pressure during periods of US dollar strength.
Inflation in AEs is projected to edge up from 2.5 
percent in 2025 to about 2.8 percent in 2026, before

Financial Sector Development 
5
During the first quarter of 2026, the Central Bank of 
Somalia continued to make important progress in 
strengthening Somalia’s institutional and regulatory 
framework. Ongoing reforms focused on enhancing 
financial sector regulation and supervision, 
promoting financial inclusion, and improving the 
Anti-Money Laundering and Counter Financing of 
Terrorism (AML/CFT) framework contributed to 
reinforcing confidence in the country’s financial 
system. Preparations for the reintroduction 
of the Somali Shilling under a currency board 
arrangement also moved forward, underscoring 
the Banks’ commitment to monetary stability 
and stronger macroeconomic management. 
These reforms remain part of Somalia’s broader 
economic modernization agenda, supported by 
international partners including the International 
Monetary Fund and the World Bank.
During the same period, the Central Bank of 
Somalia achieved a major institutional milestone 
by obtaining the internationally recognized ISO/
IEC 27001:2022 certification for its Information 
Security Management System (ISMS). The 
certification, issued by the Management Systems 
Certification Body, confirms that the Bank’s 
information security framework, including its 
ICT infrastructure, applications, and critical 
information assets, complies with global best 
practices in information security management 
and risk control. This achievement reflects 
Banks’s continued commitment to strengthening 
operational resilience, institutional governance, 
cybersecurity, accountability, and internal control 
systems. 
Regulatory progress also extended to new segments 
of the financial sector. In January 2026, the Central 
Bank of Somalia granted operating licenses to four 
Takaful companies, marking an important step 
in the formal regulation of Somalia’s insurance 
sector in line with Shariah principles and risk-based 
supervisory standards. This brought a previously 
unregulated segment of the financial system under 
formal oversight, strengthening consumer protection 
and widening the scope of CBS supervision. Further 
strengthening the financial regulatory framework, 
in March 2026 the National Payment System Law 
was enacted, providing a comprehensive legal 
basis for the regulation, oversight, and operation of 
payment systems and payment service providers 
in Somalia. CBS also announced its participation in 
the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System 
(PAPSS), an important step toward improving 
cross-border payment infrastructure, supporting 
intra-African trade, and integrating Somalia further 
into the continent’s financial architecture.
As of 2026Q1, Somalia’s financial sector comprised 
15 licensed banks (including 14 domestic 
commercial banks and 1 foreign bank), 17 licensed 
money transfer businesses (remittances), 6 mobile 
money operators, 19 microfinance institutions, 
7 Takaful (Islamic insurance) institutions, and 2 
payment systems. In recent years, the Central 
Bank of Somalia has strengthened the legal and 
regulatory framework governing non-bank financial 
institutions, including Takaful companies and 
microfinance institutions, through new licensing, 
reporting, and supervisory regulations introduced 
in 2025.

5.3	National Payment System
Payment system activity moderated in the first 
quarter of 2026 after the elevated levels recorded in 
the fourth quarter of 2025, which were partly driven 
by a sharp increase in December transaction values. 
The system operated through two main channels: 
the Real Time Gross Settlement system, which 
processes large-value interbank payments, and 
the Automated Clearing House, which facilitates 
retail and lower-value transactions.
During the quarter, the national payment system 
processed 337,788 transactions with a combined 
value of approximately US$592.9 million. Compared 
with the previous quarter, transaction volume 
declined by 14 percent from 392,103 transactions, 
while transaction value fell by 26 percent from 
US$798 million. On a year-on-year basis, transaction 
volumes were broadly stable compared with 
339,504 transactions in 2025Q1, while total 
transaction value increased by 23 percent from 
US$484.3 million, indicating a higher average 
value of payments processed through the system.
RTGS processed 10,558 transactions during the 
quarter with a combined value of approximately 
US$467.8 million. Although RTGS accounted for 
less than 4 percent of total transaction volume, it 
represented around 79 percent of total transaction 
value, consistent with its role in settling large-value 
interbank obligations. On a year-on-year basis, 
RTGS transaction value increased by 31 percent 
from US$357.9 million in 2025Q1, pointing to 
stronger high-value payment activity.
The Automated Clearing House accounted for most 
payment activity by volume, processing 327,230 
transactions with a combined value of US$125.2 
million during the quarter. ACH transactions 
represented approximately 97 percent of total 
system volume, broadly in line with previous 
quarters. On a year-on-year basis, ACH transaction 
value declined slightly by 1 percent from US$126.4 
million 
in 
2025Q1, 
while 
volumes 
declined 
modestly from 330,251 transactions.
