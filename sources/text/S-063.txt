FOREWORD
Over the last year, Somalia has continued taking important steps towards a stronger economy and 
more resilient institutions. In December, Somalia qualified for full and irrevocable debt relief under 
the Highly Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC), eliminating the debt burden that had plagued the country 
for over three decades. It also joined the East Africa Community (EAC), the 30-year UN arms embargo 
was lifted, and Somalia was elected to a non-permanent seat at the UN Security Council in June 2024. 
These are huge achievements, which should also help boost investor confidence and attract foreign 
direct investment, encouraging increased broad-based private sector activity and investment.
At the same time, the impacts of climate change continue to devastate the country. Somalia slowly 
started to emerge from the severe 2022 drought, but was hit by devastating floods later in the year 
and early 2024. Somali households are also frequently exposed to shocks, with over two-thirds of 
the country’s population reported having experienced at least one shock in 2021–2022 that severely 
affected their economic situation. Climate-induced displacements, heightened conflicts over scarce 
natural resources, and increased vulnerability among marginalized groups like rural populations, 
women and youth exacerbate the country’s fragility.
In this context, economic prospects for Somalia are improving although the pace of economic growth 
remains modest. As such, we see that the economy is rebounding, inflationary pressures have eased, 
and fiscal performance has improved. The outlook remains positive, with economic growth set to 
accelerate, albeit at modest pace. Nevertheless, despite Somalia’s steps toward recovery, the outlook 
poses significant risks, including global economic shocks, climate-related shocks, and security threats.
The 9th edition of the World Bank’s Somalia Economic Update (SEU) assesses key economic 
developments, prospects, and policies in Somalia. The Update is intended for a wide audience 
including policy makers, business leaders, the community of analysts and professionals engaged in 
economic debate, and the public.
This SEU addresses climate change challenges for economic growth. It highlights the urgent need of 
the Government of Somalia to support sustained and long-term growth, anchored on macroeconomic 
stability and broad-based structural reforms, while laying the groundwork for sustainable development 
and enhanced climate resilience, ensuring a more secure future for its people.
Kristina Svensson
Country Manager, World Bank Somalia

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Somalia’s economy rebounded in 2023 as the 
country emerged from the severe 2020–23 drought 
and the 2022 global commodity price shocks. 
Real gross domestic product (GDP) growth is 
estimated at 3.1 percent in 2023, up from 2.4 
percent in 2022. Favorable rains starting in April 
2023 led to improved agricultural production, 
reduced food insecurity, and served to support 
private consumption. The recovery in exports 
was faster than expected as livestock exports—
the key driver of export earnings—rebounded 
with improved weather conditions. Nevertheless, 
net exports continue to be a drag on growth as the 
economy heavily relies on imports. 
Private 
sector 
credit 
growth 
strengthened 
construction, 
real 
estate, and investment. However, 
remittances, 
which 
support 
consumption, 
investment, 
and 
terms of trade, were muted in 2023, 
reflecting tighter global economic 
conditions.
Inflationary pressures eased, supported by a 
recovery in the agriculture sector and declining 
global commodity prices. Overall inflation, 
however, remained sticky compared with pre-
2022 levels, averaging 6.1 percent in 2023 
compared with 6.8 percent in 2022. Recovery 
in agricultural production helped to ease local 
staple food prices, with food inflation averaging 
just 0.7 percent in 2023, compared with 13.9 
percent in 2022. The decline in global commodity 
prices led to lower fuel and energy prices locally, 
though still high compared with pre-2022 levels, 
contributing to the stickier overall inflation. This 
is compounded by increasing local house rents, 
and telecommunication services, mainly from 
telephone and telefax equipment price increases, 
and transportation costs. The economy’s de facto 
dollarization continues to be a source of price 
stability, especially in times of low and stable 
global commodity prices. The exchange rate 
(Somali shilling [SSh] to the US dollar) remained 
relatively stable, averaging SSh 27,201 to US$1.00 
in 2023.
The Federal Government of Somalia (FGS) 
has continued to maintain broad-based fiscal 
stability, 
even 
though 
challenges 
remain. 
Fiscal performance improved in 2023, driven by 
sustained domestic revenue mobilization efforts. 
As a result, the budget closed with a small surplus, 
but fiscal challenges remain as domestic revenues 
continue to be too low to cover 
the country’s huge development 
needs. While domestic revenues 
increased to 2.8 percent of GDP in 
2023, up from 2.5 percent in 2022, 
this accounts for 45 percent of the 
FGS’s total revenues, leaving a large 
share of public expenditures to be 
financed by external grants. Donor 
grants accounted for 55 percent of 
total revenues in 2023. Furthermore, almost all 
expenditures are recurrent and non-discretionary, 
with wage bill and security costs consuming most 
of the total FGS spending. Spending on social 
services is financed through external grants. In this 
regard, the FGS’s capacity to finance investments 
that could reduce poverty or increase economic 
growth is limited.
Somalia achieved a historic debt relief milestone 
in December 2023, eliminating the debt burden 
that had plagued the country for over three 
decades. The country received full and irrevocable 
debt relief of US$4.5 billion, almost wiping out all 
of Somalia’s external debt, which is estimated to 
have fallen to 5.4 percent of GDP in 2023, down 
from 64 percent in 2018. In the post-Heavily 
Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) environment,

Executive Summary
Somalia now has expanded opportunities to 
finance investments in development priorities and 
expand basic service delivery. Nevertheless, the 
FGS must continue taking important steps to avoid 
falling back into debt distress. The authorities will 
need to continue to strengthen macroeconomic 
institutions, 
strengthen 
debt 
management 
capacity, and rely on concessional borrowing to 
allow the country to continue domestic reforms 
to boost growth and strengthen public finances. 
The Government’s commitment to maintain 
fiscal discipline and increase domestic revenue, 
together with structural reform in key areas, will 
help to safeguard long-term debt sustainability.
The 
country 
has 
also 
made 
significant strides toward regional 
integration. Somalia was admitted 
into the East Africa Community 
(EAC) bloc on November 24, 2023, 
and became a full member on 
March 4, 2024. It is also making 
progress toward joining the World 
Trade Organization (WTO). These 
efforts are seen as opening up 
opportunities to enhance trade 
integration in the region and 
globally. For example, by joining the EAC, Somalia 
has gained access to a market of over 300 million 
people, bringing opportunities for economic 
expansion, diversification, and job creation. 
Medium-Term Outlook and Risks
The recovery in economic growth is projected to 
continue into the medium term. Real GDP growth 
is projected to expand to 3.7 percent in 2024 and 
3.9 percent in 2025. This is due to the continued 
rebound 
in 
household 
demand, 
domestic 
investment, and government spending. Average 
Gu and Dyer rains in 2024 are projected to lead 
to continued recovery of agricultural productivity, 
improve household incomes and food security, 
and boost exports in 2024 and 2025. This, coupled 
with further easing in global commodity prices 
and continued social protection measures, will 
cushion household incomes, especially among 
the vulnerable, supporting private consumption 
growth. Economic reforms and increased public 
investment with Heavily Indebted Poor Countries 
Completion Point (HIPC CP) dividends will boost 
investor confidence and attract foreign direct 
investment (FDI), encouraging increased broad-
based private sector activity and investment. 
Nevertheless, despite Somalia’s steps toward 
recovery, the outlook poses significant risks, 
including global economic shocks, climate-related 
shocks, and security threats. A 
further slowdown in global growth 
could contribute to inflationary 
pressures, pushing up commodity 
prices once again. Given Somalia’s 
dependence on imports, especially 
consumption goods, higher prices 
from trading partners will pass 
through to domestic prices, adding 
to 
inflationary 
pressures 
and 
suppressing 
economic 
activity. 
Higher oil prices would increase 
Somalia’s import bill, weakening its 
already vulnerable external sector. A slowdown in 
the global economy would also likely contribute 
to a further weakening of remittance inflows 
from Somalia’s large diaspora, affecting private 
consumption 
and 
investment. 
Importantly, 
weather volatility is a perennial source of risk, 
with extreme climate events such as droughts 
and floods, as experienced recently, likely to mute 
ongoing agricultural recovery, in turn reversing or 
slowing overall growth. Similarly, insurgency and 
conflict continue to pose risks to economic activity. 
Terrorist attacks by Al-Shabaab (AS) continue 
to threaten the security situation, discouraging 
potential domestic and foreign investors, and 
putting more pressure on the FGS to increase 
government spending on security.

Executive Summary
Somalia Climate Change and Adaptation 
Policy Actions
Somalia has witnessed severe climate disasters 
and faces a multitude of climate risks, which 
pose a serious threat to its future economic 
development, industrialization, trade, and urban 
development. Climate has shaped Somalia’s 
human geography and economy, and climate 
change and variability are major contributors 
to ongoing fragility. The interplay between 
climate impacts and Somalia's social and political 
fragilities amplifies the risks. The country has 
witnessed severe climate disasters, notably the 
longest drought in recent decades, followed by 
severe flooding in vulnerable areas. There is some 
overlap in climate risk and poverty rates, with the 
poor vulnerable to climate change concentrated in 
the Shabelle river basin (floods), the 
northeast (heat) and, more broadly 
Somalia (three risks). Somalia is 
highly vulnerable to climate shocks 
that hurt growth and hinder poverty 
reduction efforts. Frequent shocks 
including repeated climate-related 
events, such as cycles of droughts, 
floods, and locust infestations, have 
interrupted the country’s economic trajectory. 
As a result, growth has been low and volatile, 
exacerbating the high levels of poverty across 
the country. Acute climate disasters, especially 
prolonged droughts and inland flooding can have 
wide-ranging social and economic consequences 
on production and exports, food security, and 
social vulnerability and cohesion.
The cycles of extreme events—droughts and 
floods—are the most obvious and currently 
impactful manifestation of climate change 
and climate variability. Impacts are remarkably 
widespread, but particularly severe on rural and 
pastoral households, and are a major driver of 
rural-urban migration. Somali households are 
frequently exposed to shocks, with over two-
thirds of the country’s population reportedly 
having experienced at least one shock in 2021 
or 2022 that severely affected their economic 
situation. 
Climate-induced 
displacements, 
heightened conflicts over scarce natural resources, 
and increased vulnerability among marginalized 
groups, especially rural populations, women, and 
youth, exacerbate the country's fragility.
Somalia needs to get ahead of the impacts of 
acute climate disasters by investing in resilience 
and preparedness rather than ex-post spending 
on humanitarian aid, which continues to 
consume the bulk of external assistance. This will 
require investments in disaster risk management 
(DRM), social protection, and more resilient 
rural livelihood systems (potentially including 
future retreat from some areas where livelihood 
systems are no longer viable). 
Furthermore, as Somalia navigates 
rapid urbanization, partly driven 
by climate-induced vulnerabilities 
in rural areas, climate-proofing 
development requires a broader 
set 
of 
policy 
actions 
and 
investments. These include resilient 
infrastructure and livable cities 
through climate-smart urban planning that ensures 
sustainable energy, transport, and water services, 
and includes green spaces and reduces urban heat 
islands. Investments in public health and human 
capital, such as post-disaster emergency health 
provision, disease vector monitoring and control 
programs, health and safety regulations for public 
buildings and workspaces, and public awareness 
campaigns, are also required.
Extreme events are unlikely to reduce in the 
foreseeable climate future, but the burden of 
climate impacts will shift. Climate resilience will 
need to be fully mainstreamed into Somalia’s 
development. Demographic and development 
trends will expose more urban and infrastructure 
assets to flood hazards, and shifting climate 
conditions will put more chronic stress on

Tight monetary policies, restrictive financial

expected to end as the global headline inflation 
has reduced, policy rates are expected to 
decline only gradually, as core inflation remains 
more persistent. In an environment of relatively 
high real interest rates, economic growth in 
the United States in 2024 is expected to reach 
1.6 percent, down from 2.5 percent in 2023, 
while slightly increasing in the Euro area to 0.7 
percent in 2024, up from 0.4 percent in 2023 
as energy prices ease. Financial conditions in 
the United States became looser during 20231 
and, as of 2024Q1, economic growth has been 
outperforming expectations, putting pressure 
on global interest rates. High interest rates 
in advanced economies have created more 
challenging financial conditions in emerging 
markets and developing economies (EMDEs), as 
several EMDEs still face liquidity challenges and 
elevated borrowing costs. Moderate consumer 
sentiment and challenges experienced by the 
property sector have been slowing China’s 
growth, which is expected to fall to 4.5 percent 
in 2024, down from 5.2 percent in 2023. Despite 
these headwinds, global trade is nonetheless 
expected to pick up this year, driven partially 
by a recovery of demand for goods, after 
remaining flat in 2023. Going forward, global 
growth is projected to accelerate in 2025 (to 
2.7 percent) as both advanced economies and 
EMDEs recover, interest rates decrease, and 
global financial conditions ease.2  
Commodity prices are expected to continue 
their downward trend in 2024. Despite still 
being above pre-pandemic levels, prices of 
most commodities fell in 2023 due to moderate 
demand. It is expected that oil prices will remain 
relatively unchanged in 2024 at around US$81/
bbl, falling by only 2.4 percent from 2023 levels 
as the global economy decelerates. In the same 
way, food prices fell by 9 percent in 2023 and 
are expected to fall by a further 1 percent in 
2024 due to abundant supply of major crops. 
The prices of coffee (arabica) and tea (average) 
followed a similar trend in 2023, with declines 
of 19.3 and 10.2 percent, respectively. These 
events are contributing to a decline of global 
inflation, which will continue in 2024–25 but 
will remain above its pre-pandemic average 
beyond 2024.
Despite global headwinds and a challenging 
domestic context, economic growth in Sub-
Saharan Africa (SSA) is expected to rebound

 Box 2.1: Recurrent climatic shocks are intensifying Somalia’s already fragile environment
Climate change may increase the frequency of shocks and introduce a multitude of more gradual changes, 
affecting growth and livelihoods. Somalia faces escalating climatic shocks that are compounded by insecurity and 
economic instability challenges. Recurrent climate-related stocks, such as cycles of droughts, floods, and locust 
infestations, have severely disrupted agricultural activities, livelihoods, and depleted water resources. These 
impacts have led to rising food insecurity, poverty, and internal displacement, exacerbating national instability and 
economic woes. The country has experienced 14 droughts in the past two decades, devastating the agriculture 
sector, which employs over 70 percent of the population. Locust infestations and recurrent flooding have further 
aggravated these challenges.
Somalia faces a multitude of climate risks that pose a serious threat to its future economic development, 
industrialization, trade, and urban development. Somalia is highly vulnerable to climate shocks that hurt growth 
and hinder poverty reduction efforts. Frequent climate-related stocks, such as cycles of droughts, floods, and 
locust infestations, have interrupted the country’s economic trajectory. As a result, growth has been low and 
volatile, exacerbating the high poverty rates across the country. Acute climate disasters, especially prolonged 
droughts and inland flooding can have wide-ranging social and economic consequences on production and 
exports, food security, and social vulnerability and cohesion.
The interplay between climate impacts and Somalia’s social and political fragilities amplifies the risks. 
Addressing the immediate risks posed by recurrent climate disasters require investments toward disaster risk 
management (DRM), such as strengthening emergency response architecture, climate information systems, and 
integrating DRM into strategic and spatial planning for key sectors, along with adaptive social safety nets and a 
national disaster risk financing strategy. Other key strategies include enhancing resilience of rural livelihoods and 
natural resources, and investments in water management systems.
Looking ahead, rainfall variation in Somalia is projected to increase, which is expected to lead to more frequent 
incidences of drought and flooding. As capacity for water resource management is at a nascent stage and 
institutions for DRM response need to be developed, these extreme weather events are associated with damaging 
exports of livestock, diminishing the livelihoods of pastoralists, internal displacement, and the deterioration of 
agricultural value chains, as well as increase flood risk in rapidly expanding cities. Adapting to and mitigating the 
effects of climate change requires action at multiple levels, from supporting households to cope with shocks and 
mainstreaming climate-risk analysis in routine investment decisions and sectoral planning, to encouraging long-
term growth of less climate-dependent sectors. Part II of this report provides a detailed deep dive into climate 
change impacts in Somalia.

4. Conclusion and Summary Policy Options 
Supporting sustained and long-term growth 
in Somalia will require building economic 
resilience through persistent reform efforts. 
Rebuilding the economy from the effects of 
the prolonged civil war will require maintaining 
macroeconomic 
stability, 
strengthening 
institutions, and advancing structural reforms 
that boost private sector development and 
job creation. The country can only finance 
much-needed investments if resources are 
available from both private investors and 
development partners. Accessing resources 
from development partners will need to go in 
tandem with strengthening the governance 
environment, as the country taps into the 
post-HIPC CP dividends. Moreover, structural 
reforms are needed to reinvigorate private 
investment, including improving the business 
environment, unlocking access to finance, and 
connecting workers to jobs through investing in 
human capital.

5. The Context of Climate Change in Somalia 
The Federal Government of Somalia (FGS) has 
set poverty reduction, inclusive growth, and 
socio-economic development at the core of 
its National Development Plan for 2020–2024 
(NDP9), as the country works to break cycles 
of crises arising from decades of conflict, 
political instability, and underdevelopment. 
The country is highly vulnerable to climate 
hazards, including those exacerbated by climate 
change, yet the country receives little in the 
way of climate finance. The drought that began 
in Somalia in 2020 and continued until early 
2023 resulted in famine that affected more than 
6.6 million people (IPC, 2023). People faced 
extreme personal hardships, from the loss of 
livelihoods to hunger, misery, illness and death. 
All of this is difficult to monetize. However, the 
increase in humanitarian appeals, from US$1.09 
billion in 2021 to US$2.27 billion in 2022 (OCHA, 
2022), reflects the need for greater financial 
support to provide critical resources to these 
vulnerable people. Thus, there is a need to 
better understand what barriers and enablers 
exist in Somalia to access international support 
to address the adverse effects of climate change 
through climate resilient development, and 
to improve understanding of the tradeoffs in 
implementing various climate-related activities.
Embedding climate adaptation and disaster risk 
management (DRM) within socio-economic 
development and land-use planning can 
lessen vulnerabilities and exposure to climate 
change, thereby reducing climate change 
risks. The mitigation of greenhouse gas (GHG) 
emissions through a green energy transition, 
and sustainable land and water use can reduce 
the severity and extent of climate-related 
hazards, while adaptation to a changing climate 
and emissions mitigation will also reduce 
climate change risks. Not everyone will benefit 
equally, however, according to the ND-Gain 
Index, which measures countries’ vulnerability 
to climate change and the readiness to improve 
their resilience to climate change. While the 
ND-Gain Index ranks Somalia among the most 
vulnerable countries, it is also in a group of 
countries that receives the least climate finance. 
Other countries that are categorized as being in 
fragile and conflict-affected situations (FCAS) 
also consistently lag behind in accessing and 
receiving climate finance.
Somalia continues to face multiple and 
overlapping shocks that reduce economic 
growth and contribute to widespread poverty. 
Climate change is making periodic droughts 
and floods more frequent and severe and 
is inextricably linked to Somalia’s social and 
political 
vulnerabilities. 
Recurrent 
shocks, 
including a prolonged 2020–23 drought, 
disrupted Somalia's growth, devastating crops, 
livestock, and exports. Many fled their homes in 
search of food and water. The severe drought, 
coupled with global commodity price surges, 
intensified inflation, hampering household 
consumption. Somalia’s vulnerability to climate 
change is rooted in its arid and semi-arid climate, 
with high temperatures, erratic rainfall patterns, 
and lack of investment in climate mitigation and 
adaptation, and its limited capacity in sound 
environmental management. The country’s 
agriculture sector, which employs a substantial 
portion of its workforce, has been severely 
affected, leading to declining crop yields and 
livestock production. As Somalia has reached 
the HIPC Completion Point and debt relief has 
reduced the risk of debt distress to moderate, 
growth is expected to accelerate as reforms 
payoff, including reforms that were supported 
by the HIPC process, the Extended Credit Facility 
(ECF)-supported program, and IDA development 
policy financing. However, the outlook is subject 
to significant risks, including increasingly 
frequent climatic shocks.

6.1 Climate Overview 
Somalia’s natural and human geography is 
shaped by its harsh climate. Lying at the eastern 
extremity of the Sahel, Somalia has an arid to 
semi-arid climate. Average annual rainfall is 
under 200 millimeters in much of the country, 
although it is signifi cantly more in the northern 
highlands and the south. Mean daily maximum 
temperatures exceed 30°C in most areas, 
although they fall much lower in the northern 
highlands and are tempered by cool off shore 
currents along the eastern seaboard. Most 
of Somalia’s land area is covered by desert 
and semi-desert ecosystems, with sparse or 
seasonal grassland vegetati on. Over 50 percent 
of the country supports only extensive, 
nomadic pastoralism, with just 13 percent 
suitable for culti vati on, including seasonal 
agropastoralism, and a much smaller irrigated 
agropastoralism zone located along the two 
main river valleys (the Shabelle and the Juba). 
These limited agricultural areas, and nearby or 
coastal citi es, support the vast majority of the 
populati on (Figure 6.1).
By the end of the century, Somalia’s climate is 
likely to be signifi cantly diff erent—and probably 
even harsher. Unless global climate acti on 
accelerates markedly, average temperatures 
in Somalia are likely to be at least around 
3°C higher by the end of the century than at 
present, which means the country would be 
hott er than any nati on is currently (Mali and 
Burkina Faso are the hott est countries, at a litt le 
over 29°C) or has been within human history. 
Rainfall trends are uncertain: a moderate 
increase in rainfall is the most likely outcome 
over the course of the century, but Somalia 
could either become signifi cantly more arid 
(close to a pure desert country such as Qatar) 
or signifi cantly wett er (becoming a generally 
semi-arid country such as Kenya). If realized, 
such changes would have profound eff ects on 
both natural and agricultural ecosystems.
In additi on to the changes in mean conditi ons, 
variability and occurrence of climate extremes 
are also expected to increase. Recent trends 
toward increasing numbers of extremely hot 
and wet days are likely to conti nue or accelerate.

While low levels of precipitati on are a key driver

at present, or even to increase, due to increased

crop production, with an increasing probability

for this include climate change-related water 
shortages, lack of availability of and access to 
good pasture, invasion of unpalatable plant

shocks such as drought (FRS, 2018). 
Rising temperatures between the months of 
September and March may cause the soils 
in Somalia to become drier. Dry spells may 
increase in frequency and duration, and the 
projected increase in temperature will also 
increase evapotranspiration and, hence, crop 
water demand (Richardson et al., 2022). Staples, 
such as maize, sorghum, and millet will struggle 
with rising temperatures in Somalia. Projections 
suggest crop yields across SSA will decrease by 
10 percent under warming scenarios of 2°C, 
and up to 20 percent beyond 2°C (Carleton, 
2022). Beyond a temperature increase of 3°C, 
all present-day cropping areas for maize, millet, 
and sorghum in Somalia are likely to become 
unsuitable. Somali cereal production levels 
have not increased in the past 60 years. Studies 
have typically suggested a high level of current 
crop losses in Somalia due to climate change 
of around 30–50 percent. According to the 
Somalia Agriculture Technical Group (SATG), 
these grain losses (of the total harvest) rise to 
an average of 20–30 percent in Somalia and 
these losses may exceed this range in some 
cases. The greatest determinant of yields will 
be water availability, and the high uncertainty 
in predictions for Somalia means that there are 
currently no robust predictions.
Projected shifts in weather patterns are also 
likely to provide suitable environments for an 
increasing incidence of crop pests and disease. 
This will affect agricultural livelihoods and 
food availability across the region (Richardson 
et al., 2022). There may be a reduction in the 
prevalence of some vectors if climatic conditions 
become too warm or humid for them to thrive. 
At present, however, making robust predictions 
of likely pest and disease impacts is not possible, 
as there is inadequate understanding of the 
specific relationships between weather events, 
including seasonal patterns, and the severity of 
impact of individual pests and diseases.
Shortages of water and associated pasture 
growth also contribute to reduced productivity 
and reproductive performance of livestock. 
This includes a slow growth rate of animals, 
deterioration in body condition, reduced milk 
production, and poor reproductive performance 
in mature animals. Changes in temperature may 
compromise the quantity and quality of forage by 
increased lignification of plant tissues, reducing 
digestibility and the rate of degradation.
Heat stress on livestock is also a threat given 
that it decreases livestock productivity through 
reduced food intake and weight loss, reducing 
the chances of survival and reducing fertility 
(Bernabucci et al., 2010; Lallo et al., 2018; 
Nardone et al., 2010). Average maximum 
temperatures and the number of very hot days 
each year (daily maximum temperature above 
35°C) are projected to increase with high certainty 
across the country, with central Somalia—an 
important area for livestock—particularly badly 
affected. The frequency of severe heat stress

Climate change shifts key environmental 
factors that influence the abundance of pests, 
pathogens, and vectors that affect livestock. 
These effects are species specific, and in many

can be made (Richardson et al., 2022). Somalia 
has suitable environments for Rift Valley Fever 
(RVF). For example, Ae. Aegypti (the vector for 
RVF) is expected to be favored by temperature 
and rainfall increases, hence RVF incidence 
is likely to increase in the future (Diallo et al.,

most severe in the southern and central parts 
of Somalia, but in future may become more 
common in the north of the country (Diallo

fisheries 
are 
also 
expected 
to 
be impacted by climate change through

be impacted by increases in water temperature

driving away active species, such as tuna, which 
are intolerant of low oxygen levels. To date, the 
Somali upwelling does not seem to have been 
impacted, but climate change is already altering

bio-geochemistry off Somalia’s coast (World

a strong climate change scenario the Somali

and bigeye, and for tuna larvae from as early

substantially impact on mangrove ecosystems 
in Somalia due to sea-level rise of 1.1 to 3.8 
meters, changing ocean currents, increasing the 
intensity of storms, increasing temperatures, 
changing precipitation patterns, and reducing 
overall oceanic acidity (pH) (Ward et al., 2016).

Climate change in Somalia is characterized by 
recurrent drought and regular inland flooding, 
leading to failed crops, loss of livestock, and 
Somalia’s chronic food insecurity. Climate crises 
threaten Somalia’s socio-economic progress by 
increasing water and food scarcity, the need 
for humanitarian assistance, displacement, 
and the degradation of traditional livelihoods. 
Climate 
change 
and 
natural 
disasters 
increasingly affect people’s well-being and 
development prospects. A recent World Bank 
study shows that climate change may cause 
216 million people to migrate in their own 
country by 2050 and push as many as 132 
million people into poverty by 2030 (World 
Bank, 2021). Fragile contexts such as those in 
Somalia have recently faced cyclical climate-
induced shocks from drought, to flooding 
and locust infestations. Because of Somalia’s 
economy reliance on natural resources and 
man-made degradation of natural resources 
such as charcoal production and overgrazing, 
climate change could make the country more 
vulnerable to poverty and food insecurity. 
Greater drought exposure tends to decrease 
consumption by a significant margin in rural 
Somalia, increasing poverty levels. This 
adverse impact of climate change on the poor 
will be far-reaching.
The recent drought in Somalia was among 
the most devastating in recent history. 
Following news about numerous livestock 
deaths from shortage of water, grim stories of 
human suffering started to emerge. Drought 
dampened economic activity in 2022, wiping 
out the agriculture sector and intensifying a 
humanitarian crisis. Relentless drought and high 
food prices weakened household livelihoods 
and purchasing power. The drought was the 
longest—it started in late 2020 and continued 
up to early 2023—and most severe in recent 
history. Indeed, it surpassed the 2010–2011 
and 2016–2017 droughts in both duration and 
severity. The debilitating drought left Somalia 
on the verge of a humanitarian catastrophe, 
destroying crops and livestock, and forcing huge 
numbers of people to leave their homes in search 
of food and water. Drought also decimated 
the performance of the agriculture sector as 
one-third of all livestock in the worst-affected 
areas had died since mid-2021. In addition, 
crop production remained extremely poor. This 
intensified a humanitarian crisis, with nearly 
half of the population being food insecure, and 
1.3 million people being displaced.
Most of Somalia’s many climate challenges are 
high-frequency events or chronic processes 
that affect the poor disproportionately and 
are likely to increase with climate change. The 
extent and burden of climate impacts will shift 
due to both climate change and wider socio-
economic trends. Demographic growth and 
economic development will expose more urban 
and infrastructure assets to flood hazards, and 
shifting climate conditions will put more chronic 
stress on livestock and agricultural systems, 
human health and productivity, and the water 
supply. Extreme events such as droughts and 
floods currently impose the largest economic 
impacts, but slow-onset impacts on agriculture 
and human health may surpass them in future. 
Biophysical effects of climate change impact the 
economy through a number of impact channels, 
the main ones of which are discussed below

7.1 	 Damage and Physical Capital
Disruption of infrastructure and associated

exclusion intersect and compound each other—

form of Islam one follows (Musse and Gardner,

climate events, such as drought and flooding.

internally displaced in Somalia at the end of

effects of drought in areas where IDPs choose 
to settle by increasing competition for scarce 
resources (Figure 7.4). They faced higher risks 
during the pandemic, especially because of the 
limited space in temporary shelters and internal 
displacement camps.

7.6 	 Climate, Fragility and Conflict 
Resource scarcity and displacement are the two 
major pathways through which climate change 
shapes social fragility and conflict. About 40 
percent of all intrastate conflicts can be linked 
to the exploitation of natural resources (UNEP, 
2021). There is growing evidence that links 
increased temperatures and drought to conflict 
risk in Africa, particularly in populations that 
depend on agriculture and natural resources.
 
In Somalia, climate crises have historically been 
influenced by political factors—specifically, 
conflict and political instability (Maxwell 
and Majid, 2016). In particular, the shape and 
intensity of competition over natural resources 
has been formed by Somalia’s climate and 
continues to be affected by climate change. This 
in turn has influenced the social divisions and 
hierarchies, and the political economies that 
have driven conflict.
Control of its scarce arable land has been 
closely connected to power in Somalia’s 
recent political history in ways that intersect 
with climate change (Jaspars, Adan and 
Majid, 2019). During the Barre military junta 
government in the 1970s and early 1980s, there 
was intensive cash cropping in the fertile land 
along and between the rivers, relying on cheap 
and exploitable labor. After state collapse in the 
late 1980s, clan-based militias fought for control 
over resources. Competing claims of ownership 
are also common in urban areas, frequently 
over land that was once public or either looted 
or vacated by fleeing households (RVI and HIPS, 
2017). The history of nationalizing land has 
allowed for patronage between the state and 
influential clan members and political elites, 
while marginalizing vulnerable groups such 
as those living in displacement and increasing 
conflict (Deherez, 2009).
Small-scale conflict over land resources is 
widespread in Somalia and is considered 
to aggravate the existing resource scarcity 
and humanitarian crisis (Thulstrup et al., 
2020). Protracted land-based conflicts have 
also weakened customary natural resource 
management systems, rendering traditional 
dispute resolution mechanisms ineffective 
or disempowered (Vivekananda et al., 2019). 
Customary law has become an ineffective 
tool to settle the growing number of resource 
conflicts, often driven by extreme weather and 
becoming increasingly complex and virulent. 
In such situations, shifts in resource access 
and competition have a high potential to both 
escalate into violent conflict and to further 
degrade the resource base. It is widely accepted 
that the combined social impacts of climate 
and conflict contribute to maintaining weak 
institutions and governance in Somalia, which in 
turn exacerbates or at least perpetuates conflict. 
While there is little clear evidence of internal 
displacement leading directly to increased 
conflict, it exacerbates general vulnerability and 
erodes local conflict-management institutions.
Al-Shabaab 
has 
exploited 
crises 
and 
vulnerabilities linked to climate impacts 
in ways that drive further fragility. High 
youth unemployment associated with shocks 
and displacement is also believed to have 
contributed 
to 
al-Shabaab 
recruitment 
(UNDRR, 2022; El-Bushra and Gardner, 2016). 
During a key period of al-Shabaab control of 
southern Somalia (2006–12), it also exploited 
charcoal production that drove the loss of over 
7 percent of remaining forest (Rembold et al., 
2013), while earning it US$38–US$56 million, 
strengthening its ability to both conduct 
attacks and expand recruitment (Dek, 2021; 
Ujunwa et al., 2021). Even following a charcoal 
export ban, Kismayo port is alleged to be a 
major conduit for illegal charcoal, involving 
local businesspersons, political leaders in the 
Jubaland administration, al-Shabaab, and units 
of AMISOM (Majid and Abdirahman, 2021). 
These further drives land degradation and 
vulnerability to climate impacts.

Somalia has made progress in developing a 
climate policy framework, but more work 
is needed to mainstream climate in sector 
policies and strategies. Climate and disaster risk 
management in Somalia is increasingly featured 
within the national conversation around political 
stabilization and development trajectories. The 
core Somalia climate change policy documents 
recognize Somalia’s dire climate status and 
reference its ranking as one of the world’s most 
vulnerable countries. Somalia’s Ninth National 
Development Plan 2020–2024 (NDP9) identifies 
environmental and climate change as one of 
the most critical drivers of poverty, linked to 
food insecurity and reliance on food imports. 
It identifies a direct correlation between 
increasing frequency of climate emergencies 
and displacement, and calls for improving the 
resilience of the traditional livestock and crop 
production subsectors, while diversifying away 
from climate-dependent sectors to achieve 
climate-proof economic growth. However, 
most policies and initiatives remain very high 
level. Considerable work is still required to 
develop policy frameworks that mandate or 
incentivize specific priority actions and to build 
institutions capable of implementing, enforcing, 
and 
monitoring 
these. 
Other 
challenges 
impeding adaptation include the law-and-
order situation, an unstable bureaucracy, and 
ineffective capacity development, coupled with 
significant climate finance needs. Given the 
high degree of uncertainty around medium- 
and longer-term climate outcomes in Somalia, 
it will be important to prioritize responses to 
current and immediate, rather than to uncertain 
future, conditions. Moreover, given very limited 
financial resources in relation to the scale of 
needs, it is critical that national development 
planning should be informed by climate risks, 
and adaptation planning needs to be focused 
around key growth sectors.
Climate adaptation and development cannot 
be separated in Somalia; neither can one 
succeed without the other. This will require 
working on two overall priorities in parallel: 
(i) managing the impact of climate disasters, 
so they do not torpedo development; and (ii) 
climate-proofing development for economic 
growth and diversification away from inherently 
vulnerable sectors. In the first area, DRM, social 
safety nets, and providing for more resilient 
rural livelihoods and production systems are key. 
For the second, it will be imperative to support 
the development of livable cities supported 
by resilient infrastructure and services, as well 
as sustainable human capital growth through 
addressing increasing climate health stressors. 
Given a wide range of important climate 
risks that Somalia faces, the lists of relevant 
measures are extensive. Most are already cited 
in Somalia’s Nationally Determined Contribution 
(NDC). More detailed work on key sectors and 
risks will be needed to identify and prioritize 
specific interventions and investment options, 
and to assess their economic, social, and 
environmental feasibility. 
For adaptation for the agriculture sector 
with respect to water storage and irrigation, 
we recommend the FGS to: (i) invest in soil 
and water conservation infrastructure such 
as terraces, subsurface dams, and rainwater 
harvesting; (ii) invest in upgrading and 
expanding irrigation in the Juba and Shabelle 
river basins (as irrigated agriculture is a 
key component to ensuring national food 
security; and (iii) incorporate a requirement 
to enhance rainwater harvesting through 
water ponds for both livestock and wildlife 
in large linear infrastructure projects (e.g., 
roads, fiber-optic cables).

Most adaptation priorities in Somalia are 
focused on addressing the acute challenges 
caused by current climate conditions, and 
are inherently no-regrets,11 but the risk of 
maladaptation must be taken seriously. 
Managing the impacts of climate disasters is 
largely about responding to current extreme 
climate conditions, whether this involves building 
DRM systems or adopting more sustainable 
agricultural 
and 
rangeland 
management 
approaches. Many investments to support 
resilient development, whether providing flood-
resistant infrastructure or investing in human 
capital, will also respond mainly to current 
climate conditions, especially where they 
support general adaptability or infrastructure 
that has a relatively short economic life. The 
priority interventions discussed below are 
overwhelmingly no-regrets. However, to avoid 
maladaptation long-term investment decisions 
should be supported by sensitivity analysis for 
a range of climate outcomes, and/or by using 
other tools to support decision-making under 
uncertainty. In particular, this includes major 
and long-lived infrastructure investments whose 
performance will depend on future rainfall, and 
spatial planning. Maladaptation may involve 
suboptimal investments (e.g., a large water 
storage project when smaller distributed 
structures would have been more cost-effective), 
ineffective investment (e.g., a water storage 
structure no longer fit for purpose because 
the rainfall is either much lower or higher 
than predicted), or harmful investments (e.g., 
a water storage structure that is causing more 
losses to downstream users than local benefits). 
Decisions on where to invest resources in either 
rural or urban development should consider 
the risk of livelihoods becoming non-viable or 
that extremely expensive defensive structures 
would be required. These are particularly 
important considerations for transport network 
development and urban planning.
Climate adaptation in Somalia must be rooted 
in efforts to disrupt the linkages between 
climate, social fragility, and conflict. This requires 
a do-no-harm approach, i.e., interventions to 
address one dimension of the problem should 
not exacerbate another, by employing social risk 
management tools informed by the social and 
political context. Peacebuilding and mediation 
need to be informed by climate and security 
risks, including through appropriate social and 
climate screening. Ensuring that women, youth, 
disadvantaged clans, and other vulnerable 
groups have full and equitable representation 
in the adaptation planning process is key to 
avoiding adverse social impacts of climate 
investments. 
Wherever 
possible, 
win-win 
solutions should be identified to address both 
the climate and social dimensions of risk in 
tandem, so climate adaption efforts are pro-
peace and peacebuilding initiatives support 
climate adaptation. 
Climate adaptation investment will need to 
be undertaken by the state, as well as private 
individuals and companies. The role of the 
state is critical in facilitating farmers, property-
owners, and businesses to act in their own best 
interests through providing appropriate climate 
information, technical guidance, incentives, 
and regulatory requirements. The largest public 
investments will probably be in infrastructure 
(e.g., transport, power supply, and urban 
development), where the primary objective 
will rarely be climate focused, but climate 
resilience will need to be built in, in accordance 
with climate-informed planning and design 
specifications, typically at some significant 
incremental cost.

8.1 	 Resilience to and Preparedness for 
Extreme Climate Events
A priority of the FGS is to strengthen national 
crisis preparedness and response capacities, 
and to link these more closely with nationally-
owned recovery and development planning 
processes. Following the inauguration of a 
new federal government in mid-2022, the 
decision was made to abolish the Ministry of 
Humanitarian Affairs and Disaster Management 
and to consolidate (under Law No. 17) the 
national emergency response and preparedness 
function in a re-constituted Somalia Disaster 
Management Agency (SODMA). Under the new 
arrangement, SODMA is responsible for the 
oversight and coordination of the entire national 
DRM function, including early warning, disaster 
preparedness, disaster response (including 
displacement), and the development of durable 
solutions to displacement. In this capacity, 
SODMA houses both the National Emergency 
Operations Center (NEOC) and the Multi-
Hazard Early Warning Center, and is required 
to maintain active relations with relevant 
ministries, departments and agencies (MDAs) at 
the federal level, and with disaster management 
agencies within the five FMSs and the Benadir 
Regional Administration (Mogadishu).
The Government’s federal and state-level 
DRM capacities interact directly with the UN-
led humanitarian system that, through the 
humanitarian planning process, coordinates 
the delivery of most emergency assistance in 
Somalia. In this way, SODMA is a member of the 
Humanitarian Country Team (HCT) and federal 
ministries participate in various UN-led cluster 
(technical) working groups. Similarly, state-level 
DRM agencies are members of “Area HCTs” 
and technical ministries will participate in local 
cluster working groups. There are no equivalent 
coordination structures for early warning or 
preparedness. 
The NEOC is at an early stage in its establishment 
and, while work has started on an office 
building, it is currently without dedicated 
accommodation nor a full complement of 
staff.  Using the World Bank’s Ready2Respond 
diagnostic tool, the FGS and the World Bank 
commissioned an assessment of national 
emergency preparedness and response (EP&R) 
capacity in Somalia. This includes nearly US$77 
million in investment needs, which has informed 
millions of dollars of ongoing capacity support 
to SODMA/NEOC. 
Somalia 
lacks 
a 
nationally-owned 
and 
managed emergency alert and early warning 
system. Currently, early warnings are produced 
by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)

Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET). Seasonal 
climate outlook information is provided by the 
Inter-Governmental Authority on Development 
Climate Prediction and Application Center. While 
progress has been made to strengthen hydrology 
and meteorology (hydromet) and early warning 
services over the past decade, these services 
are still fragmented and not integrated into 
government institutions. Somalia still lacks a 
nationally unified and coordinated hydromet 
service. In addition, the limited capacity of the 
Government in translating and disseminating 
hydromet information is inhibiting users’ access 
and uptake of early warning information, and

8.3 	 Accessing Finance for Climate 
Adaptation Related Activities
Climate finance for both adaptation and climate 
resilient development is limited in Somalia. 
Even though the country’s 2021 NDC to the 
UNFCCC states climate finance needs of US$5.5 
billion a year (equivalent to US$55.5 billion 
between 2021 and 2030), current inflow stands 
at just over US$300 million a year, representing 
just 6 percent of need (SPARC, 2023). However, 
it is important to understand that this figure for 
climate finance needs is unrealistic, especially 
given Somalia’s size. Compared with Somalia’s 
GDP of US$7.6 billion, an annual investment of 
US$5.5 billion is not technically feasible. Vast 
resources are needed to help Somalia adapt to 
climate change and address the famine that the 
country has faced since 2020. This means that 
access to, and the effectiveness of, finance for 
climate-related activities is fundamental.
Of the five sources of climate finance—
international 
vertical 
funds 
(e.g., 
GCF, 
GEF), carbon finance, Official Development 
Assistance (ODA), domestic revenue, and 
private 
investment—ODA 
and 
private 
investment are likely to remain the key sources 
for Somalia in the foreseeable future. Although 
steps to increase concessional funding from the 
vertical climate funds (VCFs) are of course very 
welcome, finance from such funds represents 
a small flow to Somalia compared with 
bilateral finance and finance from multilateral 
development banks (MDBs). The Green Climate 
Fund (GCF), the Adaptation Fund and the Global 
Environmental Fund (GEF) account for only 
1 percent of the total finance going toward 
climate-related activities in Somalia (based 
on 2019 and 2020 data from the OECD DAC 
database). Bilateral financial providers and 
MDBs currently provide 51 and 35 percent, 
respectively, of the climate finance to Somalia. 
However, most bilateral finance is allocated to 
meet humanitarian needs related to the  famine 
crisis, such as food security. Humanitarian 
actions do not necessarily address medium- or 
longer-term climate risks that will arise due to 
climate change interacting with demographic 
and economic shifts. This continued focus 
on short-term needs without understanding 
the implications of various interventions for 
medium- and long-term climate change risk 
management can perpetuate a dependence 
on humanitarian finance and deter efforts to 
promote climate-resilient and risk-informed 
development. Bilateral funding is also uncertain 
and volatile. Although it involves greater 
discretion on the part of donors compared with 
VCF funding, bilateral funding depends on the 
thematic and political priorities of the donor 
countries, which often change. Furthermore, 
bilateral donors may prefer to allocate finance 
to countries with a stable investment climate, 
where the returns may be higher. Funding for 
climate change adaptation in Somalia is not 
always a priority for bilateral donors.

Addressing Climate Change
Challenges for Economic Growth
Economic prospects for Somalia are improving although the pace of economic growth remains
modest. The economy rebounded in 2023 as the country emerged from the severe 2020—23 drought
and the 2022 global commodity price shocks. Favorable rains starting in April 2023 led to improved
agricultural production, boosted exports recovery, reduced food insecurity, and served to support
private consumption. Similarly, inflationary pressures have eased, and the fiscal performance has
improved. Medium term outlook remains positive, with economic growth set to accelerate, albeit at a
modest pace as significant risks remain, including global economic shocks, climate-related shocks,
and securit threats.
Somalia achieved a historic debt relief milestone in December 2023, eliminating the debt burden that
had plagued the country for over three decades. In the post-Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC)
environment, the country now has expanded opportunities to finance investments in development
priorities and expand basic service delivery. Nevertheless, the government must continue taking
important steps to avoid falling back into debt distress.
Somalia faces a multitude of climate risks that pose serious threats to its future economic development,
industrialization, trade, and urban development. Frequent shocks including repeated climate-related
events, such as cycles of droughts, floods, and locust infestations, have interrupted the country's
economic trajectory and exacerbated poverty levels. Moreover, climate-induced displacements,
heightened conflicts over scarce natural resources, and increased vulnerability among marginalized
groups like rural populations, women, and youth exacerbate the country's fragility. Addressing climate
challenges and risks s essential for building resilience and sustainable economic growth.
