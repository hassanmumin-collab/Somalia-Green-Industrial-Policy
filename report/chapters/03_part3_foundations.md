# Part 3: Enabling foundations

## Key messages

- Power comes first. Stand-alone solar with battery storage can supply the industrial parks at a fraction of what firms pay today, and it is the single investment that makes light manufacturing viable in Somalia.
- The riverine valleys that feed Cluster 2 once irrigated far more land than they do now. Solar pumps cut farmers' fuel bills and make regular irrigation affordable, but only within a system of water rights and river management.
- The parks need secure land on the outskirts of Mogadishu, on long leases under clear law.
- Gulf buyers of chilled meat, fish and processed food buy certificates as much as products. Somalia's standards, animal health and food safety systems must be credible before the first container ships.
- Customs duties fund most of the budget, so trade policy for the clusters must protect revenue while removing duties on the machinery and inputs that industry needs.

## 3.1 Energy: power for the parks

### The problem

Average electricity tariffs in Somalia range from 48 to 90 US cents per kWh {{C-0083}} {{C-0084}}, several times what competitors in the region pay (Figure 1.1). Private providers supply over 90 percent of urban electricity through isolated, diesel-based mini-grids {{C-0090}}, and the World Bank estimates that more than 55 such providers operate {{C-0492}}. The sector now has a regulator. The National Electricity Authority, established by the Electricity Act of 2023, has adopted tariff regulations and licensed 20 electricity service providers {{C-0097}}. The National Transformation Plan (NTP) sets a target of cutting electricity tariffs by 50 percent by 2029 {{C-0177}}.

### The solution: one solar-plus-storage company for all the parks

The policy will build the parks' electricity supply as a single stand-alone solar and battery system, owned and operated by one park power company and sized to the load of the tenant firms. In the base scenario, the parks are projected to need 70 GWh of electricity a year at full build-out (modelled estimate; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0004}}. Cluster 3, with its flour mills, garment lines and hygiene-product plants, accounts for the largest share (Figure 3.2).

{{FIG 3.2}}

Solar panels with four-hour batteries can meet most of this demand during the working day and into the evening. Sized to cover that share, the park plant is projected to need 27.6 MW of solar capacity in the base scenario (modelled estimate; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0003}}, at a projected capital cost of USD 39.4 million over 2028 to 2032 in the same scenario {{M-0008}}.

The cost of the power is the reason for this choice. Box 3.1 sets out how the model calculates it. In the base scenario, the levelised cost of the park supply is projected at USD 0.115 per kWh (modelled estimate; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0001}}. Of this, PV capital accounts for a projected USD 0.056 {{M-0005}}, battery capital for USD 0.043 {{M-0006}} and operation and maintenance for USD 0.016 (base scenario, modelled estimates; illustrative) {{M-0007}}. The Ministry of Energy's valuation of diesel self-generation equals an estimated 40 US cents per kWh {{D-0079}}, and Kenya's estimated average tariff is 20 US cents {{C-0489}} (Figure 3.1).

{{FIG 3.1}}

In the base scenario, the park solar supply is projected to save tenant firms USD 12.6 million in 2032 against diesel self-generation (modelled estimate; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0002}}. The same supply is projected to avoid 30,674 tonnes of CO2 in 2032 in the base scenario (modelled estimate; illustrative) {{M-0009}}. Because the solar supply is cheaper than the diesel it replaces, these emission reductions come at a negative cost. That makes the park plant a strong candidate for results-based climate finance, including under Article 6 once the Carbon Markets Act is in force (Part 6).

> **Box 3.1. How the model calculates the cost of park solar power**
>
> The levelised cost of electricity is the constant price per kWh that recovers the plant's capital and operating costs over its life at the chosen discount rate.
>
> Levelised cost = (annualised PV capital cost + annualised battery capital cost + annual operation and maintenance) ÷ electricity generated per kW of PV in a year.
>
> Each capital cost is annualised with the capital recovery factor, which spreads the cost evenly over the asset's life at the discount rate, as a loan repayment would. Batteries have a shorter life than panels, so they are annualised over fewer years. Equipment costs come from the Ministry of Energy's generation plan, with a premium for site works, interconnection and security. Electricity generated per kW comes from the Global Solar Atlas estimate for Mogadishu. The PV plant is sized to the solar share of the parks' mature demand, which is the sum over production lines of output multiplied by electricity use per unit. All inputs, their sources and their ranges are listed in the annex on model methodology and assumptions.

### How it will be procured and financed

The park power company will be a special-purpose company licensed by the National Electricity Authority, selling power to the park operator and tenant firms under a long-term power purchase agreement (Figure 3.3). Government provides the land, the licence and, once the legal framework exists, authorisation for any Article 6 transfer. Development finance institutions (DFIs), guarantee providers and private investors provide equity and loans. Solar equipment is eligible for duty-free import into Somalia, according to the Africa Solar Industry Association {{C-0216}}, which the policy will confirm in customs regulations.

{{FIG 3.3}}

**Policy measures: energy**

- Establish the park power company in the preparatory period and tender the solar-plus-storage plant as one package for all parks, with capacity added in phases as tenants arrive.
- Commission a load study with the first tenants, to replace the design estimates of electricity use with measured demand before the tender.
- Ask the National Electricity Authority to issue a licence and tariff framework for private park networks, so that the power purchase agreement is enforceable.
- Seek a partial risk guarantee from a DFI to lower the cost of capital, which is the largest single driver of the power price.

## 3.2 Solar irrigation and water governance

The Shabelle and Juba valleys are the source of Cluster 2's sesame and fruit. Before the war, the Ministry of Agriculture estimated the total irrigated area at 222,950 hectares {{C-0206}}. The area equipped for irrigation was 200,000 hectares in 1984 {{C-0210}}, and some 700,000 hectares are estimated to be able to produce crops under pump or recession-controlled irrigation {{C-0207}}. At the time of the civil war in 1991 there were nine functioning barrages along the Shabelle and one along the Juba {{C-0539}}. Much of that infrastructure has since deteriorated {{C-0208}}. According to a 2026 preprint that has not been peer reviewed, irrigation in the Afgoi district is now estimated to rely on diesel-powered pumps drawing from the Shabelle {{C-0538}}.

{{FIG 3.4}}

Fuel-powered pumping makes irrigation expensive and irregular. According to a 2026 news report from Afgoye district, one farmer estimated that he spent USD 100 to 150 each time he irrigated with a fuel-powered pump {{C-0530}} {{C-0543}}, and another estimated that each round cost him USD 300 to 500 in fuel {{C-0544}} {{C-0531}}, with fuel selling at an estimated USD 1 per litre {{C-0533}}. According to the same news report, a farmer invested an estimated USD 4,600 in a solar system {{C-0532}} and his June harvest earned an estimated USD 3,000 in profit, more than double what he made with a fuel-powered pump {{C-0534}}. These are individual cases, not survey results, but they show the mechanism: cheaper water means more frequent irrigation and higher yields.

The policy will finance the replacement of diesel pumps with solar pumps through a results-based programme. In the base scenario, 2,000 pumps are projected to be replaced by 2032 (modelled estimate; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0011}}, at a projected investment of USD 10.0 million over 2028 to 2032 (base scenario, modelled estimate) {{M-0013}}. They are projected to save farmers USD 4.5 million of diesel in 2032 {{M-0012}} and to avoid 11,977 tonnes of CO2 in the same year (base scenario, modelled estimates; illustrative) {{M-0014}}. No census of irrigation pumps exists, so the pump count is a planning figure to be replaced by a survey in the preparatory period.

Solar pumps have almost no running cost, so they remove the brake that fuel prices put on water use. Without rules, that invites over-abstraction in the dry season. The programme will therefore be tied to water governance: registration of pumps and abstraction points, seasonal allocation agreed through river user associations, and monitoring of river flows with FAO's Somalia Water and Land Information Management programme (SWALIM).

**Policy measures: solar irrigation**

- Survey pumps and irrigated plots in the Shabelle and Juba valleys during the preparatory period.
- Offer solar pumps through a results-based subsidy and credit line, conditional on registration with a river user association.
- Rehabilitate priority barrages and primary canals with development partners, so that solar pumping complements gravity irrigation instead of replacing it.

## 3.3 Land for the parks

The parks will sit on government land on the outskirts of Mogadishu, close to the port and the city's markets (Figure 1.6). The NTP already sets as a target the creation of Special Economic Zones for export-oriented industries {{C-0182}}, and this policy provides the industrial content for them. Secure land is a precondition for investors: a park that cannot guarantee title will not attract a Gulf joint venture or a DFI loan.

The legal framework is incomplete. The law on urban planning, building and land management exists only as a draft bill, prepared by the Ministry of Public Works in 2017 {{C-0540}}. Until a law is passed, the parks will rely on a presidential or cabinet decision allocating the land, and on long leases registered with the Banadir Regional Administration.

Evidence from the region shows what serviced land is worth. For Ethiopia's apparel sector, the World Bank estimated that a plug-and-play industrial park near Djibouti would reduce apparel exporters' costs by 2 percent and cut six days from delivery time {{C-0376}}. The Somali parks will be serviced in the same way, with power, water, drainage, effluent treatment and a single service window for permits.

**Policy measures: land**

- Identify and survey the park sites and secure them by cabinet decision in the preparatory period.
- Lease plots to investors for long terms, with rents that recover the cost of shared infrastructure.
- Pass the urban planning and land management law, or a dedicated special economic zones law, before the second phase of park development.

## 3.4 Standards and certification

Exports of chilled meat, fish and processed food depend on certificates that Gulf regulators and buyers trust. Somalia's livestock trade already shows the stakes: exports were estimated at more than 5.9 million head in 2025 {{C-0054}}, and every shipment depends on health certification. The Livestock Sector Development Strategy sets as a target a unified Animal Health Certificate issued only by Federal Government public veterinarians, as agreed with the Federal Member States and Saudi Arabia {{C-0535}}. It also cites the three-week quarantine among the regulatory costs of exporting {{C-0536}}.

Chilled meat raises the bar further. The Gulf pays an estimated 42 percent more for chilled beef than for frozen {{D-0058}}, but chilled meat has a short shelf life, so every step from herd to importer must be certified and fast (Figure 3.6). The Somali Standards and Quality Control Law of 2020 {{C-0195}} provides the legal basis for national standards. What is missing is the capacity to apply them to export plants.

{{FIG 3.6}}

**Policy measures: standards and certification**

- Complete the unified Animal Health Certificate and link it to animal identification and traceability for animals bought by the export abattoirs.
- Equip an accredited food-testing laboratory in the parks, so that samples do not travel abroad for testing.
- Certify the export plants for halal and food safety management (HACCP), and apply for plant listing with each Gulf importing country before the plants open.

## 3.5 Trade and customs

Customs duties are the backbone of public revenue. In 2024, taxes on international trade were USD 241.6 million {{C-0424}} out of tax revenue of USD 394.6 million {{C-0537}}, an estimated 61.2 percent of the total {{D-0080}} (Figure 3.5). Measured against goods imports, these taxes equalled an estimated 3.17 percent, an indicative effective rate {{D-0059}}.

{{FIG 3.5}}

This dependence shapes trade policy for the clusters. Every tonne of flour, soap or clothing made in Somalia instead of imported reduces customs revenue, so the policy cannot rely on duty exemptions for finished goods. It will instead remove duties on the machinery, spare parts and intermediate inputs that the parks need, and recover revenue through the formal taxation of park firms (Part 6). The reform agenda supports this. Under the IMF-supported programme, the authorities have set as a target a gradual transition to ad valorem customs duties {{C-0541}}, and initial discussions with the Federal Member States on the East African Community Common External Tariff have begun {{C-0542}}.

**Policy measures: trade and customs**

- Exempt capital goods and listed intermediate inputs for park firms from import duty, with a published list and a time limit.
- Keep duties on finished goods consistent with the ad valorem reform and the Common External Tariff, instead of raising them to protect new industries.
- Give park exporters a single customs window in the park, with electronic pre-clearance at the port.
