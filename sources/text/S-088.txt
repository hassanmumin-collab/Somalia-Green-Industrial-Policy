Executive Summary  
Composition and performance of the private sector 
Firms in the Federal Republic of Somalia are predominantly in the services sector, vast majority 
is informal. Private sector activity in the country is predominantly comprised of low-value-added 
service firms, with 72 percent of establishments operating in wholesale and retail trade, followed by 
public and social services and market-oriented services (12 percent and 11 percent, respectively), 
and manufacturing (5 percent).1 The majority of firms operate in the informal sector, with formal 
establishments representing about 28 percent of all businesses.  
Formal firms are overwhelmingly micro-sized, highly concentrated in a few urban centers, and 
largely male-owned. Nearly 97 percent of formal firms employ 1–9 people and are mostly structured 
as sole proprietorships, which account for 91 percent of these establishments.2 These firms are 
geographically concentrated, with Mogadishu alone hosting 31 percent of all formal establishments 
in the Federal Republic of Somalia,3 and generating 67 percent of total sales and 62 percent of formal 
private sector employment across the five surveyed cities.4 Female participation in firm ownership 
also remains extremely limited. Only 7 percent of businesses are majority-owned by women— 
substantially below both the Sub-Saharan Africa and global averages, where female-majority 
ownership stands at about 16 percent—underscoring persistent gender gaps in access to economic 
opportunities.5 
Sales growth in the formal private sector is highly uneven. In 2024, 54 percent of formal firms in 
the five cities that were analyzed experienced higher sales, up from 43 percent in 2023. While this is 
a positive trend, the share of firms reporting declining sales also rose, from 3 percent to 8 percent.6 
Specifically, 54 percent of firms reported an average increase of 17 percent, 38 percent saw no 
change, and 8 percent reported an average decrease of 14 percent. An establishment-weighted 
average of firms’ sales changes indicates a moderate improvement in sales performance, estimated 
at around 8 percent. Sales performance was geographically uneven. Improvements were 
concentrated in Mogadishu, where 65 percent of firms reported sales growth in 2024. In contrast, 
Bosaso lagged, with weakening sales performance (only 11 percent reported increases, while 32 
percent reported declines). Finally, firm size also mattered, as larger firms (10+ employees) led sales 
increases, with 61 percent reporting higher sales in 2024—more than double the 29 percent recorded 
in 2023, and higher than the 48 and 49 percent of smaller firms (5–9 employees) that reported 
increased sales in 2023 and 2024, respectively—suggesting that firm size continues to be a key driver 
of growth.

Unlike sales, productivity has grown only modestly, with gains concentrated among stronger 
firms while weaker firms fell further behind. Across the five cities, average labor productivity—
measured as output per worker—was US$1,540 (median US$1,333). This is modest compared to the 
median labor productivity for firms in several Sub-Saharan African countries (US$14,179).7 
Mogadishu and Beledweyne were the most productive in 2024, while Bosaso and Kismayo lagged. 
Notably, smaller firms were more likely to fall into the high or medium-high productivity categories 
(58 percent) compared to larger firms, where only 40 percent fell into these brackets. This challenges 
the common assumption that larger firms are inherently more efficient and suggests that some thrive 
in protected or non-tradable sectors without investing in innovation. When looking at productivity 
growth, overall improvements in 2024 were modest. Productivity increased only in Baidoa, remained 
broadly flat in Mogadishu, and declined in other cities. Moreover, the productivity distribution reveals 
growth among more productive firms, while those below the median experienced worsening 
performance. This divergence signals that recent gains in 2024 are not broadly shared and raises 
concerns about inclusive growth.  
Job creation remains limited despite stronger sales growth, reflecting weak labor absorption. 
In 2024, only 14 percent of firms reported expanding their workforce, down from 30 percent in 2023, 
while the share of firms reducing staff rose from 3 to 8 percent. Even in Mogadishu, which leads in 
sales growth, job creation was minimal. In effect, in Mogadishu 88 percent of firms reported no 
employment change in 2023 and 91 percent in 2024, while Bosaso saw sharp cuts in employment 
(41 percent of firms reduced staff in 2024).  
Weak labor absorption is accompanied by investment levels that are slightly below regional 
levels and marked differences across cities. On average, 31 percent of firms made fixed-asset 
purchases in 2024, slightly below regional and global averages (35 and 38 percent, respectively).8 
City patterns on this front diverged. Firms in Mogadishu led current investments (43 percent of firms), 
while firms in Kismayo (11 percent) and Bosaso (14 percent) lagged. By firm size, larger firms invested 
more than smaller ones (35 versus 28 percent), yet higher investment was not consistently linked to 
productivity gains. 
About 84 percent of firms sell primarily to local markets. Participation beyond local markets is 
low across the five cities that were analyzed and even more so in Baidoa (2 percent), Kismayo (2 
percent), and in Beledweyne (8 percent). Mogadishu and Bosaso show higher national reach (about 
18 percent each), and only firms in Mogadishu report international sales. By size, just 6 percent of 
smaller firms operate beyond local markets, versus 25 percent nationally and 4 percent 
internationally among larger firms. Overall, only 7 percent of firms export directly, and 42 percent use 
imported inputs—both below the global benchmark (11 percent and 56 percent, respectively).

Key business environment constraints 
Market competition emerged as the most pressing constraint for Somali firms in 2024, 
surpassing infrastructure issues. Across cities, 44 percent of firms cited competition as their top 
obstacle, and 57 percent of smaller firms versus 26 percent of larger firms identified competition as 
their primary constraint. Within competition, the main obstacles cited were price distortions (39 
percent of firms), entry barriers (28 percent of firms), and competition from informal firms (13 
percent of firms), with their relative importance varying across cities and firm sizes. By firm size, price 
distortions are slightly more binding for smaller firms (41 percent versus 34 percent). Price 
distortions were also most acute in Baidoa, Beledweyne, and Bosaso, where focus group discussions 
(FGDs) highlighted monopolistic practices in telecom, electricity, and imported goods as pressing 
issues. FGDs also attribute price distortions and entry barriers to monopolistic control over key 
industries, state favoritism, and weak competition oversight. Insecurity adds another layer of 
complexity, especially in the southern and central regions. Entry barriers are more binding for larger 
firms (32 percent versus 26 percent) and dominate as a constraint to competition in Mogadishu (42 
percent). Though reforms such as online registration have begun, enforcement remains uneven, and 
the institutional and regulatory landscape is fragmented across federal, state, and municipal levels. 
Competition from informal firms was also cited as a key reason for competition constraints 
overwhelmingly affecting smaller firms (20 percent of smaller firms versus 2 percent of larger firms). 
Taken together, IBES results and FGD perspectives underscore the importance of strengthening fair 
competition oversight and reducing regulatory fragmentation to level the playing field. 
Transportation and logistics constraints also remain a major barrier to firm performance in the 
Federal Republic of Somalia, driving up costs and limiting the ability of businesses to grow and 
compete. In both 2023 and 2024, transportation was among the most frequently cited obstacles to 
operations. Where transport is the top obstacle, firms most often cite high cost (54 percent), followed 
by limited storage (17 percent), speed (15 percent), and reliability (10 percent). In 2024, 
transportation ranked as the second highest obstacle across the five cities surveyed (13 percent), 
with this issue being reported most important in Bosaso (18 percent) and least reported in Kismayo 
(2 percent). Larger firms were also nearly twice as likely as smaller ones to cite transport as the top 
obstacle. Poor road conditions, high port fees, and limited storage and cold chain infrastructure were 
reported in FGDs as key factors disrupting supply chains and inflating operational costs, especially 
in sectors such as agribusiness. Focus group participants also emphasized that the majority of paved 
roads are in disrepair, with seasonal flooding further impeding the movement of goods. These 
logistical shortcomings contribute to fragmented markets, favor established firms with greater 
resources, and trap smaller businesses in local markets. As a result, the private sector continues to 
struggle with high prices, inconsistent supply, and limited access to broader investments and trade 
opportunities. The quality of logistics in the Federal Republic of Somalia has slipped in international 
comparisons. Its ranking on the World Bank Logistics Performance Index fell from 127/150 (2007) to 
138/140 (2023), while it stands at 133/140 for trade- and transport-related infrastructure quality.9

Furthermore, more than 90 percent of paved roads have deteriorated, with the Mogadishu-Bosaso 
trip sometimes taking five days.10 As a result, the private sector continues to struggle with 
inconsistent supply and limited access to broader investment and trade opportunities. 
Internet connectivity is increasingly recognized as a critical constraint to business growth, 
especially for larger firms and those in Mogadishu. In 2024, 7 percent of firms—up from just 2 
percent in 2023—identified it as a major constraint, with 90 percent of these citing low-speed 
connections as the main issue. Connectivity challenges were reported especially by firms in 
Mogadishu (13 percent) and by larger firms (17 percent). Persistent infrastructure gaps, especially in 
central and southern regions, limit speed and reliable connectivity. Larger and more digitally reliant 
firms face the greatest challenges, as poor internet hinders basic operations such as sales, 
coordination, and digital finance. Demand-side gaps also matter. Sectoral and geographic disparities 
in digital adoption persist, with broadband access and digital business practices remaining limited 
outside Mogadishu, especially in interior and secondary cities. Connectivity limitations also help 
explain why many firms remain local rather than expanding beyond their municipality. 
Access to electricity continues to hinder business operations, particularly due to high costs and 
unreliable service. These issues are especially acute outside major cities, where limited 
infrastructure restricts firms’ ability to operate beyond their local markets. Even in urban areas, 
where access is comparatively better, poor service quality and high electricity costs remain 
persistent concerns. Focus group participants emphasized that electricity constraints often limit 
mobility and delivery capacity, further discouraging business expansion. In 2023, 11 percent cited 
electricity as the top obstacle versus 5 percent in 2024; among those affected in 2024, high cost was 
the leading issue (57 percent), followed by unreliability (40 percent). FGDs further noted that the lack 
of electricity outside firms’ local municipalities is a direct reason many do not expand geographically. 
Similarly, financial constraints remain a critical barrier to firm growth, despite not being 
consistently cited as the top concern. In 2024, only 10 percent of Somali firms applied for credit—
half the rate observed in 2023—with smaller businesses and firms in Bosaso facing the most severe 
barriers. Rejection rates declined to 34 percent in 2024 (from 70 percent in 2023), consistent with 
borrower discouragement as applications fell. Nationwide, 55 percent of firms are credit constrained 
(69 percent of smaller firms versus 37 percent of larger firms), with the highest share in Bosaso (76 
percent) and the lowest in Kismayo (15 percent).11 Those rates are considerably higher than the 
regional and global averages (25 and 14 percent of firms, respectively). Among non-applicants, 55 
percent were inhibited by barriers (26 percent cited collateral requirements, 17 percent procedures, 
others cited unfavorable terms or fear of rejection), while 45 percent reported no need. The credit 
market remains thin and urban-concentrated, serving larger, well-collateralized borrowers. Supply-
side gaps (limited branch presence, high transaction costs, and weak product diversification) and 
demand-side constraints (low financial literacy and awareness) both suppress access. A notable

Firms’ expectations 
Despite the abovementioned constraints, business expectations seem to be improving, 
offering grounds for cautious optimism. Between 2023 and 2024, the share of firms expecting 
higher sales in the next six months increased from 51 to 74 percent. However, expectations were not 
always aligned with realized outcomes. While in 2023, firms that expected an increase in sales 
anticipated an increase of 21 percent over six months, the realized 12-month average growth in 2024 
was about 8 percent, indicating overoptimism. City-level heterogeneity on this front is pronounced. 
In 2024, Kismayo was the most optimistic (97 percent of firms expecting higher sales), while Bosaso 
was the least (28 percent of firms). Investment intentions also remained high, with 64 percent of 
firms planning to purchase new or used assets. The share of firms expecting to fall into arrears 
declined markedly—from 15 to 8 percent—suggesting an improvement in financial resilience. Short-
term survival prospects improved, with firms saying they would be unable to operate beyond one 
month if sales stopped falling from 30 percent in 2023 to 8 percent in 2024.  
Employment expectations, however, remain flat. Roughly one-third of firms expected to hire new 
workers during the next six months in both years (31 percent in 2023 and 37 percent in 2024), with 
little movement in the share anticipating their workforce to remain stable (68 percent in 2023 and 63 
percent in 2024), and a negligible proportion of firms expecting reduced headcounts. However, only 
14 percent of firms reported expanding headcount in 2024 (less than half the share that had expected 
to do so in 2023). The noteworthy disconnect between rising sales and investment expectations and 
stagnant employment projections could stem from several factors, including structural labor market 
barriers, high labor costs, or uncertainty about long-term demand rather than labor-intensive growth. 
This highlights the need for labor market reforms and measures to reduce hiring frictions—
particularly to foster inclusive, job-rich growth.

1. Introduction 
This report arrives at an important moment in time, as the Federal Republic of Somalia enters a 
critical phase in its development. Since the formation of the Federal Government in 2012, the 
country has made significant strides in political normalization, institutional reform, and economic 
recovery. The country has sustained robust growth, averaging 5.25 percent between 2013 and 2024 
(World Development Indicators), and continues to rebuild essential governance and regulatory 
systems. However, deeply rooted fragility persists, with nearly 90 percent of the population facing 
multidimensional vulnerability. With constrained fiscal space and delivery capacity, the private 
sector’s role in services and employment is pivotal. Yet, informality, market concentration, and low 
productivity blunt its impact, making the translation of macro-level gains into inclusive, job-rich 
growth challenging for the country. As a result, economic opportunities remain insufficient for a 
rapidly growing and urbanizing population.12 More robust, inclusive, and private sector-led economic 
growth is needed to ensure that the hard-won gains of the last decade take root. 
Supporting the development of the private sector is a priority for the Federal Government. This 
is underscored in the National Transformation Plan (NTP) 2025–2029 and the Centennial Vision 2060 
(CV2060). In effect, the NTP states that, “[The country] is committed to fostering private sector 
growth by improving the business environment, removing regulatory barriers, and promoting 
financial inclusion,” whereas the CV60 notes “[This strategic document] prioritizes the establishment 
of a thriving private sector, nurtured by a supportive ecosystem, to unlock opportunities for job 
creation, income generation, and foreign investment attraction.” The World Bank Group is also 
committed to supporting the Federal Government in achieving this objective, as indicated in the 
Country Partnership Framework for the Federal Republic of Somalia for FY24–28 under Pillar 1: 
Inclusive, private sector-led job creation and economic growth. As part of this pillar the World Bank 
Group plans on assisting the private sector to create more productive and higher-wage jobs, focused 
on bridging the “missing middle” of productive small and medium enterprises (SMEs). 
In this context, understanding the private sector—a constrained yet vital pillar of the 
economy—is crucial. Given limited public sector fiscal space and underdeveloped institutional 
capacity for basic service delivery, the Federal Republic of Somalia’s private sector plays a central 
role in delivering services, generating employment (accounting for about 95 percent of jobs), and 
supporting communities. Yet it remains largely informal, fragmented, and undiversified. A small 
number of conglomerates dominate key markets, while most enterprises are informal micro-
businesses with low productivity and limited links to wider markets.13 Supporting private 
enterprises—especially in underserved regions—can reduce poverty and promote resilience. 
Unlocking the sector’s full potential is, therefore, crucial to achieving a more inclusive and 
prosperous country.

However, reliable data on the Federal Republic of Somalia’s private sector remain scarce, 
limiting the possibility to design evidence-based reforms. Some advances have begun to shift this 
landscape. For instance, the 2024 Federal Republic of Somalia Integrated Business Establishment 
Census (SIBEC) conducted by the Somali National Bureau of Statistics (SNBS) marks a historic 
milestone in national development planning, as it is the first national census in over 50 years to 
provide reliable data on the composition of the private sector, offering an essential foundation for 
evidence-based policy making. Yet, data on performance of the private sector remained scarce, as 
indicated by the 2024 Somalia Country Private Sector Diagnostic (CPSD), which underscored the 
urgent need for granular firm-level data to better understand firm performance and the key 
constraints to private sector growth, thereby laying the basis for strong, evidence-based policy 
making.  
To fill the remaining critical data gaps, this report presents new and comprehensive evidence 
on composition, performance, and constraints to growth of the Federal Republic of Somalia’s 
formal private sector. It draws on two rounds of representative firm-level surveys conducted by the 
World Bank (that is, the 2023 and 2024 Integrated Business Establishment Surveys [IBES]). The 
surveys covered 700 formally registered firms across five cities—Baidoa, Beledweyne, Bosaso, 
Kismayo, and Mogadishu—with 86 percent of respondents tracked in both years.14 Qualitative focus 
group discussions (FGDs), also conducted in 2023 and 2024, complement the firm-level surveys by 
adding context and nuance, capturing perceptions and dynamics that quantitative data alone cannot 
reveal.15 Taken together, these data collection efforts provide an unprecedented view of the formal 
private sector in the Federal Republic of Somalia.  
The report aims to guide evidence-based policy making and complement the existing and 
ongoing private sector diagnostic efforts in the Federal Republic of Somalia. It builds on recent 
diagnostics, such as the CPSD, which set out reforms priorities for the country’s private sector using 
available data. The purpose of this extended report is to offer a diagnostic overview of the private 
sector landscape, without proceeding into detailed policy recommendations. The new survey and 
focus group evidence presented in this report provide additional context on firm-level conditions and 
challenges, offering empirical support that reinforces and further substantiates the CPSD’s findings 
and recommendations. The diagnostic is also expected to serve as input to other analytical 
initiatives, including the upcoming Growth and Jobs Report, where policy priorities will be analyzed 
in greater depth. In December 2025, this effort was further complemented by the publication of the 
World Bank Enterprise Survey data, which can be leveraged to deepen the diagnostic of constraints 
to private sector growth by analyzing firms’ day-to-day experiences with the business environment.16

It is important to stress that important gaps remain. As indicated by the SIBEC data used in the 
report to provide information on the business demographic in the country, formal firms account for

investment between 2023 and 2024. Chapter 3 examines key business environment constraints,

connectivity and digital technology, and electricity), and access to finance and government support. 
Chapter 4 analyzes firm expectations, covering anticipated sales, workforce adjustments, capital

future policy interventions, prioritization, and areas for further diagnostic and analytical work.

2. Composition and performance of the private sector in the Federal Republic 
of Somalia 
This chapter profiles the Federal Republic of Somalia’s formal private sector, highlighting 
differences across firm types. The analysis of firm structure, performance, and investment patterns 
helps identify both strengths and structural bottlenecks. Although fewer than one in three 
establishments in the Federal Republic of Somalia are formally registered, focusing on formal firms 
is key to boosting investment and promoting more and better jobs. The formal sector is a bellwether 
for sustainable growth, investment attraction, and integration into global markets. Examining 
productivity in the formal private sector is crucial for investment and job creation, while 
improvements in this area can also benefit informal firms through spillovers, such as knowledge 
transfer and stronger market links.18 Within the formal sector, the focus of this report is on privately 
owned firms in manufacturing, wholesale, and retail and market-oriented services sectors.19  
The chapter integrates multiple data sources. The chapter starts by setting the stage on the 
composition of the private sector, leveraging the 2024 SIBEC.20 It details the distribution of formal 
firms by size, sector, location, and ownership structures, including women’s participation. The rest 
of the chapter draws primarily on the 2023–2024 IBES and focuses on formal firms with five 
employees or more across five cities: Baidoa, Beledweyne, Bosaso, Kismayo, and Mogadishu.21 
These cities account for 56 percent of all formal firms in the Federal Republic of Somalia.22 The 
analysis tracks productivity trends between 2023 and 2024. It examines firm investment behavior 
and international links, particularly through trade. The majority of firms in the IBES sample employ 
between 5 and 50 workers, with only a small share exceeding that range. To facilitate meaningful 
analysis, when IBES data are used, firms are grouped into two categories: smaller firms (5–9 
employees) and larger firms (10 or more employees).

A majority of firms reported increasing sales in 2024, showing improvement over 2023. Figure

change, and 8 percent reported an average decrease of 14 percent in 2024 (up from 3 percent in 2023) 
(See Figure 2.3a and Figure 2.3bError! Reference source not found.).  
This positive trend masks a sharp geographic divergence. Regional analysis reveals that while 
some areas experienced better performance in 2024 compared to the year before, others continued 
to face significant challenges. Mogadishu’s share of firms with rising sales jumped from 44 percent 
in 2023 to 65 percent in 2024, driving the overall positive performance at the aggregate level. On the 
contrary, firms in Bosaso remained the weakest performers, with only 11 percent reporting sales 
increases while 32 percent reported declines (Figure 2.3a). Other cities fell between these two 
extremes. The differences in net increase are also noticeable, with Mogadishu firms whose sales 
increased doing so by 16 percent on average, in comparison to an average of 10 percent in Bosaso. 
Similarly, firms in Mogadishu whose sales decreased did so by 20 percent on average, in comparison 
to an average of 11 percent in Bosaso (Figure 2.3b).

declines. Among firms with more than 10 employees (larger firms), the share reporting higher sales

reporting declines edged down from 3 percent in 2023 to 2 percent in 2024. In contrast, smaller firms 
(with 10 or fewer employees) showed little improvement. While the share of these firms reporting an 
increase in sales remained almost unchanged (48 percent in 2023 and 49 percent in 2024), 13 
percent of smaller firms reported a decline in sales in 2024 (up from 3 percent the previous year) 
(Figure 2.3c). On net, larger firms, which on average increased sales by 21 percent and decreased by

Employment 
Despite sales gains, job creation in 2024 was weak, with most firms holding headcount 
constant. Sustained increases in sales often drive job creation, with businesses expanding their 
operations and workforce to meet growing demand. Unfortunately, this is not the case in the Federal 
Republic of Somalia. The share of firms expanding their workforce in the five cities in the analysis fell 
from 30 percent in 2023 to 14 percent in 2024, while the share reducing employment rose from 3 
percent to 8 percent. In Mogadishu, the share of firms expanding their workforce changed only 
minimally—88 percent of firms reported no employment change in 2023 and 91 percent in 2024.

negligible reductions in 2023 (Figure 2.4a). Both smaller and larger firms reduced hiring momentum.

proportion of firms increasing their workforce among larger firms—from 52 percent expanding in 
2023 to 16 percent in 2024. Downsizing remained low among larger firms but rose sharply among 
smaller firms–from 3 percent to 13 percent (Figure 2.4b).

3. Key business environment constraints  
This chapter examines how business environment constraints may help explain firm 
performance in the five cities in the analysis. The analysis focuses on three key areas: (a) market 
competition—particularly price distortions, entry barriers, and competition from informal firms; (b) 
infrastructure, including transportation and logistics, connectivity and digital technology, and

identify market competition, transportation, connectivity, and electricity as the main obstacles. The 
selection was further informed by data on firms’ experiences cited by FGD respondents, which reveal 
persistent issues with access to finance. The chapter draws on both objective indicators from the 
IBES and the subjective perspectives of business owners expressed in the FGDs, combining lived 
experiences with assessments of the business environment.

3.1.1 Price distortions  
Distorted market prices in the Federal Republic of Somalia stem from a combination of 
structural weaknesses, market monopolies, and the absence of effective regulatory oversight. 
Focus group participants emphasized how monopolistic control over essential sectors—such as 
telecommunications, electricity, and import/export—enables a handful of firms to manipulate
=== PAGE 28 ===
28 
 
market conditions to their advantage.36 FGD participants reported that companies often benefit from 
favoritism by state actors and operate in an environment with minimal regulatory accountability. As 
a result, they can restrict market entry, inflate prices artificially, and compromise the quality of goods 
and services.37 This is particularly pronounced in sectors such as construction materials, 
electronics, and essential food items. Without strong competition frameworks, there is little to 
prevent anti-competitive behaviors, and market dynamics are often driven by speculation rather than 
underlying supply and demand dynamics. In comparing the current situation to 2023, respondents 
in the 2024 FGDs offered mixed perspectives on progress. Some noted a slight improvement in 
market stability, attributing this to increased government regulation and licensing, while others 
reported that price volatility had worsened, particularly for imported goods and fuel. 
3.1.2 Entry barriers 
Somali businesses—especially larger firms in Mogadishu—report facing a multifaceted set of 
entry barriers. Among firms experiencing market competition as their worst constraint, 28 percent 
cited entry barriers as the most significant hindrance. Notably, Mogadishu recorded the highest 
prevalence of this challenge, with 42 percent of firms in the city indicating entry barriers as a major 
operational obstacle (Table 3.1). Larger firms are more affected by entry barriers than smaller ones, 
with 32 percent reporting obstacles compared to 26 percent for smaller firms (Table 3.2), likely 
reflecting stricter and more complex compliance demands.  
Respondents in FGDs described entry barriers as a complex web of challenges. These include 
limited access to finance, infrastructure gaps, and regulatory uncertainty. While issues with 
infrastructure and access to finance are discussed in the next sections, this section focuses mainly 
on regulatory barriers. Participants in FGDs noted that dominant firms in different sectors, including 
electricity and telecommunications, use their control over essential services to block new 
competitors, primarily by setting entry costs prohibitively high. Such concentration of market power 
limits firms’ entry into the market and restricts consumer choices, undermining overall market 
efficiency. Without regulatory mechanisms to level the playing field, these monopolies are able to 
maintain dominance, often to the detriment of potential new entrants and smaller firms. 
FGD participants consistently emphasized that business registration and licensing processes 
impose substantial financial and administrative burdens on new entrants. Despite recent 
progress,38 the institutional and regulatory landscape remains fragmented and underdeveloped. 
Entrepreneurs described licensing procedures as complex, time-consuming, and regionally 
inconsistent, often requiring interaction with multiple government bodies and, in some areas, even 
local peace committees. The risk of inadvertently duplicating business names—due to insufficient 
government information—was cited as a common and costly issue. A lack of transparency,

3.1.3 Competition from informal firms 
The third most frequently cited challenge within market competition was competition from 
informal firms. At the aggregate level, 13 percent of firms that identified market competition as a 
major constraint pointed to informal competition as the main issue, with the highest share in Bosaso 
(25 percent) and the lowest in Kismayo (3 percent) (Table 3.1). The issue is particularly pronounced 
among smaller firms, with 20 percent identifying direct competition from informal firms as their main 
market challenge (Table 3.2). This is partly because smaller formal firms often operate in the same 
low-margin segments as their informal counterparts. In contrast, only 2 percent of larger firms 
viewed informal competition as a significant obstacle. Instead, they were more affected by entry 
barriers and distorted market prices. 
Competition with informal firms varies significantly across cities and by firm size. At the 
aggregate level, 37 percent of firms reported competing with informal businesses, when prompted 
(Figure 3.3a).40 This is lower than both the Sub-Saharan African average, where 56 percent of firms 
face competition from informal firms, and the global average of 42 percent. Despite this relatively 
positive picture overall, competition from informal businesses is more pronounced in certain 
geographical areas of the economy. The challenge is significantly more acute in Baidoa (67 percent) 
and Beledweyne (60 percent). It is also notable in Kismayo (49 percent) and Mogadishu (31 percent). 
Strikingly, only 16 percent of firms in Bosaso reported facing competition from informal firms (Figure 
3.3a)—a finding that warrants further investigation. As far as size goes, half of the smaller firms report 
facing competition from informal firms compared with 20 percent of the larger firms. As noted above, 
this is understandable since smaller firms tend to operate in retail, repair services, and local trade—
where competition from informal firms is most intense (Figure 3.3b).  
Faced with competition from informal businesses, formal firms are often forced to choose 
between lowering their prices to unsustainable levels or maintaining them and losing 
customers. Participants in FGDs emphasized the range of interlinked challenges posed by informal 
operators, who are free from tax obligations, licensing fees, regulatory compliance, and can offer 
lower prices, thereby creating severe market distortions. The challenge is heavily felt in sectors with 
thin profit margins, where even slight price differences can significantly influence consumer choices.

3.2 Infrastructure 
This section focuses on three key infrastructure areas that firms in the five cities analyzed 
identified as major obstacles to operations: transport and logistics, digital connectivity, and 
access to electricity. Efficient transport and logistics systems reduce the time and cost of moving 
goods and services, enabling firms to reach wider markets, optimize supply chains, and compete 
more effectively. Digital connectivity enhances access to information, markets, and customers, 
while also enabling innovation, automation, and more efficient business operations. Meanwhile, 
reliable and affordable electricity is essential for virtually all economic activity, from powering 
machinery and equipment to supporting modern office functions. Together, these infrastructure 
components are necessary for businesses to scale, invest, and generate employment opportunities.  
3.2.1 Transportation and logistics  
Transport and logistics were identified as the most significant challenge to private sector 
development in 2023 and the second most relevant challenge in 2024. In 2023, transportation 
was cited as the main obstacle by 19 percent of firms (Figure 3.1a). In 2024, 13 percent of firms 
identified transport as the most significant constraint to operations, making it the second most

highlighting it as problematic.41 In the remaining cities transportation issues were cited as the top

Among transportation issues, the cost of transportation is the principal concern for firms in the 
five cities that were analyzed. About 54 percent of businesses that identified transportation and

concerns about storage facilities (17 percent) and speed of transport (15 percent). Reliability was 
also cited by 10 percent of firms that reported transportation as a constraint (Figure 3.4b). 
Poor road conditions, limited connectivity, and insecurity are factors that drive transportation 
costs and consumer prices. The country faces high road transport costs and elevated port and 
airport fees that complicate long-haul transportation. Geographic and economic fragmentation—
especially between rural and urban, and coastal and landlocked areas—remains pronounced. Over 
90 percent of the Federal Republic of Somalia’s paved roads have deteriorated, while most rural 
roads are gravel or earth, making travel particularly difficult during the rainy seasons. The lack of all-
season roads limits rural connectivity, increases fuel and maintenance costs, and prolongs travel 
times. For instance, it takes five days to travel between Mogadishu and Bosaso by road. High 
transport costs are further aggravated by roadblocks and by the absence of a unified vehicle registry.

Poor regional road links also isolate the Federal Republic of Somalia from broader trade integration. 
While corridor rehabilitation is under way, progress remains constrained by insecurity, limited

road networks, insecurity along trade routes, and unreliable port operations lead to delays and cost

FGDs reinforced and contextualized the abovementioned infrastructure challenges. Roads are 
reported to be often either nonexistent or in severe disrepair due to long-standing damage from the 
civil war, making the movement of goods costly and inefficient. These challenges intensify during the 
rainy season, when flooding further degrades road conditions and disrupts supply chains. This 
contributes to the concentration of activities in local markets previously discussed (84 percent of 
firms serve only local markets; see Figure 2.9a), drives up operational costs, and forces firms to 
contend with goods scarcity and inflated prices, thereby limiting their potential for growth and 
expansion of markets. FGDs also uncovered that markets beyond major municipalities are 
fragmented, with limited consumer purchasing power, making outreach to those areas financially 
unattractive for many firms—especially SMEs—thereby dampening the returns on investment in 
logistics capacity. 
Logistics are also identified as a challenge, with one recurring concern—particularly among 
firms in the agri-business sector—being the lack of cold chain infrastructure. Perishable goods 
often spoil before reaching markets, leading to revenue loss, product scarcity, and food safety 
concerns. Larger firms reported underutilized production capacity due to the unreliable supply of 
raw materials, while smaller producers and retailers described frequent stock-outs and their inability 
to ensure product quality. Participants also pointed out the competitive disadvantages faced by new 
and smaller firms due to high transport costs and logistical unreliability. These barriers were reported 
to deter market entry and expansion. Established businesses are better equipped to mitigate such 
costs through private fleets and storage, reinforcing their dominance. Meanwhile, smaller 
businesses lack the capacity to absorb transportation shocks or invest in workarounds.

infrastructure, the Federal Republic of Somalia ranks 133rd out of 140 countries.43 Despite some 
recent progress, quality standards and infrastructure in the Federal Republic of Somalia are largely 
deficient, and relevant institutions are at a nascent stage. Since the collapse of the state in 1991, the 
Federal Republic of Somalia has lacked a government-led quality infrastructure ecosystem. Practical

3.2.2 Connectivity and digital technology  
Internet connectivity was cited as the third most binding constraint in 2024, making it far more 
salient than in 2023. Reliable internet connectivity is essential for firm growth as it enables access 
to markets, customers, and suppliers beyond local boundaries. It supports key business functions 
such as digital marketing, online sales, data management, and communication, while also improving 
operational efficiency and access to digital financial services. Issues with internet connectivity were 
identified as the most binding obstacle to operations by 7 percent of firms in 2024—a considerable 
increase compared to just 2 percent in 2023, when it ranked as the third-to-last reported obstacle 
(Figure 3.1a and Figure 3.1b) signaling the likely presence of higher demand for connectivity from 
firms.45  
Issues with internet connectivity were cited as the most binding obstacle to operations mainly 
by larger firms and firms in the capital city. In 2024, internet connectivity was cited as the top 
obstacle, primarily in Mogadishu, where 13 percent of firms reported it as the main constraint. 
However, this does not imply that connectivity is less adequate in the capital city. Rather, firms in

3.3 Access to financial resources and government support 
In addition to market competition and infrastructure, firms consistently identify two related 
barriers to growth— limited access to finance and insufficient government support. Many firms 
in the FGDs noted the difficulty in securing start-up or expansion capital, which significantly hampers 
their ability to enter or grow within the formal economy. Government support—whether through 
financial assistance such as grants or subsidized loans, or non-financial support such as training, 
advisory services, or regulatory facilitation—is also recognized as an important factor for business 
growth. This section discusses both aspects.

Firms in the five cities that were analyzed are severely credit constrained. Across the five cities 
that were analyzed, 55 percent of firms are credit constrained, a rate that is more than double the 
Sub-Saharan Africa average of 25 percent and four times the global share of 14 percent.47 By city, the

financial access challenges they face (Figure 3.7a). Financial constraints are a major barrier to firm 
entry, investment, and growth. Limited access to finance restricts firms’ ability to invest, hire, expand, 
or innovate, keeping them small and less competitive. Over time, this widens gaps between firms, as 
those with access to finance can grow and adapt, while constrained firms remain vulnerable.

rejection. The remaining 45 percent of firms indicated they simply did not think that a loan was 
necessary (Figure 3.7b). These patterns—together with the sharp drop in applications and high 
rejection rates, and more generally, the high percentage of firms being credit constrained—indicate 
that firms’ lower demand for credit may be driven less by a lack of investment ambition and more by 
real or perceived barriers in the credit system. Mistrust in the financial system and low financial 
literacy may further discourage businesses from applying for credit. During the FGDs, entrepreneurs 
cited legacies of bank failures, low financial literacy, and limited awareness of formal financial 
products as drivers of distrust. Complex procedures and unclear terms hinder loan applications.

Those firms that do apply for loans have to navigate several challenges. Banks require land or

unsecured loan products, these requirements effectively exclude a significant portion of businesses, 
particularly those without substantial fixed assets, from accessing formal credit. Participants in 
FGDs also highlighted that, in addition to collateral, banks often demand guarantors, and in some 
cases, the involvement of a peace elder or a well-known businessperson, further raising the barrier 
to entry for many borrowers. The high cost of finance is another critical challenge reported across 
business sectors and sizes during FGDs, driven by factors such as rising interest rates, application 
and processing fees, expenses related to meeting collateral requirements, and the opportunity costs 
associated with the lengthy loan application process. 
Taken together, these patterns point to a thin, urban-concentrated credit market that serves 
larger, well-collateralized borrowers, leaving many SMEs and firms outside major cities 
underserved. As noted in the CPSD, the availability of—and access to—a diverse range of financial 
services in the Federal Republic of Somalia is restricted by both supply-side and demand-side 
factors. The small degree of financial intermediation that is available is largely provided by a handful 
of institutions largely concentrated in urban areas and is generally focused on offering relatively 
larger loans. Some businesses—particularly those aiming to expand into rural regions—have 
reported, during FGDs, that they were entirely unable to obtain formal financing, significantly 
hindering their growth prospects. Beyond the collateral-related barriers already discussed, structural 
gaps such as limited branch presence outside urban centers, high transaction costs, and weak 
product diversification further constrain access. On the demand side, limited financial literacy and a 
general lack of awareness about available financial products continue to suppress demand for 
appropriate services. This situation stands in contraposition to the notable bright spot highlighted by 
recent World Bank assessments, which showed that the widespread use of mobile money has 
helped firms carry out basic transactions even in areas underserved by formal financial institutions.49  
3.3.2 Government support  
Government support to the private sector is minimal. In 2023, firms were asked whether they 
received government support over the previous year. The vast majority (99 percent) reported receiving 
no support and did not expect any in the near-term future. However, they showed high interest in 
receiving support, especially for improving their skills and adopting new technologies. When asked 
to select the three most needed policies to support their business from a list of 10, the most 
frequently cited were support to improve managerial skills (19 percent of firms) and assistance with 
adopting digital technologies (14 percent). Nearly 14 percent of firms indicated deferral of tax 
payments as the main support policy needed (Figure 3.8).

4. Firms’ expectations 
Business expectations are a leading indicator of private sector performance and investment 
behavior. Building on the analysis of outcomes in 2023–2024, the report now shifts to firms’ 
forward-looking sentiments, focusing on three dimensions: (a) sales and workforce projections, (b) 
capital investment plans, and (c) financial resilience. Together, these expectations offer predictive 
insights into how firms anticipate future demand and their ability to withstand shocks. Although 
expectations do not automatically translate into outcomes, they influence hiring, production 
planning, and investment decisions. Capturing these outlooks enables policy makers to design 
targeted support measures and foster a more conducive environment for sustained private sector 
growth. 
4.2.1 Sales and workforce expectations 
Firms’ sales expectations have become significantly more optimistic between 2023 and 2024. 
Looking six months ahead (relative to the same period a year earlier), the share expecting higher sales 
rose from 51 percent in 2023 to 74 percent in 2024, while the share of firms anticipating declining 
was negligible (1 percent and 0 percent, respectively). Accordingly, the share of firms that expected 
‘no change’ slightly decreased from 48 percent to 26 percent. In 2024, the average expected increase 
in sales was about 20 percent (Figure 4.1a and Figure 4.1b). This sharp improvement signals rising 
confidence and potential expansion through investments, inventory buildups, and higher input 
purchases. However, a comparison of expectations and actual results shows that outcomes did not 
meet projected levels. Although 43 percent of firms in 2023 increased sales (Figure 2.3a), 
respondents overestimated opportunity and downplayed risk: in 2023, 51 percent anticipated a net 
11 percent rise over 6 months whereas the 2024 actual net increase over the last 12 months was 
about 8 percent (Figure 2.3a, Figure 2.3b, Figure 4.1a, and Figure 4.1b).  
While firms were broadly optimistic, there was notable heterogeneity across locations. 
Compared to 2023, the share of firms expecting to increase sales rose in every city except Baidoa 
and Bosaso. The sharpest increase in optimism came from Kismayo, where in 2024, 97 percent of 
firms expected sales to rise by an average of 22 percent—up from 73 percent of firms expecting an 
11 percent increase in 2023. By contrast, Bosaso projected the weakest expectations. Only 28 
percent of firms anticipated growth in 2024, with an average increase of 9 percent, down from 34 
percent of firms expecting a 21 percent increase in 2023. In the remaining cities, between 75 and 86 
percent of firms expected sales to grow, with projected increases ranging from roughly 18 to 26 
percent (Figures 4.1a and 4.1b). However, when comparing six-month projections made in 2023 to 
actual 12-month outcomes in 2024, expectations proved overoptimistic across the board. For 
instance, in Mogadishu, firms were less confident, yet an expected 7 percent increase in six months 
materialized as 4 percent. Firms in Bosaso proved the least realistic: while they anticipated a 7 
percent increase, actual sales declined by 2.4 percent (Figure 2.3b and Figure 4.1b). 
Smaller firms were less optimistic than larger ones. The share of larger firms expecting sales 
growth rose sharply from 45 percent in 2023 to 92 percent in 2024, compared to smaller firms, for 
whom expectations increased more modestly—from 53 percent in 2023 to 62 percent in 2024 (Figure

4.2.2 Firm-level investment expectations 
Although investment expectations remain stable and high, signaling confidence in future 
growth, they proved overoptimistic. The share of firms expecting to invest in new or used fixed 
assets in the next six months remains steady at encouraging levels—63 percent in 2023 and 64 
percent in 2024 (Figure 4.4). This means that only about one in three firms does not plan to invest in 
the next six months, a finding that misaligns with the patterns discussed in Section 2.3, where only 
31 percent of firms, in 2024, invested in the last 12 months (see Figure 2.8a). The discordance 
between expectations and realizations—evident in 2023 when projected sales, employment, and 
investment failed to materialize in 2024—raises questions about the reliability of such expectations 
as predictors of actual outcomes. It is possible that structural constraints, such as economic 
instability, market competition, or access to finance, prevent firms from translating optimistic plans 
into concrete investment decisions. This persistent optimism gap therefore highlights both the 
resilience of business sentiment and the fragility of the conditions needed to turn that sentiment into 
sustained growth. 
Regional variation in capital investment expectations remains stark. For instance, in 2024, nearly 
all firms in Kismayo (93 percent) planned to purchase fixed assets, indicating robust local optimism 
or perhaps specific opportunities in that city. In contrast, very few firms in Bosaso (16 percent) 
anticipated investing, highlighting potential constraints or differing local economic conditions 
(Figure 4.4a). The difference between projected investment in 2023 and actual investment in 2024 is 
highest in Kismayo, where 97 percent of firms intend to invest in the next six months but only 11 
percent invested in the last 12 months (Figure 2.8a and Figure 4.4b). As expected, firm size plays a 
role: larger firms are more likely to plan for investment, with 68 percent reporting expected asset 
purchases compared to 61 percent of smaller firms in 2024. Larger firms overestimated investments 
by 55 percentage points (90 percent projected investments, but only 35 percent did so). Smaller firms 
were more modest: 53 percent expressed intention and 28 percent put it into practice (Figure 2.8a 
and Figure 4.4bError! Reference source not found.). This underlines the importance of ensuring t
hat smaller enterprises also have access to the resources and conditions needed to invest and grow.

5. Conclusive remarks  
The evidence presented in this report provides a diagnostic overview that can inform the 
Federal Republic of Somalia’s broader reform agenda in support of private investment and jobs. 
Providing detailed recommendations is beyond the intended scope of this report. However, the 
analysis highlights areas likely to have the greatest impact on firm productivity and job creation, 
which could be further explored in policy design and sequencing exercises.  
The analysis in the report confirms that firms in the Federal Republic of Somalia face binding 
constraints that must be addressed for jobs- and productivity-enhancing reforms to take effect. 
Such challenges are not uncommon in fragile and conflict-affected settings, and identifying and 
implementing these foundational, first-order policy reforms can provide clear a roadmap for building 
a more dynamic, resilient, and inclusive private sector. Key obstacles in the Federal Republic of 
Somalia include large price distortions, significant barriers to trade and investment, and critical 
infrastructure gaps. Tackling these constraints could enable Somali firms to improve 
competitiveness, increase product quality, and expand productive capacity, therefore strengthening 
private sector growth and employment outcomes.  
Table 5.1 provides a summary of these constraints, with some high-level measures that can be 
further unpacked in the upcoming analytical work, including the Somalia Country Growth and 
Jobs Report. Findings from the 2025 Enterprise Survey, along with the forthcoming Somali IBES (led 
by the SNBS), will further strengthen the analytical basis for policy dialogue. Together with the results 
presented in this report, they can provide the diagnostics needed to identify more granular 
constraints and guide targeted interventions supporting private sector–led growth in the Federal 
Republic of Somalia. At the same time, addressing remaining evidence gaps—particularly on 
informality, which accounts for over 70 percent of all firms—will remain crucial.

A.1. Survey methodology and implementation  
The Federal Republic of Somalia Integrated Business Surveys collected data from formally registered 
establishments across five Somali cities: Baidoa, Beledweyne, Bosaso, Kismayo, and Mogadishu in 
2023 and 2024. For multi-establishment firms, each establishment may be a separate unit of 
analysis, provided its financial statements are individually identifiable. Firms with multiple locations 
but with only consolidated financial statements are considered a single unit of analysis.  
The survey exclusively targeted establishments officially registered with either the Ministry of 
Commerce and Industry or the local municipality, which 
• 
Employ a minimum of five workers, categorized into three size classes: small (5 to 19 
employees),50 medium (20 to 99 employees), and large (100 or more employees) and 
• 
Operate within non-farm and non-financial private sectors. Specifically, the following sectors 
were included manufacturing (Group D); construction (Group F); various service sectors 
(Groups G and H); transport, storage, and communications (Group I); and information 
technology (division 72 of Group K) as per the International Standard Industrial Classification 
(ISIC) Rev. 3.1.  
Data collection occurred in two consecutive rounds including 700 firms each.  
• 
Round 1 took place between August and November 2023.  
• 
Round 2 was a follow-up of the previous round conducted between October 5, 2024, and 
November 18, 2024.  
Nearly 86 percent of the firms interviewed during round 1 were also interviewed during the second 
round of data collection. The distribution of complete interviews in both years is detailed in Table A.1. 
The sampling method employed was stratified random sampling, ensuring a representative cross 
section of businesses. Weights were computed and used in the analysis to project the results to the 
population of inference. Table A.1 presents the sample design in both rounds of data collection.

A.3. Focus group discussions 
The qualitative assessment consisted of two rounds of 12 FGDs within the same strata (location, size, 
and sector) targeted in the Federal Republic of Somalia Business Survey. The two rounds of FGDs 
were complementary to the two survey rounds.  
The first round took place between December 19, 2023, and January 4, 2024. Round 2 took place 
between February 22, 2025, and March 19, 2025. All participants are individuals responsible for 
making major strategic and financial decisions within the businesses they represent. To ensure only 
the people who meet the above criteria were recruited to participate in the FGDs, establishments 
were contacted by phone to determine their eligibility and willingness to participate in the FGD. Table 
A.2 shows the distribution of the FGDs across the five locations in the study. 
The objective of the FGDs was to add further context to the Federal Republic of Somalia Business 
Survey results in select areas covered by the survey. The FGDs were facilitated using an open-ended 
discussion guided by experienced moderators and notetakers, ensuring that key topics were covered 
while allowing room for participants to share their views and experiences. Each FGD aimed to have 
at least seven participants from private sector businesses that cut across eligible sectors and sizes 
(small, medium, large)—which was achieved in all but one FGD. Moderators and notetakers were 
trained in Somali. Discussions were recorded, and recordings were transcribed word for word in 
Somali, then translated into English and reviewed for accuracy and completeness. 
Tables A.3–A.5 provide a more detailed overview of the firms that participated in the FGDs. It includes 
the dates when each session was held, the number of businesses involved in each discussion, and 
the location, for both 2023 and 2024.
