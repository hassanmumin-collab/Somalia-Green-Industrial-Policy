Part II section (5) of the central bank of Somalia Act 2012 the key 
mandate of the Central Bank of Somalia (CBS)
The Bank shall:
a)	 Formulate implement and be responsible for monetary policy and implement 
the foreign exchange policy.
b)	 Hold and manage the foreign exchange reserves of Somalia.
c)	
License, regulate and supervise all banks and financial institutions to foster 
the liquidity, solvency, and proper functioning of a stable financial system.
d)	 Formulate and implement such policies as to best promote the 
establishment, regulation, and supervision of efficient and effective payment, 
clearing and settlement systems.
e)	 Where appropriate, administer payment, clearing and settlement systems.
f)	
Act as banker and adviser to, and as fiscal agent for the government and 
public entities.
g)	 Act as sole issuer of legal tender Somali currency notes and coins; and
h)	 Shall compile, analyze, and publish the monetary, financial balance of 
payments statistics and other statistics covering various sectors of the 
national economy.

Governor’s Message Governor’s 
Message
It is with great pride that I present the Central Bank of Somalia’s Annual Report for 
2024, a year marked by meaningful progress in fulfilling our mandate to promote 
financial stability, inclusion, and institutional resilience.
A key milestone was Somalia’s transition into the post-HIPC era following the 
attainment of the HIPC Completion Point in December 2023. In 2024, the country 
sustained strong reform momentum, maintaining its engagement with the IMF and 
World Bank. Under the new Extended Credit Facility (ECF), Somalia met critical 
targets in public financial management, revenue mobilization, and financial sector 
development, demonstrating strong commitment to macroeconomic stability.
Institutionally, the Central Bank advanced key structural reforms, particularly in 
regulatory modernization, supervisory oversight, and payment system digitalization. 
We strengthened internal controls, enhanced AML/CFT compliance, and robust 
risk management systems to uphold financial integrity and operational soundness.
Significant strides were also made in currency reform agenda. Through the “Kalkaal” 
Currency Exchange Project, CBS laid the groundwork for the phased reintroduction 
of the national currency. The formal adoption of a Currency Board Arrangement 
(CBA) provided a transparent and rules-based framework for future currency 
issuance. Other preparations included new banknotes design, legal framework 
development, reestablishment of CBS branches in Federal Member States and 
extensive stakeholder consultations.
To support effective policymaking, CBS invested in economic data infrastructure 
and analytical capacity. Regular publication of macroeconomic indicators, such 
as balance of payments, monetary aggregates, and financial data, has improved 
transparency and informed national dialogue.
On the international front, CBS continued to strengthen its global partnerships. 
Through membership in the World Bank’s RAMP and deeper engagement with the 
IFSB and AFI, CBS has advanced best practices in reserves management, Islamic 
finance, and financial inclusion.

structural weaknesses and newly emerging risks continued to weigh 
on the long-term outlook. The world economy appears to have 
entered a period of subdued expansion, with growth falling short of the 
average recorded between 2000 and 2019. The medium-term forecast is 
increasingly shaped by rising geopolitical tensions, unpredictable policy 
shifts, evolving trade dynamics, and the escalating threat of climate-induced 
disruptions. According to the IMF’s World Economic Outlook (January 
2025), global output growth is estimated to be 3.2 percent in 2024, with

In 2024, advanced economies (AEs) are estimated to have grown by 1.7 
percent, supported by steady economic expansion in the United States 
and a modest recovery in the Euro Area, though partially offset by a 
significant slowdown in Japan. This growth occurred despite the absence 
of major shifts in economic policy. In contrast, emerging markets and 
developing economies (EMDEs) continued to grow at a faster pace than 
AEs, although their estimated GDP growth slowed slightly from 4.4 percent 
in 2023 to 4.2 percent in 2024. This deceleration is expected to persist in 
2025, gradually aligning with the region’s estimated potential growth rate. 
Meanwhile, low-income developing countries (LIDCs) are estimated to 
have maintained stable growth of around 4.1 percent in 2024, driven by 
resilient domestic demand and ongoing investment.
Africa’s economic performance in 2024 reflected a modest but fragile 
recovery amid a challenging global environment. According to AfDB, Africa’s 
GDP growth is estimated at about 3.3 percent for 2024, up slightly from 
3.0 percent in 2023. This pickup was supported by strong public spending 
and recovering domestic demand. However, macroeconomic imbalances 
continued to constrain economic stability. Inflation remained stubbornly 
high, averaging 18.7 percent continent-wide in 2024, driven by food supply 
disruptions, currency depreciation, and the lingering impact of fuel and 
subsidy reforms in several countries.

1.1	 Global Trade
According to UNCTAD’s Global Trade Update (March 2025), global trade 
in goods and services recorded a strong recovery in 2024, expanding by 
approximately 3.7 percent and reaching an all-time high of nearly US$33 
trillion. This growth was primarily driven by the robust performance of 
services trade, which grew by 9 percent and contributed around US$700 
billion to the total. In contrast, goods trade posted a more modest increase 
of 2 percent, adding approximately US$500 billion over the year. Developing 
economies outperformed advanced economies in 2024. Trade in developing 
countries rose by 4 percent annually, driven especially by East and South 
Asia, while developed economies experienced stagnation and even 
quarterly contractions of 2 percent in both imports and exports during the 
fourth quarter. At the same time, widening global trade imbalances were 
observed, with the United States continuing to register the largest trade 
deficit and China recording the highest surplus. The European Union also 
returned to a trade surplus after previous deficits, largely due to elevated 
energy prices.
However, the global trade outlook for 2025 is clouded by growing geopolitical 
tensions, protectionist trade measures, and policy uncertainty, especially 
linked to evolving tariff strategies among major economies. Despite these 
risks, potential tailwinds such as easing global inflation and China’s economic 
stimulus could support trade flows in the near term.
1.2	 Global Inflation and Commodity Prices 
Global headline inflation is projected to have eased from 6.7 percent in 
2023 to 5.7 percent in 2024, with forecasts indicating a further decline 
to 4.2 percent in 2025, according to the IMF’s World Economic Outlook 
published in January 2025.The report attributes this easing to softer 
commodity prices, improved supply chain conditions, and the lingering 
effects of tight monetary policy, although policy-related uncertainties may 
still influence inflation outcomes.

In advanced economies, inflation has fallen below targets as energy prices 
declined through 2024 and food prices stabilized, falling by 2 percentage 
points from an estimated 4.6 percent in 2023 to 2.6 percent in 2024. 
On the other hand, inflation in EMDEs has continued to decline, coming 
within a percentage point of pre-pandemic levels by late 2024, estimated 
to decrease from 8.1 percent in 2023 to 7.8 percent in 2024, with a further 
drop to 5.6 percent in 2025. Global food commodity prices declined in 
2024 as supply growth rebounded. The global food price index averaged 
122.0 points for the year, reflecting a 2.1 percent decrease from 2023. This 
decline was primarily driven by significant drops in cereal and sugar prices.
Looking ahead, the disinflation trend is expected to persist, with only 
minor deviations from the IMF’s earlier forecasts published in October 
2024. As labor markets gradually soften and energy prices decline further, 
headline inflation is likely to continue converging toward central bank 
targets. Nonetheless, inflation in the United States is projected to remain 
slightly above the 2 percent objective in 2025, while inflation in the Euro 
Area is expected to remain more subdued. In China, low inflation is likely 
to persist. As a result, divergence in expected interest rate paths between 
the U.S. and other major economies is anticipated to widen.
1.3	 Global Financial Stability
Global financial conditions have generally eased slightly since mid-2024, 
driven by interest rate cuts from major advanced economy central banks, 
most notably in the United States. In the Euro area, real interest rates 
have also become somewhat accommodative, reflecting a more subdued 
economic outlook. However, this improvement was partially reversed in 
the second half of 2024, as financial conditions tightened in emerging 
markets and developing economies. In the second half of 2024, financial 
conditions in EMDEs weakened due to slower-than-expected disinflation 
in some countries, uncertainty surrounding potential shifts in U.S trade 
policies. EMDEs currencies experienced increased volatility, with several 
depreciating significantly against the U.S. dollar in the fourth quarter, driven 
by a wave of capital outflows from EMDEs debt and equity markets. This 
shift in monetary policy has helped galvanize investment flows, particularly 
into riskier assets, as investors seek higher returns amid improved liquidity 
and reduced borrowing costs; however, rising economic and geopolitical 
uncertainty increases the likelihood of adverse shocks, exposing fragilities.

1.4	 Implications for the Domestic Economy
Despite ongoing challenges such as shifting global trade dynamics, the Red 
Sea crisis, and rising geopolitical tensions, Somalia’s domestic economy 
demonstrated notable resilience in 2024. As global inflation eased, domestic 
inflation remained relatively stable, staying within single digits throughout 
the year. Somalia’s major trading partners continued to be the United Arab 
Emirates (UAE), Oman, Saudi Arabia, China, India, and Türkiye. Among 
these, the UAE, Oman, and Saudi Arabia remained Somalia’s top export 
destinations. The UAE’s economic growth is estimated to hold steady 
at 4.0 percent in 2024, while Oman and Saudi Arabia are estimated to 
see modest increases of 1.3 percent and 1.7 percent, respectively. On the 
import side, China, India, and Türkiye remained the largest sources of 
goods. However, China’s growth rate is anticipated to slow to 5.0 percent 
in 2024. India’s growth is estimated to ease to 6.8 percent, and Türkiye 
is projected to experience a third consecutive year of deceleration, with 
growth falling to 3.2 percent. Somalia’s domestic economy continued to 
show resilience, with real GDP growth estimated at 4.1 percent in 2024 
and projected to remain at the same level in 2025.

T
he domestic economy remained largely resilient in 2024, despite 
challenges and risks arising from climate change and rising global 
geopolitical conflicts and tensions. The real GDP growth is estimated 
to moderate in 2024 with a growth of 4.1 percent and is projected to slow 
further to 4.0 percent in 2025, reflecting the expected negative impact of 
foreign aid cuts on economic activity, and the adverse impact of climate shocks.
Despite challenges and risks arising from climate change and rising global 
geopolitical conflicts and tensions, Somalia’s macroeconomic performance 
remained stable in 2024, and the domestic economy is projected to experience 
moderate growth. The headline inflation rate remained in the single digits, 
slowing to an estimated 5.6 percent in 2024 from 6.1 percent in 2023. The 
current account deficit widened to 14 percent of GDP in 2024, up from 12 
percent in 2023, mainly due to higher import demand despite strong growth 
in livestock and agricultural exports. Somalia’s domestic revenue performance 
is higher than expected in 2024, demonstrating strong growth and resilience. 
Domestic revenue increased by 12 percent, reaching US$369 million in 2024. 
This growth reflects ongoing efforts to diversify tax sources including rental 
& sales tax and expand the tax base in customs. 
In the post-HIPC era, the Federal Government of Somalia (FGS) has been 
actively engaging with its creditors to restructure and settle outstanding 
debts as part of its broader economic recovery and fiscal sustainability

increased economic activities have resulted in additional revenue while 
providing improved regulatory control over important economic activities.

4.1	 Balance of Payments 
Somalia’s Balance of Payments (BoP) position over the past years reflects a fragile 
yet evolving external sector, shaped by persistent trade deficits, high reliance on 
remittances, and growing engagement with international partners. The BoP structure 
reveals a persistently negative current account, largely driven by structural trade 
imbalances and a limited domestic production base. Despite a widening goods and 
services deficit in recent years, the current account has been consistently supported 
by strong inflows in the secondary income account primarily remittances which remain 
the most reliable source of external financing for the Somali economy.
The capital account recorded steady inflows, largely from international donors and 
development partners. These non-debt-creating transfers have played an essential role 
in funding public investment, strengthening institutions, and supporting reconstruction. 
Together with remittances, they have helped maintain external stability while external 
debt levels have declined significantly under international debt relief frameworks, 
Somalia continues to face considerable vulnerabilities due to its dependence on imports, 
limited export diversification, and exposure to external shocks such as geopolitical 
developments, climate events, and fluctuations in remittance flows.
In this context, the Balance of Payments provides a vital framework for assessing the 
sustainability of Somalia’s external transactions and guiding macroeconomic policy. 
Strengthening the BoP requires a multi-pronged strategy focused on export promotion, 
local production capacity, financial sector integration, and continued engagement 
with development partners. 
4.1.1	
Current Account
Somalia’s current account remains structurally in deficit, reflecting the country’s 
persistent external vulnerabilities and dependence on merchandise goods and services. 
In 2024, the current account deficit widened to US$1,622.1 million, up from US$1,280.9 
million in 2023, a deterioration of approximately 27 percent. This widening of the 
deficit pushed the current account shortfall to over 13 percent of nominal GDP, one 
of the highest in the recent period.
In 2024, the merchandise trade deficit reached US$5,688.0 million, up from US$4,161.4 
million the previous year, showing a 37 percent increase. This expansion was largely due 
to an increase in imports, which grew to US$7,717.7 million, reflecting higher demand 
for consumer goods, especially food, fuel, and construction inputs. While import 
demand remains strong, it also reflects an expanding economic base and urbanization. 
At the same time, exports of goods rose significantly, increasing by 54 percent from 
US$1,010 million in 2023 to US$1,556.2 million in 2024. Export growth was mainly 
driven by the livestock sector and some high-value crops, supported by favorable 
external demand and gradual improvements in compliance and trade logistics. Although 
the export base is still narrow, the year’s progress suggests room for future scaling 
through targeted investment and market access strategies.
Somalia’s secondary income inflows remained a cornerstone of external resilience. 
In 2024, net inflows reached US$6,493.5 million, up by 60 percent from US$4,051.5 
million in 2023. Remittance receipts grew to US$7,424.1 million, providing essential 
support to households across the country and enabling consumption smoothing, 
private investment, and social stability.

4.2	External Public Debt
In 2024, Somalia marked a historic turning point in its economic recovery 
journey by achieving the Completion Point under the Heavily Indebted Poor 
Countries (HIPC) Initiative. This milestone, officially reached on December 
13, 2023, is the conclusion of over a decade of persistent reforms and policy 
efforts across public financial management, macroeconomic stability, and 
governance. With this achievement, Somalia secured irrevocable debt relief 
amounting to approximately US$4.5 billion, equivalent to over 90 percent 
of its external debt stock. As a result, the country’s external debt-to-GDP 
ratio fell dramatically from about 64 percent of GDP in 2018 to less than 
6 percent by the end of 2023, significantly improving the fiscal outlook 
and unlocking space for critical development investments.
The debt relief under the HIPC framework involved a wide range of 
multilateral, bilateral, and commercial creditors. Of critical importance 
was the agreement reached with the Paris Club of bilateral creditors, 
which wrote off nearly 99 percent of Somalia’s eligible bilateral debt, 
including the cancellation of over US$2 billion. In a notable bilateral step, 
the United States Government cancelled US$1.14 billion in debt obligations 
in November 2024, further consolidating Somalia’s path toward full re-
engagement with the international financial system.
The debt relief was not merely a financial transaction it was the result of 
deep institutional reforms and rigorous policy implementation. Somalia 
successfully completed the requirements under the IMF Extended 
Credit Facility (ECF) and implemented key HIPC triggers. This included 
enacting robust Public Financial Management (PFM) law, enhancing the 
transparency of budget processes, rolling out electronic procurement and 
cash management systems, and publishing audited government accounts 
for the first time in decades. Moreover, Somalia strengthened its Anti-
Money Laundering and Combating the Financing of Terrorism (AML/
CFT) framework, modernized its financial infrastructure, and expanded 
digital financial services, including QR-code–based payment systems and 
a centralized invoice tracking mechanism. These reforms were supported 
by the Central Bank’s technical coordination and debt recording systems, 
enabling accurate, verifiable debt data, a critical prerequisite for international 
debt relief negotiations.
4.2.1	
Debt Composition 
In 2024, debt relief significantly improved Somalia’s fiscal position. 
External debt service obligations, which had constrained fiscal space for 
years, were substantially reduced, allowing the government to reallocate 
resources toward education, health, security, and infrastructure. Additionally, 
investor and donor confidence improved, and Somalia became eligible 
for new concessional financing terms. Importantly, the Government of 
Somalia committed to avoiding new unsustainable debt accumulation by 
strengthening debt management systems and adopting prudent borrowing 
strategies.

Remittances continue to play a vital role in Somalia’s economic stability, 
serving both as a financial lifeline for many households and a key driver of 
economic activity. These funds support millions of families by providing access 
to essential goods and services, while also encouraging entrepreneurship 
and creating job opportunities. Beyond helping individual households, 
remittances bring in important foreign currency, which contributes to 
stabilizing exchange rates, increasing consumer spending, and strengthening 
overall financial resilience.
In 2024, improvements in Somalia’s financial sector further enhanced 
the impact of remittances. The growth of mobile money services, better 
regulatory oversight, and increased financial inclusion have made the 
transfer process safer, faster, and more reliable. These developments have 
encouraged more people to move away from informal cash transfers toward 
formal financial institutions, helping to build a stronger banking system.
Total inward transfers to Somalia reached US$6,230 million (52 percent 
of GDP) in 2024, reflecting a 10 percent increase from US$5,669.8 million 
(46 percent of GDP) in 2023 and marking the highest annual inflow on 
record. This sustained growth underlines the resilience of Somalia’s external 
sector and the increasing role of formal financial channels in facilitating 
remittances, business transactions, and development financing.

US$2,181.3 million in 2023 to US$2,719.1 million in 2024 reflecting a 25

ver the course of 2024, Somalia made measurable improvement 
in revitalizing its monetary policy framework, marking a pivotal 
year in the country’s economic reform journey. The Central Bank

International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank (WB), focused on

6.1	 Currency Reform
For over three decades, Somalia has operated without officially issued 
national currency, leading to a proliferation of counterfeit currencies and 
a heavily dollarized economy. This absence of credible national currency 
hindered effective monetary policy implementation and excluded large 
segments of the population from formal financial services.  In a landmark 
initiative, the CBS has prioritized the reintroduction of the Somali Shilling 
(SOS) as a cornerstone of its Strategic Plan for 2025–2029. This move 
aims to restore monetary sovereignty, enhance financial inclusion, and 
facilitate small-value transactions essential for daily economic activities.
The currency exchange project involves replacing old and counterfeit 
notes with newly designed SOS banknotes. This reform is not merely a 
monetary adjustment, but a socio-economic intervention designed to 
empower citizens marginalized by the dollarized economy. By providing 
a secure and accessible local currency, the CBS seeks to integrate these 
populations into the formal financial system, thereby enhancing economic 
participation and stability.
This historic endeavor signifies not only the revival of a national symbol 
but also a commitment to economic resilience and self-determination. 
The reintroduction of the Somali Shilling marks a proud moment for the 
CBS, reflecting its dedication to restoring trust in the national currency 
and fostering inclusive economic growth.
6.2	Transition to a Currency Board Framework
In tandem with the reintroduction of the Somali Shilling, the CBS is preparing 
to implement a Currency Board Arrangement (CBA) to provide a stable 
and predictable policy environment. A CBA will anchor the SOS to a stable 
foreign currency, maintaining a fixed exchange rate and ensuring that the 
monetary base is fully backed by foreign reserves. This arrangement is 
expected to enhance confidence in the national currency across Somalia 
while maintaining a dual currency regime with the U.S. dollar. CBS has also 
joined the World Bank’s Reserve Advisory and Management Partnership 
(RAMP) to enhance its reserve management capabilities and ensure the 
successful implementation of the currency reform. This partnership is 
expected to provide CBS with the necessary tools and expertise to manage 
foreign exchange reserves effectively.

7.1	 Board of Directors
The Central Bank of Somalia (CBS), established under The Central Bank Act Law 
No. 130 of 22 April 2012, and is governed by a Board of Directors that serves as 
the supreme policy-making organ of the Bank. The Board is composed of seven 
members: the Governor (who serves as the Chair), the Deputy Governor (Vice 
Chair), and five non-executive directors.
The Governor, as the Chief Executive Officer and Chair of the Board, holds the 
authority to convene meetings and lead discussions on strategic decisions. In the 
Governor’s absence, the Deputy Governor assumes these responsibilities. The 
CBS Act grants the Board authority to delegate specific duties to its committees, 
individual members, or Bank staff, except for the formulation of core monetary 
policy, which remains a non-delegable responsibility.
The Board maintained a high level of engagement in 2024 with a deliberate 
focus on institutional strengthening, both in governance and technical capacity. 
As part of its forward-looking agenda, the Board placed emphasis on enhancing 
CBS’s corporate governance culture by promoting awareness and accountability 
across all levels of the organization. The Bank’s top leadership operated within a 
governance framework that prioritizes both accountability and autonomy balancing 
policy discretion with adherence to statutory mandates and international best 
practices. Board oversight extended to risk management as well, with a focus 
on identifying operational and strategic risks, ensuring continuity of operations, 
and protecting the reputation of the institution.
In 2024, the Board was supported by four standing committees: the Audit 
Committee, Licensing and Supervision Committee, Currency Committee, and 
Human Resource Committee. These committees ensure that specialized aspects 
of the Bank’s functions are effectively overseen.

2. 
STATUTE AND PRINCIPAL ACTIVITIES 
 
Central Bank of Somalia (CBS) is the Central Bank of the Federal Republic of Somalia. The Bank is a body 
corporate with legal entity, with perpetual succession and common seal, with power to acquire, own, possess and 
dispose of common seal, and of property, and to contract, to sue and to be sued in its own name. The Bank has the 
ability to exercise any type of central Banking function unless specifically excluded under The Central Bank Act 
2012, and will enjoy all the prerogatives of a central Bank
The objective of the Bank as stated in the Central Bank of Somalia Act, 2012 are: 
 
 
The primary objective of the Bank shall be to achieve and to maintain domestic price stability.  
 
 
The other objective of the Bank, which shall be subordinated to the primary objective of the Bank, shall be to 
foster and maintain a stable and competitive market-based financial system.  
 
 
Without prejudice to these two objectives, the Bank shall support the general economic policies of the 
Government.  
 
A summary of functions and objectives of the Bank Central Bank of Somalia Act, 2012 are to: 
 
 
formulate implement and be responsible for monetary policy and implement the foreign exchange policy;  
 
 
hold and manage the foreign exchange reserves of Somalia;  
 
 
license, regulate and supervise all banks and financial institutions so as to foster the liquidity, solvency and 
proper functioning of a stable financial system;  
 
 
formulate and implement such policies as to best promote the establishment, regulation and supervision of 
efficient and effective payment, clearing and settlement systems;  
 
 
where appropriate, administer payment, clearing and settlement systems;  
 
 
act as banker and adviser to, and as fiscal agent for the Government and public entities;  
 
 
act as sole issuer of legal tender Somali currency notes and coins; and  
 
 
Shall compile, analyze, and publish the monetary, financial balance of payments statistics and other statistics 
covering various sectors of the national economy.

Intangible assets - computer software costs 
 
Costs incurred on computer software are initially accounted for at cost as intangible assets and subsequently at cost 
less any accumulated amortization and impairment losses. Amortization is calculated on a straight line basis over 
the estimated useful lives not exceeding 5 years. 
 
Impairment of tangible and intangible assets  
 
At the end of each reporting period, the Bank reviews the carrying amounts of its tangible and intangible assets to 
determine whether there is any indication that those assets have suffered an impairment loss. If any such indication 
exists, the recoverable amount of the asset is estimated in order to determine the extent of the impairment loss (if 
any). Where it is not possible to estimate the recoverable amount of an individual asset, the Bank estimates the 
recoverable amount of the cash-generating unit to which the asset belongs. Where a reasonable and consistent basis 
of allocation can be identified, corporate assets are also allocated to individual cash-generating units, or otherwise 
they are allocated to the smallest group of cash-generating units for which a reasonable and consistent allocation 
basis can be identified.  
 
Recoverable amount is the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. In assessing value in use, the 
estimated future cash flows are discounted to their present value using a pre-tax discount rate that reflects current 
market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to the asset for which the estimates of future 
cash flows have not been adjusted. 
 
If the recoverable amount of an asset (or cash-generating unit) is estimated to be less than its carrying amount, the 
carrying amount of the asset (or cash-generating unit) is reduced to its recoverable amount. An impairment loss is 
recognized immediately in profit or loss. 
 
Where an impairment loss subsequently reverses, the carrying amount of the asset (or a cash-generating unit) is 
increased to the revised estimate of its recoverable amount, but so that the increased carrying amount does not 
exceed the carrying amount that would have been determined had no impairment loss been recognized for the asset 
(or cash-generating unit) in prior years. A reversal of an impairment loss is recognized immediately in profit or 
loss, unless the relevant asset is carried at a revalued amount, in which case the reversal of the impairment loss is 
treated as a revaluation increase. 
 
Provisions 
 
Provisions are recognized when the Bank has a present legal or constructive obligation as a result of past events, it 
is probable that an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits will be required to settle the obligation and 
a reliable estimate of the amount of the obligation can be made. 
 
Finance costs 
  
Finance costs comprise finance costs on borrowings. 
  
Borrowing costs that are not directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset 
are recognised in the profit or loss using the effective interest method. 
 
Gold 
 
Gold is held by the Bank as part of its foreign reserves. Gold is initially recorded at cost at initial recognition, 
including transaction costs. Subsequent to initial measurement, it is accounted for under the revaluation model. 
 
Gold reserves are held to support the national currency (Gold Bullion) and as such are classified as non-current 
assets, and accounted for using either cost model or the revaluation model. The Bank accounts for Gold Bullions 
using the revaluation model.Such reserves are not traded and the levels frequently do not change from one year to 
the next.

Financial liabilities are derecognised when and only when the Bank obligations are discharged, cancelled or they

Offsetting financial instruments 
 
Financial assets and liabilities are offset and the net amount reported in the statement of financial position when there 
is a legally enforceable right to set off the recognised amounts and there is an intention to settle on a net basis, or 
realise the asset and settle the liability simultaneously. 
 
Leases 
 
The Bank assesses at contract inception whether a contract is, or contains, a lease. That is, if the contract conveys the 
right to control the use of an identified asset for a period in exchange for consideration. 
 
The Bank as a lessor 
 
Leases in which the Bank does not transfer substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership of an asset 
are classified as operating leases. Rental income arising is accounted for on a straight-line basis over the lease terms 
and is included in revenue in the statement of profit or loss due to its operating nature.

of the Bank may be increased by such amounts as may be proposed by the Board and approved by the Ministry of 
Finance.

As at the reporting date, the Bank had the following reserves which represent its net assets.

Revenue reserve

(b)

subsequent gains/losses going through the revaluation reserve. This is not  subject to distribution.

28 
CONTINGENCIES AND COMMITMENTS 
 
Given the development and security situation in Somalia, as well as difficulties in obtaining all relevant historical 
transaction data and relevant entity accounting documentation and agreements, contingent assets and liabilities 
may exist. The Bank still has some land and buildings which it owns but has not been able to regain control after 
the collapse of the Somalia government in 1991. Additionally, the Bank has not been able to reliably measure 
the value of these land and buildings. However, the Directors have ensured that all assets which the bank controls 
and all known liabilities have been included in these financial statements.  
 
29 
RELATED PARTY TRANSACTIONS 
 
Parties are considered to be related if one party has the ability to control the other party or exercise significant 
influence over the other party in making financial or operational decisions. The Bank acts as fiscal agent to the 
Government in accordance with the Central Bank of Somalia Act. Government includes the Federal Government 
of Somalia, selected regional authorities and salary payment accounts for Parliamentarians. Other than 
transactions and balances in the normal course of business with the Government, there have been no significant 
transactions with related parties during 2024, nor are there known significant assets or liabilities with related 
parties as at 31 December 2024 (2023  Nil).
