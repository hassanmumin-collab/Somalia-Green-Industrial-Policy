# Part 1: Why Somalia needs a green industrial policy

## Key messages

- Somalia consumes far more than it produces. Imports of goods and services were 75 percent of GDP in 2025 {{C-0008}}, against exports of around 20 percent {{C-0007}}. The gap is financed by remittances and aid, not by domestic production.
- Electricity is the binding constraint on manufacturing. Average tariffs of 48 to 90 US cents per kWh {{C-0083}} {{C-0084}} are the highest in the region, and no light manufacturer can compete at that price.
- Somalia exports its most valuable products in raw form: live animals, raw sesame and raw frankincense. The value added in slaughtering, processing, grading and branding is captured abroad.
- Climate shocks are already an economic cost. The government's own assessment put the effects of the 2023 Deyr floods at USD 176.1 million {{C-0499}}, and floods cost Mogadishu an estimated USD 120 million a year on average {{C-0493}}.
- Solar power, proximity to Gulf markets and a young labour force give Somalia a real opening. A green industrial policy is the instrument that turns these advantages into factories, jobs and exports.

## 1.1 A consumption economy financed from abroad

Somalia's GDP was USD 13,234 million in 2025 {{C-0001}}, and real GDP grew by 3.1 percent {{C-0003}}. Growth has been steady, but its composition is narrow. Household consumption exceeds 100 percent of GDP {{C-0009}}, which is possible only because a large share of what households consume is imported. Gross national disposable income was USD 17,299 million in 2025 {{C-0006}}, well above GDP, reflecting remittances and aid.

The import bill shows what the economy does not yet produce. The National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) estimates that more than 90 percent of essential commodities consumed in Somalia are imported {{C-0038}}. In 2024 Somalia imported about USD 2,517.7 million of food {{D-0002}}, USD 907.9 million of clothes and footwear {{D-0003}} and USD 658.5 million of oil and gas {{D-0001}}. Several of these are goods that a country with Somalia's resources and labour force could make at home: flour, dairy products, soap, hygiene products and garments.

Exports are concentrated in one sector. Livestock held about 73 percent of total goods exports in 2025 {{C-0053}}, and exports were estimated at more than 5.9 million head of animals {{C-0054}}. A single disease outbreak or import ban in a Gulf market can therefore move the whole balance of payments.

## 1.2 Energy: the binding constraint

The most prohibitive factor for light manufacturing in Somalia is the cost of electricity. Average tariffs range from 48 to 90 US cents per kWh {{C-0083}} {{C-0084}}, the highest in the region. The World Bank's estimated average tariff is 20 US cents in Kenya {{C-0489}} and 25 in Djibouti {{C-0485}}. In Ethiopia, the region's manufacturing competitor, it is estimated at between 0.5 and 3 US cents {{C-0487}} {{C-0488}}. A garment, soap or flour plant in Mogadishu pays many times what its competitors pay for the same kilowatt-hour.

The reason is structural. Private providers supply over 90 percent of urban electricity through isolated, diesel-based mini-grids {{C-0090}}. Their costs move with the world oil price. When conflict escalated in the Middle East in late February 2026, domestic fuel prices in Mogadishu rose from USD 0.60 to USD 1.50 per litre within days {{C-0039}} {{C-0040}}. Firms that generate their own power face the same exposure: the Ministry of Energy's valuation of diesel self-generation equals an estimated 40 US cents per kWh {{D-0079}}.

Solar power with battery storage changes this arithmetic. Built at the scale of an industrial park, it is projected to deliver electricity at USD 0.115 per kWh (modelled estimate, base scenario; illustrative, not a forecast) {{M-0001}}, well under half the cost of diesel self-generation. Somalia's plans already point in this direction: the National Transformation Plan (NTP) sets a target of cutting electricity tariffs by 50 percent by 2029 {{C-0177}}. This policy therefore makes stand-alone solar and storage for the industrial parks its first enabling investment (Part 3).

Labour costs, by contrast, are not the obstacle. The World Bank's study of light manufacturing in Africa estimated that in Ethiopia labour productivity in well-managed firms can approach levels in China and Vietnam, while wages were only a quarter of China's {{C-0374}}. The lesson for Somalia is that competitive light manufacturing is possible in the region once power, land and logistics are fixed.

## 1.3 Raw exports leave value abroad

Somalia exports animals, not meat. Its live cattle and camel exports in 2025 correspond to an estimated 56,789 tonnes of carcass meat a year {{D-0062}}. Slaughtering, chilling, packing, and the hides and offal that come with them, all happen in the importing country.

The Gulf market for chilled beef shows what is being missed. The six Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states imported fresh or chilled beef at an estimated average of USD 6.99 per kg in 2023 {{D-0054}}. Live cattle from Somalia were valued at about USD 1.97 per kg of live weight in Oman {{D-0056}}. Proximity is a proven advantage in this market: Pakistan, a nearby halal supplier of lean beef, supplied an estimated 36.9 percent of GCC chilled beef imports by value {{D-0064}}, at an estimated USD 5.02 per kg {{D-0066}}. Ethiopia's small trade reached an estimated USD 10.00 per kg in Saudi Arabia {{D-0067}}.

The same pattern holds for crops and aromatic resins. Sesame processing in Somalia is minimal and most sesame is exported raw {{C-0159}}. Frankincense is shipped as raw resin lumps with little in-country refining {{C-0414}}, although the National Export Strategy estimates that production was worth around USD 80 to 90 million a year before the recent drought {{C-0413}} {{C-0529}}. Buyers in France and Germany paid an estimated USD 11.44 per kg for gums and resins from Somalia in 2023 {{D-0068}}, and the United Arab Emirates an estimated USD 1.45 per kg {{D-0076}}. Somalia's small exports of essential oils fetched an estimated USD 200 per kg {{D-0069}}.

Fisheries show the widest gap between potential and practice. Domestic fishers land an estimated 50,000 tonnes a year {{C-0153}}, while foreign fleets are estimated to catch around 100,000 tonnes in Somali waters {{C-0154}}. Fish exports reached over USD 25.86 million in 2025 {{C-0151}}, a fraction of what a processing and cold-chain industry could earn.

## 1.4 Climate shocks and a fragile urban core

Climate shocks are a recurring cost to the economy, not a distant risk. Somalia's third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) estimates direct climate-attributable losses from floods and drought over 2000 to 2021 at 3.3 percent of GDP {{C-0501}}. The rapid post-disaster needs assessment led by the Somali Disaster Management Agency estimated USD 126.6 million of damages {{C-0527}} and USD 49.5 million of losses {{C-0528}} from the 2023 Deyr floods in the sixteen districts assessed. Transport accounted for an estimated USD 29.7 million of these effects {{C-0500}}.

Mogadishu is where these risks meet the industrial strategy. The industrial parks will sit on the outskirts of the city, while the port that handles their inputs and exports sits inside it. The Mogadishu Port Authority states that the port can handle more than 3 million tonnes of cargo a year {{C-0502}}, an estimate of capacity that matters only if goods can reach it. An ongoing World Bank flood risk assessment estimates average annual flood losses in Mogadishu at USD 120 million {{C-0493}}. The same assessment estimates that 61 percent of households report income losses from floods, driven by workers being unable to reach workplaces and by disrupted supply chains {{C-0494}}. Every flood that closes the roads between the parks and the port stops production and delays exports. Drainage, sewerage and reliable public transport are therefore part of the industrial strategy (Part 5).

{{FIG 1.5}}

Household energy is a second climate front. Charcoal and firewood are the most common cooking fuels, with charcoal used by 47.9 percent {{C-0163}}, rising to 60.6 percent in urban areas {{C-0165}}. Charcoal drives deforestation, and United Nations Security Council resolution 2806 reaffirms the ban on the import and export of Somali charcoal {{C-0176}}. Efficient cookstoves and biomass pellets are an industrial opportunity as well as an environmental one, and the policy treats them as manufacturing lines within Cluster 3 (Part 4).

## 1.5 Why a green industrial policy, and why now

Markets alone will not solve these problems, for three reasons. First, the constraints are linked: a meat plant needs power, water, certified animals, cold storage and a reliable road to the port at the same time, and no single investor can provide them all. Second, several of the missing pieces are public goods, such as drainage, standards, animal health certification and the regulation of a new power supply. Third, first movers bear costs that later entrants do not, which is why investors wait. Industrial policy exists to coordinate these investments and to share the early risk.

The policy is green because the green option is the competitive one. Solar power is the cheapest source of electricity available to Somali industry. Processing at home cuts the emissions and losses of shipping live animals and raw materials, and cleaner cooking reduces charcoal demand. The green choice also opens finance that brown alternatives cannot reach. NDC 3.0 states that participation in carbon markets under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement will be crucial {{C-0081}}.

The policy builds on what Somalia already has. The World Bank found that the most successful industrial parks in China were built on local comparative advantage {{C-0375}}. For Somalia, that advantage lies in livestock, fisheries, riverine agriculture, aromatic resins, a young labour force and a location next to the Gulf.
